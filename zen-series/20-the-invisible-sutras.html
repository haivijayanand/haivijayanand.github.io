The Story

Tetsugen, a Zen monk in Japan, set himself a great task. The sutras were then available only in Chinese, and he wanted to carve them into wooden blocks and print them in Japanese, so that ordinary people could read them.

He travelled the country for ten years collecting money — much of it in tiny coins from people who had little to give — and he thanked every giver with the same gratitude.

At last he had enough. Just then the river flooded and famine followed. Tetsugen took everything he had raised and spent it feeding the hungry. Then he began collecting again.

Years later he had the sum once more — and an epidemic swept the land. Again he gave it all away to help the sick and the poor.

He started a third time, and some twenty years after he first set out, the blocks were finally carved and printed. They are still kept today at the temple of Manpuku-ji, near Kyoto.

People say Tetsugen made three editions of the sutras. The first two cannot be seen — and they are finer than the third.

Reflection

Tetsugen’s goal was a good one, and he did reach it. What makes him memorable is that he twice set it aside, without hesitation, when the people in front of him needed help more. He never treated the plan as more sacred than the purpose behind it.

We can grow so attached to our projects that we forget why we began them. The sutras were meant to ease suffering; feeding the starving did that directly. Tetsugen’s invisible editions are a reminder that the finest work we do may never appear on any list of achievements.

Carry this

Never let the plan become more important than the purpose.
