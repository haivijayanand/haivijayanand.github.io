Phase Analysis in Vibration Diagnostics: Triaxial Sensors, Cross-Phase and Resonance

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
PhaseDiagnosticsResonanceTriaxial accelerometers

Unbalance, misalignment, a bent shaft and resonance can all produce a large 1× peak. Amplitude alone cannot tell them apart; phase can. By comparing the timing of vibration at different points and directions, measured relative to a tachometer or between sensors, the pattern of motion becomes visible and the root cause usually follows.

Key takeaways

  • Absolute phase is measured against a once-per-revolution reference; relative (cross) phase between two sensors needs no tachometer.
  • Unbalance: radial horizontal and vertical about 90° apart; phase steady; similar across the coupling.
  • Misalignment: about 180° phase difference across the coupling, axially and often radially.
  • Resonance: phase shifts by 90° at the natural frequency and nearly 180° through it; amplitude peaks there.
  • Triaxial sensor arrays let you build operating deflection shapes (ODS) showing how the whole machine moves.

1. Measuring phase

Absolute phase: angle between the tachometer pulse and the vibration peak at 1× (filtered) Cross phase: φ = arg(G_xy) from the cross-spectrum of two channels at the frequency of interest Time delay: φ = 360° · f · Δt

Keep sensor orientation and sign convention consistent: a triaxial accelerometer reversed on one bearing adds 180° and can fake a misalignment pattern. Record which direction each axis points (e.g. X horizontal towards the motor, Y vertical up, Z axial).

3. Resonance and the Bode plot

During a run-up or coast-down, plotting 1× amplitude and phase against speed (Bode plot) or as a polar (Nyquist) plot identifies critical speeds: amplitude peaks and phase passes through 90°. A machine running near resonance shows high 1× vibration that balancing alone cannot fix: change stiffness or mass, or move the speed.

4. Triaxial arrays and operating deflection shapes

  • Place triaxial sensors at each bearing housing, the machine feet and the base frame.
  • Measure all points simultaneously, or move one sensor while keeping a fixed reference, and compute the cross-phase of each point to the reference at the frequency of interest.
  • Animate amplitude and phase on a wire-frame model: the ODS. Rocking of the frame, a soft foot, or a twisting base become obvious.
  • Use the same frequency line (e.g. exactly 1×) for all points, ideally with order tracking.
