    The factory as a system

    Every factory takes inputs — materials, energy, people, machines, information and money — and transforms them into products a customer pays for. The transformation creates value only if the customer would pay more for the output than the inputs cost. Everything in this course — planning, quality, maintenance, safety, cost — is about running that transformation reliably, safely and at the lowest total cost.

    Engineer's viewMachines, processes, settings, tolerances, cycle times. "Can we make it, and make it right?"
    Manager's viewCapacity, cost, cash, delivery, people, customers. "Can we make it on time and at a profit — every day?"
    The customer's viewRight product, right quality, right quantity, right day, right price. Nothing else counts.

    The best plants are run by people who hold both views at once. A process engineer who knows the machine-hour rate makes better decisions; a manager who knows why a laminate delaminates sets better priorities.

    Five process types

    TypeVolume / varietyFlowExamples
    ProjectOne-off, uniqueResources come to the productBuilding a plant, a ship, installing a gravure press
    Job shopVery low volume, very high varietyJumbled — each job its own routeTool room, cylinder engraving, maintenance workshop, prototype shop
    BatchLow to medium volume, medium varietyDisconnected, in lotsPrinting jobs, pouch making, machining families of parts, pharma granulation
    Line (repetitive)High volume, low varietyConnected, pacedCar and two-wheeler assembly, bottling, a dedicated sachet line
    ContinuousVery high volume, commodityContinuous, 24 × 7Refinery, paper mill, blown-film extrusion, polymer plant
    The product–process matrix (Hayes and Wheelwright). Plot product volume and standardisation on one axis and process type on the other. Successful plants sit on the diagonal: low volume and high variety with flexible job shops, high volume and low variety with dedicated lines. Off the diagonal you pay for it — a flexible process making a commodity costs too much; a rigid line making many variants spends its life changing over.

    How the order meets the factory

    StrategyWhat is heldLead time to customerExample
    Make-to-stock (MTS)Finished goodsShortestStandard carry bags, stretch film, bolts
    Assemble / finish-to-order (ATO)Semi-finishedShortPlain film in stock, printed to order; PCs configured to order
    Make-to-order (MTO)Raw materialsMediumPrinted laminated pouches for a brand, custom gears
    Engineer-to-order (ETO)Nothing specificLongestSpecial-purpose machines, test rigs, a new printing press

    The point where the customer order enters is the order decoupling point. Upstream of it you plan to forecast; downstream you work to firm orders. Moving it is a strategic decision: holding plain film lets a converter promise ten days instead of twenty-five.

    A flexible packaging plant

    Running example: one plant, several process typesA typical converter makes printed laminated pouches and rolls for food, FMCG and pharma customers. Blown-film extrusion runs nearly continuously (LDPE / LLDPE / HDPE, days per campaign). Printing (flexo, rotogravure, digital) and lamination run as batches of customer jobs with heavy changeovers — cylinders or sleeves, inks, viscosity, register. Slitting and pouch / bag making are batch processes with many small jobs. Cylinder engraving or plate making is a job shop. One order flows through all of them — which is why scheduling, WIP and changeover (Week 2) decide whether the plant delivers on time.

    Other industries look the same through this lens. A machine shop has job-shop machining feeding a batch assembly; a food plant has continuous cooking feeding batch packing; an auto supplier has batch press shops feeding a paced assembly line.

    What a factory competes on

    CostMaterial yield, conversion cost per kg or per piece, overheads (Days 6, 26, 28).
    QualityConformance to specification, consistency, no complaints (Week 3).
    DeliveryShort lead time and on-time in full (OTIF) (Days 2, 8, 9, 12).
    FlexibilitySmall batches, quick changeovers, new products fast (Days 11, 19).

    A plant cannot be best at all four at once with the same process. Order winners win the business (a lower price, a shorter lead time); order qualifiers only let you be considered (food-safety certification, barrier properties that meet the spec). Know which is which for each customer segment.

    Worked example

    Where does a new product fit?A customer asks for 3 million printed sachets a month of one design, for two years. Current route: gravure print (batch) → laminate (batch) → slit → pouch machine (batch, shared with 40 other SKUs). Volume high, variety one → this sits at the "line" end of the matrix but is being made in a batch process. Options: dedicate one pouch machine and keep cylinders mounted; or quote with the extra changeover and WIP cost. Decision depends on margin: dedicating removes ~12 changeovers a month (Day 11) and cuts lead time (Day 12).

    How to map your own plant

    1. List every product family and its annual volume and number of variants (SKUs).
    2. List every process step and classify it: job, batch, line or continuous.
    3. Mark where customer orders enter (the decoupling point) for each family.
    4. Place each family on the product–process matrix. Circle the ones off the diagonal.
    5. For each family write its order winner and order qualifiers.
    6. Ask: is the process type right for the volume? Is the stock held in the right place?

    Lab: product–process matrix

    Set the volume and the number of variants of a product family and choose the process it is made on. The dot shows where you sit; the shaded band is the diagonal where cost and flexibility are in balance.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • One plant, several matrices. Do not force one process type on the whole factory; separate high-runners from specials ("plant within a plant").
    • Volume is per variant, not per plant. 500 tonnes a month across 600 SKUs is a batch business, not a continuous one.
    • Managers: ask engineers for the process type behind a missed delivery. Engineers: ask managers for the order winner behind a priority.
    • Do not move the decoupling point without moving the stock and the planning method with it.

    Takt, cycle and lead time

    Takt time = available production time ÷ customer demand in that time
    TermMeaningExample
    Takt timeThe pace the customer needs (from German Takt, beat)7.5 h × 3 shifts = 81 000 s ÷ 900 000 pouches/day = 0.09 s per pouch
    Cycle timeHow often a process actually completes one unitPouch machine at 120 pouches/min → 0.5 s per pouch per machine
    Lead timeTime for one unit to go from raw material to dispatch18 days from film roll to pouch carton
    Value-added timeTime spent actually transforming the productAbout 2 hours of printing, laminating, curing… out of 18 days

    If cycle time is longer than takt, you cannot meet demand — add machines, shifts or speed. If it is much shorter, you overproduce or sit idle. For the pouch example, 0.09 s takt against 0.5 s per machine means about six machines must run in parallel.

    Value stream mapping

    A value stream is every step, value-adding or not, that brings a product family from supplier to customer, plus the information that triggers each step. A value stream map (VSM) draws it on one page.

    Process boxesOne per step, with a data box: cycle time, changeover time, uptime, operators, shifts, batch size, scrap %.
    Inventory trianglesMaterial waiting between steps, converted to days of demand: quantity ÷ daily demand.
    Information flowHow orders reach each step — schedule pushed by planning, or a pull signal from downstream.
    TimelineA saw-tooth line at the bottom: waiting days on the top, processing time on the bottom. Total the two.
    Value-added ratio = total processing (value-added) time ÷ total lead time

    In most batch plants the ratio is well under 1 %. That is not a scandal; it is the opportunity. The lead time is almost all waiting, and waiting is cut by smaller batches, quicker changeovers, less WIP and better scheduling — not by running machines faster.

    Flexible packaging value stream

    Printed laminated pouch — current stateFilm from supplier waits 6 days in the store → gravure print (job of 3 t, 4 h) → waits 3 days → solvent-less lamination (2 h) → cures 2 days (this wait is part of the process — adhesive needs it) → waits 2 days → slitting (1.5 h) → waits 3 days → pouch making (14 h for the order) → waits 2 days in finished goods → dispatch. Lead time ≈ 18 days; processing ≈ 22 h. The cure time is necessary non-value-added time; the other waits are queues created by batch sizes and schedules.

    In a machining plant the same map shows bar stock → cutting → turning → heat treatment (sent out) → grinding → assembly, and the outside heat-treatment loop is usually the biggest wait.

    Worked example

    Takt and lead timeDemand: 12 t of laminate per day. Available: 3 shifts × 7.5 h = 22.5 h. Takt = 22.5 h ÷ 12 t = 1.875 h per tonne (112 min/t). Printing: 200 m/min × 1 m web × 45 g/m² printed web = 540 kg/h → 111 min/t → just meets takt, no spare. Inventory before lamination: 9 t ÷ 12 t/day = 0.75 day. Before slitting: 30 t = 2.5 days. Total waits 16 days + processing 0.9 day → lead time ≈ 17 days, VA ratio ≈ 5 %.

    How to map a value stream

    1. Pick one product family (same route, similar times). Do not map the whole plant.
    2. Walk the route from dispatch back to receiving, with a stopwatch and a notebook — not from the ERP.
    3. Record each process's data box and count the inventory in front of it.
    4. Convert every inventory to days of demand and draw the timeline.
    5. Draw the information flow: who tells each step what to make next?
    6. Total lead time and processing time; mark the biggest waits with "kaizen bursts".
    7. Draw the future state: where can flow be continuous, where do you need a supermarket (Day 12), where is the pacemaker?

    Lab: value stream timeline

    Edit the steps of the stream: processing time per order and waiting days before each step. The timeline, lead time and value-added ratio update. Try halving the batch-driven waits.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Walk it, don't download it. System data hides re-work loops, hold areas and "temporary" stores.
    • Separate necessary waits (adhesive cure, ink drying, heat-treatment soak) from queue waits. Only the second kind is pure waste.
    • Takt is a planning number, not a target to beat — running faster than takt just builds inventory.
    • Managers: lead time is a cash number. 17 days of WIP at ₹2 crore per day of sales is working capital you are financing.

    Practice

    1. Calculate takt for your busiest line this month.
    2. Count the WIP in front of each step of one product family and convert it to days.
    3. Draw the current-state timeline and find the value-added ratio.

    Three kinds of capacity

    MeasureMeaningExample: 9-colour gravure press
    Design capacityMaximum output at rated speed, all time available300 m/min × 24 h × 365 d
    Effective capacityDesign minus planned losses: shifts, holidays, planned changeovers and maintenance3 shifts, 340 days, 4 changeovers a day
    Actual outputWhat came off the machine as good productFrom the production log
    Utilisation = actual output ÷ design capacity Efficiency = actual output ÷ effective capacity

    Capacity in a converter is not one number: a press has capacity in metres per minute, but customers buy kilograms or pieces, and kilograms depend on web width, thickness and density. Always state the unit and the product mix behind a capacity figure.

    Overall Equipment Effectiveness

    OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality Availability = run time ÷ planned production time Performance = (ideal cycle time × total count) ÷ run time = actual speed ÷ rated speed Quality = good count ÷ total count

    OEE answers one question: of the time we planned to make product, how much made good product at full speed? It strips out planned shutdowns so that it measures how well the machine is run, not how many shifts were scheduled.

    Loss groupSix big lossesFlexible packaging examples
    Availability1. BreakdownsDrive fault, doctor-blade holder failure, heater element
    2. Set-up and adjustmentCylinder change, ink wash-up, register set, die change on pouch machine
    Performance3. Small stops and idlingWeb breaks, splice failures, sensor trips, waiting for rolls
    4. Reduced speedRunning a 300 m/min press at 180 to "be safe" on a difficult job
    Quality5. Start-up rejectsMake-ready waste until colour and register are right
    6. Production rejectsBond strength failures, pin-holes, colour variation, seal leaks
    Benchmarks. 85 % OEE is often quoted as "world class" for discrete lines; most batch converting plants measure 45–65 % honestly. What matters is the trend and the loss Pareto, not the headline. TEEP (total effective equipment performance) = OEE × loading (planned time ÷ all calendar time) — it shows how much capacity you have before you need a new machine.

    Worked example

    OEE of a pouch machine for one shiftShift 480 min, planned break 30 min → planned time 450 min Downtime: changeover 55 min + breakdown 20 min = 75 min → run time 375 min Availability = 375 / 450 = 83.3 % Rated 120 pouches/min; total count 36 000 → ideal time 300 min Performance = 300 / 375 = 80.0 % Good pouches 34 560 (1440 rejects) → Quality = 96.0 % OEE = 0.833 × 0.800 × 0.960 = 64.0 % Biggest loss: speed (75 min equivalent) and changeover (55 min) — not quality.

    Setting up OEE on the shop floor

    1. Fix the ideal (rated) speed per machine and per product group — and write it down. Do not use "the speed we usually run".
    2. Define planned time: what counts as planned shutdown (no orders, holidays, planned PM) and what does not.
    3. Give operators a short, fixed list of downtime reasons (10–15 codes) mapped to the six losses.
    4. Count total and good output; book make-ready waste separately.
    5. Review OEE and the loss Pareto daily at the machine; weekly with management.
    6. Attack the biggest loss with a small team (Days 11, 17, 20); measure again.

    Lab: OEE loss waterfall

    Enter the shift data. The waterfall shows how planned time shrinks to good, full-speed time; each bar is one loss.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Don't game the ideal speed. Lowering it raises Performance on paper and hides the loss.
    • OEE is for improving a machine, not comparing operators or plants with different product mixes.
    • Measure OEE on the bottleneck first (Day 4) — an hour lost there is an hour lost for the plant.
    • In converting, add a material view alongside OEE: make-ready waste in kg often costs more than the minutes.

    The bottleneck sets the pace

    A bottleneck is any resource whose capacity is less than the demand placed on it. The output of the whole plant can never exceed the output of its bottleneck. Signs: WIP piles up in front of it, starves everything after it, and it is the machine everyone argues about.

    Line throughput = 1 ÷ (longest station time) Balance efficiency = Σ station times ÷ (n × longest station time)
    Goldratt's rule. An hour lost at the bottleneck is an hour lost for the whole plant. An hour saved at a non-bottleneck is a mirage — it only builds WIP.

    The five focusing steps (Theory of Constraints)

    1. Identify the constraint — the machine, people, material or even a policy (minimum batch size) that limits output.
    2. Exploit it — squeeze every minute out of it: run through breaks, pre-stage the next job, never let it wait for material, inspect before it so it never works on bad input.
    3. Subordinate everything else to it — other machines run only as fast as the constraint consumes; release material to its pace.
    4. Elevate it — only now spend money: extra shift, faster machine, outsourcing part of the load.
    5. Repeat — the constraint has moved. Do not let inertia (yesterday's rules) become the new constraint.

    Drum, buffer, rope

    DrumThe bottleneck's schedule beats the rhythm for the whole plant.
    BufferA time buffer of work in front of the bottleneck so it never starves when upstream has a hiccup.
    RopeMaterial is released into the plant only at the drum's pace — this keeps WIP down everywhere else.

    Bottlenecks in flexible packaging

    Where the constraint hidesIn many converters the rotogravure press is the bottleneck: expensive, long changeovers, and every printed job passes through it. In others it is lamination (one laminator for three presses) or, surprisingly often, pouch making for small-pouch orders where pieces per minute matter more than metres. Sometimes it is not a machine at all: cylinder availability, ink-kitchen colour matching or QC release of cured laminate. Exploiting the press means: next job's cylinders, inks and substrate staged and pre-checked before the current one ends; colour approval standards ready; operators relieved at breaks so it never stops.

    On an assembly line the same thinking appears as line balancing: split the work into elements and group them into stations so that no station exceeds takt and the stations are as equal as possible.

    Worked example

    A four-step lineStation times per unit: A 40 s, B 55 s, C 35 s, D 45 s (takt 50 s) Bottleneck: B at 55 s → throughput 3600/55 = 65.5 units/h; demand needs 72/h → short by 9 %. Balance efficiency = (40+55+35+45) / (4 × 55) = 175/220 = 79.5 % Move 8 s of B's work to C → A 40, B 47, C 43, D 45 → bottleneck 47 s → 76.6 units/h ✓ New balance efficiency = 175 / (4 × 47) = 93 %. Cost of one lost hour at B before the change: 65.5 units × contribution ₹180 = ₹11 800.

    Set the time of each station and the buffer size between them, then press P to run. Units flow left to right; watch WIP build in front of the slowest station. S steps, X pauses. Add a little variability and see why buffers matter.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Look for the queue, not the busiest person. The bottleneck is where work waits.
    • Do not chase utilisation on non-bottlenecks — 100 % utilisation everywhere means mountains of WIP.
    • Product mix moves the bottleneck: short jobs make the changeover-heavy machine the constraint; long jobs make the slow machine the constraint.
    • Managers: approve capex for the constraint only after it has been exploited. Most "we need a new machine" requests are 20 % OEE improvements in disguise.

    From–to chart and load × distance

    List the departments, record how many loads (or tonnes) move between each pair per day, and measure the distance between them. The total load × distance is a single number to compare layouts.

    Handling effort = Σ (flow between i and j) × (distance between i and j)
    Systematic Layout Planning (Muther). Rate each pair of areas on closeness: A absolutely necessary, E especially important, I important, O ordinary, U unimportant, X undesirable (e.g. a welding bay next to a solvent store). Place the A pairs first, keep X pairs apart, then fit the space.

    Layout of a flexible packaging plant

    Rolls, solvents and hygieneMaterial flows as heavy rolls (300 kg to 2 t), so the layout is designed around roll handling: overhead cranes or roll trolleys, wide aisles, roll racks, and short moves between printer, laminator and slitter. The classic flow is store → extrusion → printing → lamination → curing room → slitting → pouch making → QC → packing → dispatch. Special zones: solvent and ink storage away from ignition sources and near the presses' ink kitchens (X-rating with welding and electrical rooms); curing rooms with temperature control next to lamination; food-grade areas for pouch making with controlled access, pest control and no glass. Solvent recovery or incineration plants sit outside near the gravure hall. Plan the cylinder store and engraving next to the presses. Leave space in the line for the next press.

    In a machine shop the same thinking leads to cells: a lathe, a milling machine and a grinder for one family of shafts in a U, with one or two operators walking the cell.

    Worked example

    Two layouts comparedFlows (rolls/day): Store→Print 40, Print→Lam 36, Lam→Slit 34, Slit→Pouch 20, Slit→Dispatch 14, Pouch→Dispatch 20. Layout 1 (machines where they fitted over the years): total load × distance = 9 860 roll·m/day Layout 2 (in flow sequence, U-shaped): 4 120 roll·m/day → 58 % less travel. At 2 min per 50 m by forklift: about 3 h of handling saved per day, fewer roll edge damages.

    How to plan a layout

    1. Fix the product families and volumes for the next 5–10 years, not just today.
    2. Draw the process flow for each family and build the from–to chart.
    3. Rate closeness (A, E, I, O, U, X) including safety, noise, hygiene and utilities.
    4. Calculate space for each area: machines, operator space, WIP, aisles, maintenance access.
    5. Make two or three block layouts; compare load × distance, safety and expansion.
    6. Choose handling methods and aisle widths; check fire exits and fire-fighting access.
    7. Walk the layout with operators and maintenance before you pour concrete.

    Lab: from–to layout planner

    Seven departments sit in seven bays. Swap bays to put high-flow pairs next to each other. Line thickness shows flow; the total load × distance updates.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Design the aisles first. Machines can move; columns, pits and fire exits cannot.
    • Keep WIP space small on purpose — floor space fills up with whatever it is given.
    • Do a spaghetti diagram: trace one operator's or one roll's path on the plan for an hour. The tangle tells the story.
    • Keep incompatible areas apart: solvents and hot work, food zones and maintenance workshops.

    The cost structure

    ElementBehaviourFlexible packaging share (typical)
    Direct material (films, resins, inks, adhesives, solvents)Variable65–80 % of selling price
    Direct labourSemi-fixed (shifts are paid whether busy or not)3–6 %
    Power, fuel, utilitiesMostly variable3–6 %
    Consumables (doctor blades, sleeves, tapes, cores)Variable1–2 %
    Depreciation, interest, rent, salaries, insuranceFixed6–12 %

    Because material dominates, in converting a 1 % gain in material yield (Day 26) is often worth more than a 10 % gain in labour productivity. In a machining job shop it is the other way round: machine and labour time dominate.

    The machine-hour rate

    MHR = (depreciation + interest + insurance + space + maintenance + power + operators + share of overheads) per year ÷ productive hours per year

    Productive hours, not calendar hours: a press that runs 6 000 productive hours a year must recover its costs in those hours. Every changeover hour is an hour the MHR is earning nothing.

    Contribution and break-even

    Contribution per unit = price − variable cost Break-even volume = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit Margin of safety = (actual volume − break-even volume) ÷ actual volume
    Use contribution, not full cost, for short-term decisions. Fixed costs are paid whatever you decide this month. An order that covers its variable cost and adds contribution helps profit — unless it takes bottleneck time from a better order. Then rank orders by contribution per bottleneck hour.

    In a flexible packaging plant

    Costing per kg and per thousandConverters sell by kg (rolls) or per 1 000 pieces (pouches). Material cost per kg of laminate comes from the structure (Day 28). Conversion cost = Σ (hours on each machine × its MHR) ÷ kg produced — and because the hours include make-ready, a 300 kg job carries far more conversion cost per kg than a 5 t job. That is why small jobs must carry a set-up charge or a minimum order quantity. Indicative MHRs (2026): blown-film line ₹2 500–4 000/h, 8–10 colour rotogravure ₹6 000–10 000/h, solvent-less laminator ₹2 500–4 000/h, pouch machine ₹900–1 500/h.

    How to cost a decision

    1. Write the decision: accept the order? make or buy? new machine? extra shift?
    2. List only the costs and revenues that change with the decision (relevant costs).
    3. Ignore sunk costs — money already spent.
    4. Check whether a constraint is involved; if so, use contribution per constraint hour.
    5. Add the non-money factors: quality risk, customer relationship, capacity for growth.
    6. Show the result as a range, not a single number, with the assumptions written down.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Absorbed cost lies at the margin. Dropping a "loss-making" product often raises loss, because its fixed cost stays.
    • Recalculate the MHR each year with actual productive hours — falling OEE quietly raises it.
    • Engineers: put a rupee figure on every proposal (Day 29). Managers: ask for the assumptions behind it.
    • Material price swings (resin, PET, aluminium foil) move margins more than any shop-floor action — use price-variation clauses in contracts.
