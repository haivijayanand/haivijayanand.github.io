    Boundary-value problems arrive in this chapter: instead of a starting value you have conditions at both ends (supports, free surfaces). The equations are still ODEs — just in space. The last three cards return to time: materials that flow, creep and crack.

     EI·d⁴y/dx⁴ = q(x) V = −EI·y‴, M = EI·y″, θ = y′ y deflection · q distributed load (N/m) · E Young's modulus · I second moment of area · V shear · M bending moment

    Plane sections stay plane and perpendicular to the neutral axis; curvature is proportional to bending moment, y″ = M/EI. Differentiate twice more using dM/dx = V and dV/dx = −q and you get the fourth-order equation. Four integrations, four constants — fixed by two conditions at each end: pinned (y = 0, M = 0), fixed (y = 0, θ = 0) or free (M = 0, V = 0).

    Equilibrium of a slice: dV/dx = −q and dM/dx = V. Geometry: strain at height z is ε = −z·κ, curvature κ = y″ for small slopes. Material: σ = Eε, so M = ∫σz dA = EI·y″. Combining: (EI·y″)″ = q, which is EI·y⁗ = q for constant EI.

    A straight column under compression has the trivial solution y = 0 for every load — but at special loads a bent shape also satisfies equilibrium. It is an eigenvalue problem: y = sin(πx/L) works only when P = π²EI/L². Above that load the straight column is unstable and bows sideways suddenly, long before the material yields if the column is slender.

    Cut the bent column at x: the internal moment balances P·y, so EI·y″ = −P·y. With k² = P/EI, y = A sin kx + B cos kx. Pinned ends: y(0) = 0 → B = 0; y(L) = 0 → A sin kL = 0.     Rubbers, polymers, gaskets, wood, bitumen and biological tissue are part spring, part fluid.
