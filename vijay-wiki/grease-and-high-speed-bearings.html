Grease: History, Nomenclature and Use in High-Speed Rolling Bearings

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · Updated 24 Nov 2026
TribologyLubricationRolling bearingsHigh-speed machineryAerospace

Grease is the most widely used lubricant for rolling bearings: about four out of five bearings run on it. It is simple, it seals, and it stays where it is put. At high speed, however, grease reaches its limits, and knowing where those limits lie is what separates a bearing that runs for years from one that overheats in its first hour. This article collects the history, the nomenclature and the engineering method in one place, with worked examples for bearings running at speeds of the order of 20,000 to 50,000 rpm.

Key takeaways

  • Grease is oil held in a thickener sponge. The base oil lubricates; the thickener stores, releases and seals.
  • Consistency is given by the NLGI grade (cone penetration). Most bearings use NLGI 2; high-speed bearings use NLGI 2–3.
  • The speed that matters is the speed factor n·dm (rpm × pitch diameter in mm), not rpm alone. A 22 mm bearing at 50,000 rpm can be easier to grease than a 75 mm bearing at 20,000 rpm.
  • Standard greases work up to about n·dm 0.3–0.5 million. Special synthetic greases reach about 1 million, and hybrid ceramic bearings with special grease reach 1.5–2 million. Above that, use oil.
  • In high-speed bearings, grease life, not fatigue life, usually sets the service interval. Grease life roughly halves for every 15 °C rise above about 70 °C.
  • Fill high-speed bearings sparingly (10–20 % of free volume) and run them in with stepped speeds.

1. History of grease

Ancient lubricants

The first greases were animal fats: tallow, lard and fish oils, smeared on axles and pivots. Residues found on Egyptian chariot axles from about 1400 BC have been described as tallow mixed with lime. Without knowing it, the users had made a crude calcium soap, the same chemistry as many later greases. Fat-and-lime mixtures remained the axle lubricant of carts, mills and wagons for more than 3,000 years.

Petroleum and the first industrial greases

After commercial oil drilling began in 1859, mineral oil became cheap and plentiful. Grease makers saponified fats with lime or caustic soda directly in mineral oil:

  • Calcium soap ("cup grease"): water-resistant and cheap, but its structure depends on bound water, so it is limited to about 60–70 °C.
  • Sodium soap ("fibre grease"): works at higher temperatures but washes out with water. It was used in early rolling bearings, wheel bearings and gear housings.

Standardisation (1920s–1930s)

The spread of ball and roller bearings in vehicles, aircraft and machine tools created demand for a lubricant that stays in the bearing. The National Lubricating Grease Institute (NLGI) was formed in 1933. The cone-penetration consistency scale that grew from that work is still the universal language of grease.

The lithium era (1940s)

Lithium soap greases, and especially lithium 12-hydroxystearate, appeared in the early 1940s. They offered water resistance, a dropping point near 190 °C and good shear stability in one product, and became the general-purpose "multipurpose grease". Wartime aviation pushed development further. Aircraft needed a grease that would let controls move freely at −54 °C at altitude and survive heat near engines. This produced the first diester synthetic base oils for grease and the first low-temperature aircraft grease specifications.

Complex and non-soap thickeners (1950s–1980s)

  • Complex soaps (lithium, aluminium and calcium complex) pushed dropping points above 250 °C.
  • Clay (bentonite) and silica-gel thickeners have no true melting point. These "non-melt" greases suit high-temperature, slow-moving points and wheel bearings.
  • Polyurea is ashless, very resistant to oxidation and long-lived at high temperature. It is now standard for sealed-for-life electric motor bearings.
  • Perfluoropolyether (PFPE) oils (1960s) made possible chemically inert greases for oxygen, fuel, vacuum and space service.
  • Calcium sulfonate complex (1980s) offers very high load capacity and water resistance for steel mills and marine use.

Today

Most military material specifications have been converted to performance specifications (the MIL-G to MIL-PRF change of the 1990s). Development now focuses on long-life greases for hybrid ceramic bearings, low-noise greases for electric-vehicle motors, biodegradable greases and greases for electrically stressed bearings.

2. What grease is

When the thickener can no longer release oil, because the oil has evaporated or bled away, or the thickener has oxidised and hardened, the grease has reached the end of its life, even if the bearing still looks full of grease.

4.3 ISO 6743-9 (family X)

ISO 6743-9 classifies greases by operating conditions rather than chemistry. A code such as ISO-L-XBCEB 2 gives, after the family letter X, the lowest operating temperature, the highest operating temperature, the water and rust condition, the extreme-pressure requirement, and finally the NLGI grade. It describes what the grease must do, not what it is made of, and is used less often than DIN 51825 in bearing selection.

6. Grease in high-speed rolling bearings

6.1 The speed factor n·dm

What limits grease is the rolling speed of the balls and the shear rate in the grease, both of which scale with n·dm: shaft speed in rpm multiplied by the bearing pitch diameter dm = (d + D)/2 in mm. Older literature uses DN = rpm × bore in mm, which is slightly smaller for the same bearing.

LubricationTypical n·dm limit (ball bearings)
Standard lithium or polyurea grease, steel balls0.3–0.5 × 10⁶
High-speed synthetic grease, steel balls, good cage0.8–1.0 × 10⁶
Special grease with hybrid ceramic balls and optimised cage1.2–2.0 × 10⁶
Oil-air / oil mistup to about 2.0–2.5 × 10⁶
Oil jet / under-race oil (turbine main shafts)2.0–3.5 × 10⁶

Roller bearings have lower limits than ball bearings, roughly half for cylindrical rollers and much lower for spherical and tapered rollers, because of roller-end sliding.

6.2 Physics of grease in a high-speed bearing

  1. Churning and channelling. A stiff, shear-stable grease (NLGI 2–3; polyurea, lithium complex or barium complex) is pushed aside after start-up and stays there. A soft or unstable grease keeps flowing back into the ball path and churns.
  2. Run-in. A freshly greased high-speed bearing shows a temperature peak in the first minutes to hours, while excess grease is expelled (Figure 7). Raise the speed in steps and wait at each step until the temperature has stabilised.
  3. Fill quantity. General bearings are filled to 30–50 % of the free volume. High-speed bearings are filled to about 10–20 %; machine-tool spindle practice is often about 15 %. Overfilling is the commonest cause of overheating after maintenance.
  4. Starvation. At high speed, centrifugal force and rapid over-rolling stop oil from flowing back into the track. The film then depends on the bleed rate and on the base oil viscosity at the actual temperature.
  5. Viscosity trade-off. A higher base oil viscosity gives a thicker film but more churning heat. High-speed greases use low-viscosity base oils, about 15–30 cSt at 40 °C, because speed itself builds the elastohydrodynamic film.
  6. Skidding. A lightly loaded high-speed bearing can skid: the balls slide instead of rolling, smearing the raceways. A minimum load or axial spring preload prevents this.

6.3 Grease life: the real limit at high speed

In a sealed or rarely relubricated high-speed bearing, the grease usually fails long before the steel reaches its fatigue life. Grease life:

  • roughly halves for every 10–15 °C above about 70 °C, mainly through faster oxidation and evaporation (Figure 8);
  • falls as n·dm rises, since higher speed means more shear and more heat;
  • is shortened further by vertical shafts, vibration, outer-ring rotation, contamination and moisture.

6.4 Design measures that make grease work at high speed

MeasureEffect
Hybrid ceramic balls (silicon nitride)About 40 % lower ball mass, so lower centrifugal and gyroscopic loads, less heat, less skidding damage. Grease life at a given speed can roughly double.
Outer-ring-guided cages of phenolic resin or polyimideThe porous cage soaks up oil and acts as a second reservoir; outer-ring guidance keeps it stable at speed.
Shields or low-friction sealsKeep grease in and dirt out. Non-contact shields are preferred at high speed.
Correct preloadToo light: skidding and smearing. Too heavy: heat and short grease life. Spring preload is common in fans and motors.
Measured fill in clean conditions10–20 % of free volume, cleaned bearings, filtered grease.
Temperature controlEvery 15 °C saved roughly doubles grease life. Housing cooling, airflow or lower losses all count.

6.5 Additional requirements in aerospace

  • Wide temperature range: cold soak to −54 °C at altitude and 150–200 °C near engines. Ester and PAO base oils dominate.
  • Low-temperature starting torque, which affects the size of starters, motors and actuators.
  • Fluid compatibility with hydraulic fluids, fuel, de-icing fluids and cleaners. Esters can swell some elastomers; PFPE is inert but gives weaker rust protection.
  • Vibration, which causes false brinelling and fretting in bearings that stand still or oscillate. Anti-fretting greases and correct preload help.
  • Outgassing in vacuum (space mechanisms): PFPE or very low vapour pressure greases.
  • Qualification and traceability: qualified products list, batch control, limited shelf life (often 3–5 years).
  • Compatibility when changing grease: some thickener pairs (for example clay with lithium) can soften or harden badly when mixed. Purge completely when changing type.

6.6 Failure symptoms and likely causes

Condition monitoring tip

Grease deterioration usually shows first as a rise in high-frequency vibration energy (envelope or high-frequency bands) and in bearing temperature, before discrete bearing defect frequencies appear in the spectrum.

7. Formulas used in this article

Speed factor

dm = (d + D) / 2 [mm] n·dm = n × dm [mm·rpm]

Required viscosity (ISO 281) and viscosity ratio κ

ν1 = 45000 · n^-0.83 · dm^-0.5 for n < 1000 rpm ν1 = 4500 · n^-0.5 · dm^-0.5 for n ≥ 1000 rpm κ = ν / ν1 (ν = base oil viscosity at operating temperature)

κ ≥ 1 means a full film. κ between 0.4 and 1 means mixed lubrication. ν at temperature is found from ν40 and ν100 with the Walther equation (ASTM D341).

Relubrication interval (classic SKF formula, good lithium grease, horizontal shaft, 70 °C)

tf = K · [ 14×10⁶ / (n·√d) − 4d ] [hours] K = 10 radial ball bearings; 5 cylindrical roller and needle; 1 spherical, tapered, thrust Above 70 °C: halve tf for every 15 °C. Vertical shaft: halve. A negative result means grease is not viable.

Modern manufacturer charts give longer intervals for high-performance synthetic greases, often 2–4 times. Use this formula as a conservative first estimate.

Grease quantity

Free volume V = (π/4)·B·(D² − d²)·10⁻³ − G / 7.8×10⁻³ [cm³] (G = bearing mass, kg) Initial fill = 30–50 % of V (normal speed); 10–20 % of V (high speed) Relubrication Gp = 0.005 · D · B [g]

Friction heat (Palmgren) and oil flow needed to carry it away

M0 = 10⁻⁷ · f0 · (ν·n)^(2/3) · dm³ [N·mm] if ν·n ≥ 2000 (else 160×10⁻⁷·f0·dm³) M1 = 0.5 · μ · F · d [N·mm] (μ ≈ 0.0010–0.0020 for ball bearings) P = (M0 + M1) · ω / 1000 [W], ω = 2πn/60 Q = P / (ρ · c · ΔT) [m³/s] f0 ≈ 1 grease after run-in (≈ 2 freshly packed), 1.5–2 oil bath, 2–4 oil jet

Minimum load to avoid skidding (radial ball bearings, SKF)

Frm = kr · (ν·n / 1000)^(2/3) · (dm / 100)² [kN] kr = 0.025 (60, 618 series), 0.03 (62 series), 0.04 (63 series)

What it shows

  • At 12,000 rpm this bearing is at the edge of what grease can do. The conservative interval is only about 100 h at 90 °C. A good high-speed synthetic grease does several times better, but the interval is still hundreds of hours, not thousands. Grease life, not fatigue, sets maintenance.
  • The mineral grease gives the better film (κ 2.7) but nearly twice the churning heat. Heat raises temperature, and temperature halves grease life, so the low-viscosity synthetic grease is the better choice even with κ ≈ 1.15.
  • About 190 N of radial load is needed to prevent skidding. A lightly loaded shaft needs axial spring preload, or an angular contact pair with light preload.
  • Fill about 3 g, not "full", and run in at stepped speeds such as 3k → 6k → 9k → 12k rpm.
  • At 24,000 rpm (n·dm 1.44 M) grease is not viable with steel balls. Use hybrid ceramic bearings with a special grease and planned short re-packs, or change to oil-air lubrication for continuous duty.

What it shows

A 0.24 kg bearing holding a few grams of grease cannot shed 270 W by conduction. Its temperature would run away and the grease would fail within hours. Oil does two jobs here: it lubricates and it carries the heat away. That is why high-speed gearbox bearings are jet- or splash-lubricated from the gearbox oil system. With κ only about 1.2, oil cleanliness (filtration) and oil inlet temperature directly govern bearing life. The same bearing size at 50,000 rpm would reach n·dm 3 × 10⁶, which is turbine main-shaft territory and needs jet or under-race oil.

Example 4 — Miniature bearing at 50,000 rpm (when grease still works)

Deep groove ball bearing 608 (8 × 22 × 7 mm, 12 g) in a high-speed cooling fan or small motor at 50,000 rpm and 80 °C, shielded, with a high-speed synthetic grease (25 / 5 cSt).

What it shows

Speed in rpm alone does not decide the lubrication method. At 50,000 rpm this small bearing has a lower n·dm than the 45 mm bearing at 20,000 rpm, and only about 9 W to shed, so it runs well on grease. Such bearings are sealed for life: the bearing is replaced, not relubricated, and the replacement interval comes from grease life. Choosing a hybrid ceramic version and a long-life grease, and keeping the bearing cooler, extends that interval.
