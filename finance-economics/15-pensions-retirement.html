Pensions & Retirement

How to pay for 20–30 years of life after work ends. Covers India's pension schemes from EPF and NPS to the new Unified Pension Scheme, how much to save and when to start, and the economics of ageing societies. Pick your level.

Level 1 · For school students

One day your parents and grandparents stop working. How do they still buy food and medicine? With money they saved while working: their pension.

1What is retirement? What is a pension?

Retirement is when a person stops working, usually around age 58–65. A pension is a regular income they get after retiring, paid from money saved during their working years.

People in India now live longer: many live to 80 or more. So savings must last 20 to 30 years without a salary, while prices keep rising (Topic 01).

2Watch it happen: filling the tank

Think of retirement savings as a water tank. While working, you pour in a little every year. After retiring, you take some out every year. 3Who pays pensions in India?

🏛️ Government jobsOlder staff get a fixed pension from the government. Newer staff save in NPS, and central staff can choose the new UPS, which promises half their last pay.
🏢 Company jobsWorker and employer each put 12% of basic pay into EPF, which pays interest every year.
🧺 Farmers, shopkeepers, workersMost have no employer pension. They can join Atal Pension Yojana or NPS, and the poorest elderly get a small government old-age pension.

4Three lessons

  1. Start early. Money saved young has many years to grow (compounding, Topic 09).
  2. Don't touch it. Taking retirement money out early for other things leaves the tank empty later.
  3. Beat inflation. Savings must grow faster than prices, or they buy less each year.

2Calculator: how much do you need?

The corpus needed is the value at retirement of expenses that keep rising with inflation until the plan's end age. Monthly saving assumes a fixed SIP until retirement. Raising the SIP each year with your salary lowers the starting amount. Returns are not guaranteed.

3NPS or UPS? (central government staff)

UPS: the assured optionPredictable, inflation-indexed pension and family pension, like OPS but funded by contributions. The government keeps its contribution and returns, and there is a smaller lump sum. Best for those who value certainty and expect long service.
NPS: the market optionYour corpus is yours, including the government's 14% share, and is passed on fully to heirs. The pension depends on market returns and annuity rates at retirement. It may beat UPS for long equity-heavy careers, or fall short.

Take-up of UPS has been low (about 1.18 lakh by July 2026). The switch window closed on 30 November 2025; staff who did nothing stay in NPS. UPS members keep a one-time option to move back to NPS before retirement.

4Common mistakes

  • Withdrawing EPF when changing jobs. Transfer it instead; withdrawals lose decades of tax-free compounding.
  • Too little equity when young. 25–30 years is long enough for equities to matter (Topic 07); shift gradually towards debt near retirement.
  • Ignoring health costs. Medical bills rise faster than general prices; keep health cover beyond employment (Topic 14).
  • Mixing goals. Children's education and weddings compete with retirement. Children can borrow for education; you cannot borrow for retirement.
  • Underestimating longevity. Plan to 85–90, not to the average life expectancy.

5After retirement: making the money last

  • Bucket approach: 2–3 years of expenses in safe instruments (SCSS, FDs, liquid funds), the next 5–7 years in debt funds, and the rest in a modest equity share to keep up with inflation.
  • Annuities give lifelong income and remove longevity risk, but rates (around 6–7%) are fixed and usually not inflation-linked.
  • Withdrawal rate: taking out more than about 3–4% of the corpus a year, rising with inflation, risks running out over 30 years in India's higher-inflation setting.

6Pensions and the country

  • Fiscal cost: pensions are among the biggest items in state budgets. Several states announced a return to OPS in 2022–23; the RBI warned this would burden future taxpayers, since OPS is unfunded.
  • Coverage gap: about 80–90% of workers are informal (Topic 11) and have no employer pension, so most elderly rely on family.
  • Capital markets: EPF and NPS money funds government bonds and, through equity, Indian companies (Topics 07 and 08).

Level 3 · Review for experts

The life-cycle model, defined benefit vs defined contribution, pay-as-you-go vs funding, longevity and sequence risk, behavioural design, and India's demographic window.

1The life-cycle model

max Σ β^t u(c_t) s.t. Σ c_t/(1+r)^t = W_0 + Σ y_t/(1+r)^t → consumption smoothing; saving = y_t − c_t, positive in working years, negative after
  • Modigliani–Brumberg (1954) and Friedman's permanent income: households save in mid-life to smooth consumption. In aggregate, saving depends on growth and the age structure, which is why the "demographic dividend" years are also high-saving years.
  • Empirical puzzles include the retirement-consumption drop, under-saving (present bias), the low uptake of annuities, and high saving by Indian households in physical assets (gold, land) rather than pensions.

2DC replacement rate vs a 50% promise: calculator

Wage at year t (final = 1): w_t = (1+g)^{t−N}. Corpus = Σ c·w_t·(1+r)^{N−t}. Pension = Corpus / ä_D(r_a). Replacement rate = Pension / final wage

All in real terms, against final pay. The UPS benchmark of 50% is of the last 12 months' average basic pay, and is indexed. The 24% default is NPS's 10% employee + 14% government contribution. A real annuity rate assumes an inflation-indexed annuity, which is scarce in India; a nominal annuity's real value erodes.

3Pay-as-you-go vs funded

  • Aaron (1966) / Samuelson (1958): PAYG yields an implicit return of population growth plus productivity growth (n + g). Funding yields r. When r > n + g, which is typical as populations age, funding dominates, but the transition generation pays twice.
  • OPS is an unfunded DB promise: its liability appears only as future pension spending. Moving to NPS in 2004 converted it into explicit current contributions. Reverting brings short-run fiscal relief (no contributions) and long-run cost.
  • UPS is a hybrid: funded contributions with a government guarantee topping up to the assured level. The fiscal risk shifts to the gap between assured benefits and fund performance.

4Longevity, sequence and inflation risk

  • Longevity risk: individuals cannot diversify it, but annuity pools can. The "annuity puzzle" (Yaari, 1965, predicts full annuitisation) persists because of bequest motives, health-cost liquidity needs, adverse selection in pricing and distrust.
  • Sequence-of-returns risk: the same average return gives very different outcomes depending on when losses occur. Losses near retirement do most damage. Glide paths (lifecycle funds, NPS auto choice) reduce equity exposure with age.
  • Inflation risk: India lacks a deep market for inflation-indexed annuities, so DA-indexed government pensions are uniquely valuable.

5Behavioural design

  • Defaults dominate (Madrian and Shea, 2001). Auto-enrolment with opt-out, as in the UK and New Zealand, raises participation sharply. India's EPF is mandatory for covered firms; NPS for private workers is voluntary and has low take-up.
  • Save More Tomorrow (Thaler and Benartzi, 2004): pre-commit increases to future pay rises. Lifecycle default funds and simple choice sets reduce inertia costs.
  • Liquidity versus lock-in: India allows generous EPF withdrawals (housing, illness, unemployment), which helps in emergencies but leaks retirement adequacy.

6India's demographic window

  • The elderly share rises from 8.4% (2011) to about 15% (2036), with wide state variation: southern states are ageing well ahead of the north. India risks "growing old before growing rich".
  • A universal non-contributory social pension, paid for by taxes, is debated as the floor beneath contributory schemes. Costs at 0.5–1% of GDP depend on the amount and targeting.
  • Female longevity, lower lifetime earnings and widowhood make old-age poverty disproportionately female.
