    Life equation

    Rolling bearings fail by surface fatigue (spalling) of the races. Life scatters widely, so it is stated statistically: L10 is the life that 90 % of a group of identical bearings reach or exceed.

    L10 = (C / P)p million revolutions p = 3 ball, 10/3 roller
    L10h = L10 × 10⁶ / (60·n) hours
    P = (X·Fr + Y·Fa) × fs (deep groove ball: X = 1, Y = 0 if Fa/Fr ≤ e; else X = 0.56, Y from Fa/C₀)
    Reliability: Ln = a₁·L10 (a₁: 95 % 0.64; 97 % 0.47; 99 % 0.25)
    Modern ISO 281: Lnm = a₁·aISO·L10, aISO from lubrication and contamination
    MachineDesign life L10h
    Hand tools, occasional use500–4 000 h
    8 h/day, not fully used (gear drives, motors)12 000–20 000 h
    8 h/day, fully used (machine tools, fans)20 000–30 000 h
    24 h continuous (compressors, pumps, test rigs)40 000–60 000 h
    Power stations, mines — high reliability100 000+ h

    Static capacity C₀ governs slow, oscillating or stationary bearings under shock: P₀ ≤ C₀/s₀ with s₀ = 1–2 (≥ 2 for shock).

    Arrangement, fits and lubrication

    Locating + floatingOne bearing fixes the shaft axially both ways; the other floats (NU roller, or a ball bearing free in its housing) to absorb thermal growth.
    Paired (X or O)Two angular contact or tapered roller bearings face to face or back to back, adjusted to light preload — stiff, for spindles and pinions.
    FitsThe ring that rotates relative to the load gets the interference fit (inner ring on a rotating shaft: k5/m5/m6; outer ring in a still housing: H7 or J7).
    LubricationGrease for most speeds below about half the limiting speed; oil bath/splash in gearboxes; circulating oil for high speed and heat. Clean lubricant multiplies life (aISO).

    Materials and cost

    Bearings are made from through-hardened 100Cr6 (AISI 52100) at 58–65 HRC; case-carburised steels for large or shock-loaded rollers; ceramic balls for high speed. Price rises steeply with size and precision class (P0 normal → P6 → P5 → P4 spindle grade). A bearing one size larger in the same series often costs only 20–40 % more and lasts twice as long — and bearing replacement downtime usually costs far more than the bearing.

    Worked example

    Fr = 3.5 kN, Fa = 1.2 kN, 1440 rpm, gear drive (fs = 1.2), 20 000 h wantedTry 6306 (C 29.6, C₀ 16 kN): Fa/C₀ = 0.075 → e = 0.27; Fa/Fr = 0.34 > e → X = 0.56, Y = 1.60 P = 1.2 × (0.56 × 3.5 + 1.60 × 1.2) = 4.66 kN L10 = (29.6/4.66)³ = 257 million rev = 2 970 h ✗ Needed C ≈ P × (20 000 × 60 × 1440 / 10⁶)^(1/3) = P × 12. A larger bearing has a larger C₀, so Y rises and P ≈ 5 kN → C ≈ 60 kN: a 6310 (C ≈ 62 kN) gives about 20 000 h, or use a tapered roller pair if the space is tight.

    Selection procedure

    1. Find radial and axial loads at each bearing from the shaft analysis (Days 13, 23).
    2. Choose the type from loads, speed, misalignment and the locating/floating role.
    3. Set the required life (table) and reliability; find required C = P·L101/p.
    4. Pick a bearing with bore = shaft seat and C ≥ required; recalculate X, Y with its C₀.
    5. Check static capacity, limiting speed and the maker's adjusted life (aISO).
    6. Specify fits, shoulder heights, sealing and lubrication; draw the arrangement.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Most bearings do not die of fatigue: they die of contamination, poor lubrication, misalignment, and wrong fits. Seal well.
    • Very lightly loaded roller bearings skid and smear — they need a minimum load (about 0.02 C).
    • Never hammer a bearing through its rolling elements; press on the ring being fitted, or heat it (induction heater, max 120 °C).
    • Vibration monitoring (bearing defect frequencies, envelope analysis) finds spalls months before failure.

    Lubricants and viscosity

    Dynamic viscosity μ (Pa·s; 1 mPa·s = 1 cP) is resistance to shear; kinematic viscosity ν = μ/ρ (mm²/s = cSt). Industrial oils are graded by ISO VG = ν at 40 °C: VG 32 (hydraulic, spindles), VG 68 (general bearings, turbines), VG 150–320 (gearboxes), VG 460–680 (worm drives).

    Viscosity falls steeply with temperature — Walther (ASTM D341): log log(ν + 0.7) = A − B·log T
    Viscosity index (VI) measures how flat that curve is; mineral oils ≈ 95–105, synthetics 140+

    Regimes of lubrication

    BoundarySurfaces touch through thin adsorbed layers; μ 0.05–0.15. Starting, slow speed, bushes.
    MixedPartly film, partly contact; wear occurs.
    HydrodynamicRotation drags oil into a converging wedge and builds pressure; the shaft floats. μ 0.001–0.005. No wear in theory.
    EHLElastohydrodynamic: in gears and rolling bearings the film is microns thick, pressure stiffens the oil.

    The Stribeck curve plots friction against μN/P (viscosity × speed / pressure): it falls through the boundary and mixed regimes to a minimum, then rises slowly in the full-film regime. Design on the right of the minimum, with margin.

    Petroff and Sommerfeld

    Unit load P = W / (l·d) (MPa) N in rev/s
    Petroff (lightly loaded, concentric): f = 2π² · (μN / P) · (r / c)
    Sommerfeld number S = (r / c)² · μN / P (dimensionless)
    Raimondi–Boyd charts (l/d = 1, ½, ¼): from S read h₀/c, (r/c)·f, flow, temperature rise
    Minimum film (Trumpler): h₀ ≥ 0.005 + 0.00004·d mm

    Typical radial clearance c/r ≈ 0.001–0.002; l/d ≈ 0.5–1.0. Heat generated by friction raises the oil temperature, which lowers viscosity, which changes S — so solve iteratively until heat generated equals heat carried away.

    Dry and boundary bushes are designed by p·v (pressure × sliding velocity), which controls temperature and wear. A sintered bush costs a few rupees; a hydrodynamic bearing needs an oil system, but it carries enormous loads at high speed with near-infinite life — which is why every large turbine uses one.

    Worked example

    d = l = 60 mm, c = 0.04 mm, W = 8 kN, 1500 rpm, ISO VG 68 at 60 °CP = 8000/(60 × 60) = 2.22 MPa ν(60 °C) ≈ 28.7 cSt → μ = 28.7 × 0.87 = 24.9 mPa·s; N = 25 rev/s; r/c = 750 S = 750² × 0.0249 × 25 / 2.22×10⁶ = 0.158 Raimondi–Boyd (l/d 1): h₀/c ≈ 0.47 → h₀ ≈ 19 µm > Trumpler 7.4 µm ✓ f ≈ 0.0052 → friction power ≈ 0.0052 × 8000 × 0.03 × 2π × 25 ≈ 200 W

    Design procedure (hydrodynamic)

    1. Choose l/d (0.5–1) and d from the shaft; find P and check it against the material limit.
    2. Choose clearance (c/r ≈ 0.001–0.0015) and an oil grade.
    3. Assume an average film temperature; find μ.
    4. Calculate S; read h₀/c, friction, flow and temperature rise from Raimondi–Boyd.
    5. Iterate the temperature until the assumed and calculated values agree.
    6. Check h₀ against Trumpler, maximum oil temperature (≈ 70–90 °C), and oil flow; design grooves and supply.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Most journal bearings wear at start and stop, in boundary lubrication. Jacking oil (hydrostatic lift) helps large rotors.
    • Too little clearance overheats; too much lets the shaft whirl (oil whip) at high speed — tilting-pad bearings cure that.
    • Feed oil into the unloaded zone; a groove in the loaded zone destroys the pressure film.
    • Check oil viscosity at the real running temperature, not at 40 °C.

    Thin cylinders

    Hoop σt = p·d / (2t) Longitudinal σl = p·d / (4t) Thin sphere σ = p·d / (4t)
    Valid when t/d < about 1/20 (t/r < 0.1)

    The hoop stress is twice the longitudinal — that is why pipes and sausages split lengthwise. Codes (IS 2825, ASME VIII Div 1) add a joint efficiency for welded seams and a corrosion allowance: t = p·D/(2σ·η − p) + CA.

    Thick cylinders — Lamé

    In a thick wall the hoop stress is highest at the bore and falls outward:

    σt = A + B/r² σr = A − B/r²
    Internal pressure p only (open ends): σt = p·ri²/(ro² − ri²) · (1 + ro²/r²)
    At the bore: σt = p·(ro² + ri²)/(ro² − ri²), σr = −p
    Closed ends add σl = p·ri²/(ro² − ri²)
    Diminishing returns. As the wall grows, the bore hoop stress approaches p but never falls below it. Thick walls alone cannot carry very high pressures — use a stronger material, compound (shrunk) cylinders that pre-compress the bore, or autofrettage (over-pressurising once to yield the inner layers).

    For ductile materials use the von Mises or Tresca stress at the bore; for brittle ones the maximum principal stress.

    Press and shrink fits

    A hub with a bore slightly smaller than the shaft is pressed or shrunk on. The interference creates an interface pressure p that carries torque and axial force by friction — no keyway, no stress raiser from it (though the hub edge itself concentrates stress).

    Same material, solid shaft (ri = 0), radial interference δ/2: p = (E·δ / 2R) · (ro² − R²)(R² − ri²) / (2R²(ro² − ri²))
    Hub bore hoop stress σt = p·(ro² + R²)/(ro² − R²) Shaft surface σt = −p (solid shaft)
    Torque T = 2π·μ·p·R²·L Axial force F = 2π·μ·p·R·L
    Shrink temperature ΔT = (δ + assembly clearance) / (α·2R)

    μ for steel on steel: 0.1–0.15 pressed (oiled), 0.15–0.25 shrunk (dry). Choose the ISO fit (H7/s6, H7/u6 …, Day 9) that gives the needed minimum interference, and check the stresses at the maximum interference.

    Materials and cost

    Pressure vessels use weldable plate steels (IS 2002 boiler quality, SA516-70) with certified chemistry and impact tests — material traceability is part of the cost. Hydraulic cylinder tubes are cold-drawn and honed (ST52, E355). Shrink-fitted hubs replace a key and keyway machining; their cost is in tight tolerances and controlled heating (induction heater or oil bath).

    Procedure (interference fit)

    1. Find the torque and axial force to carry, with a factor of 1.5–2 on slip.
    2. Choose L, μ and find the minimum interface pressure pmin.
    3. Find the minimum interference from pmin; choose an ISO fit whose minimum interference exceeds it.
    4. At the maximum interference of that fit, check hub and shaft stresses (von Mises ≤ Sy/n).

    Why a flywheel

    A single-cylinder engine delivers torque only in its power stroke; a punching press needs a large torque for a fraction of a revolution. Without storage, speed would swing wildly or the motor would have to be sized for the peak. A flywheel absorbs energy when supply exceeds demand and returns it when demand exceeds supply. (A governor, by contrast, controls the mean speed over many cycles by adjusting fuel or power.)

    Energy fluctuation and inertia

    Turning moment T(θ) and mean torque Tm = work per cycle / angle per cycle
    Energy excess between two angles = ∫(T − Tm) dθ — the areas above and below the mean line
    ΔE = Emax − Emin (largest swing of the running sum of those areas)
    Coefficient of fluctuation of speed Cs = (ωmax − ωmin) / ωmean
    ΔE = I·ω²·Cs → I = ΔE / (ω²·Cs)
    MachineTypical Cs
    Crushers, hammers, punching presses0.1–0.2
    Pumps, machine tools, workshop drives0.02–0.05
    Textile, paper machinery0.01–0.02
    Generators, especially AC alternators0.003–0.005

    The coefficient of fluctuation of energy Ce = ΔE / work per cycle is high for single-cylinder engines and falls quickly with more cylinders — a four-cylinder engine needs a much smaller flywheel.

    Rim flywheel

    Rim mass (approx., arms and hub ≈ 10 % extra): m ≈ I / R²
    Rim speed v = ω·R; rim hoop stress ≈ ρ·v² (free ring)
    Cast iron rims: v ≤ 25–30 m/s; cast steel: ≤ 40–50 m/s; disc flywheels of forged steel go much faster

    Mass is cheapest at large radius: doubling R quarters the mass for the same I. But stress rises with v², so radius is limited by the rim speed and by the space available.

    Materials and cost

    Grey cast iron is the traditional material: cheap, easily cast with arms, damps vibration. Steel discs (plate, profiled) replace it for high speed and safety; flywheel bursts are violent, so modern energy-storage flywheels use composite rims in containment vessels. A heavier flywheel costs material and makes start-up slower; a lighter one costs speed variation, which shows up as poor quality (textiles, generators) or a bigger motor (presses).

    Worked example

    Single-cylinder four-stroke engine, 7.5 kW at 600 rpm, Cs = 0.02, rim radius 400 mmTm = 9550 × 7.5/600 = 119 N·m; work per cycle (4π) = 1.50 kJ From the turning-moment diagram (the lab's model engine): ΔE = 2.08 kJ (Ce ≈ 1.39) ω = 62.8 rad/s → I = 2081/(62.8² × 0.02) = 26.4 kg·m² Rim mass ≈ 26.4/0.4² = 165 kg (+ ~10 % for arms and hub) v = 25.1 m/s → σ ≈ 7200 × 25.1² = 4.5 MPa — fine for cast iron, but near its speed limit.

    Design procedure

    1. Draw (or obtain) the turning-moment diagram over one cycle and the resisting torque.
    2. Find the areas between the curves; build the running energy sum; ΔE = max − min.
    3. Choose Cs for the application; find I = ΔE/(ω²·Cs).
    4. Choose the radius from space and rim speed limit; find the rim mass and section.
    5. Check rim stress, arm bending, hub and key; balance the flywheel.
    6. For a press: check motor heating — the motor must restore ΔE between strokes.

    Lab: flywheel sizing

    Choose the machine type — single, two or four cylinders, or a punching press — and see the turning-moment diagram, ΔE, inertia and rim size. Try halving Cs.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Include the inertia of everything that rotates with the flywheel (rotor, pulley, gears referred by ratio²).
    • For a press, the motor need only supply the average power plus losses — the flywheel supplies the punch.
    • Flywheels on test rigs and drives also lower torsional natural frequencies — check Day 14.

    Reliability

    Reliability R(t) is the probability that a part works without failure up to time t. Mechanical lives are well described by the Weibull distribution:

    R(t) = exp(−(t/η)β) η = characteristic life (63.2 % have failed) Mean life = η·Γ(1 + 1/β)
    β < 1: infant mortality (early failures, poor assembly) β ≈ 1: random (constant failure rate) β > 1: wear-out (fatigue β ≈ 1.5–3)
    Series system: R = R₁·R₂·…·Rₙ Parallel (redundant): R = 1 − (1 − R₁)(1 − R₂)…

    A gearbox is a series system — two gears, four bearings, two seals, oil: each must work. Ten parts at 99 % give only 90 %. Redundancy (a standby pump, two brakes on a lift) raises reliability for critical functions. Rolling bearing L10 (Day 25) is the Weibull life at R = 0.9 with β ≈ 1.1–1.5.

    DFMA and value engineering

    Day 3 introduced DFMA. Value engineering asks of every feature: what is its function (verb + noun: "locate bearing", "transmit torque"), and what is the cheapest way to perform it? Typical wins: replace a machined spacer by a circlip; combine a bracket and a housing into one casting; replace a keyed hub with a taper-lock bush; use a standard gear motor instead of a custom gearbox.

    Life-cycle cost

    LCC = purchase + installation + Σ (energy + maintenance + downtime)ₜ / (1 + r)ᵗ − residual value
    Energy cost per year = (output kW / η) × hours × tariff

    For anything that runs many hours — motors, pumps, gearboxes, compressors — energy dominates: a motor's purchase price is often only a few per cent of what it costs to run over ten years. A 5 % efficiency gain usually pays back within one or two years. For low-use equipment (a manual hoist, a test fixture), purchase price and reliability matter more.

    Worked example

    Reliability: six parts in series, each Weibull β = 1.5, η = 20 000 h, mission 5000 hOne part: R = exp(−(5000/20 000)^1.5) = exp(−0.125) = 88.2 % Six in series: 0.882⁶ = 47 % — less than half the units survive 5000 h without any failure Mean life of one part = 20 000 × Γ(1.667) = 18 060 h Cost: drive A ₹60 000 at 88 %, drive B ₹85 000 at 93 %, 15 kW output, 4000 h/yr, ₹8/kWh, 10 % discount Energy A = 15/0.88 × 4000 × 8 = ₹5.45 lakh/yr; B = ₹5.16 lakh/yr → saves ₹29 000/yr B repays its extra ₹25 000 in the first year; over 10 years its NPV is ₹1.6 lakh lower.

    Procedure

    1. Write the reliability target (e.g. 95 % over the warranty) and the mission profile.
    2. Break the machine into a reliability block diagram (series/parallel).
    3. Assign life data to each block (bearing L10, seal life, gear rating, field data).
    4. Run an FMEA; act on the highest-risk items (design change first, inspection last).
    5. Apply DFMA/value engineering to remove parts and cost.
    6. Compare final options by life-cycle cost, not by price.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • "Mean time between failures" hides the shape: an MTBF of 20 000 h with β = 3 is very different from β = 1.
    • Field data beats handbooks. Log every failure with hours, cause and photo.
    • Include downtime cost — a ₹2000 seal that stops a production line for a shift costs lakhs.

    Practice

    1. A machine has 12 parts each 99.5 % reliable over a year. What is the system reliability? How many parts can it have for 95 %?
    2. Write a five-line FMEA for a belt drive.
    3. In the lab, find the efficiency at which option B stops paying back within 10 years.

    Capstone brief: two-stage spur gear reducer

    Specification. Electric motor 7.5 kW, 1440 rpm, driving a belt conveyor at about 72 rpm (overall ratio 20 : 1 ± 3 %). Service: 16 h/day, moderate shock (Cs = 1.25). Required bearing life 30 000 h. Gears in a cast-iron housing with splash lubrication. Input and output shafts parallel, flexible couplings at both ends. Estimate the cost for a batch of 10.

    Work through it yourself first, using the labs. Then compare with the reference outline below — there is no single right answer; there are well-argued ones.

    Plan of work

    1. Ratios (Day 21): split 20 : 1 into two stages, e.g. 5 × 4, with standard tooth numbers (20/100 and 20/80).
    2. Torques (Day 13): input T₁ = 9550 × 7.5/1440 = 49.7 N·m; intermediate ≈ 244 N·m; output ≈ 955 N·m (98 % per stage).
    3. Gears (Days 2, 22): choose materials and hardness; size the module and face width of each stage for bending and wear; check contact ratio and interference.
    4. Forces (Day 23): Ft and Fr on each gear; sketch directions.
    5. Shafts (Days 6, 13, 14): layout, bending moment diagrams, ASME and DE-Goodman diameters at every shoulder and keyway; deflection and slope at gears and bearings.
    6. Keys (Day 15): standard keys at each gear and coupling; lengths.
    7. Bearings (Day 25): reactions → equivalent loads → bearings for 30 000 h; locating/floating arrangement; fits (Day 9).
    8. Couplings (Day 15): select input and output couplings with service factor.
    9. Lubrication and heat (Days 24, 26): oil grade (VG 150–220 for spur gears at these speeds), oil level, losses ≈ 2 % per stage.
    10. Housing and fasteners (Days 3, 10, 11): split cast-iron housing, cover bolts, dowels, breather, drain, dipstick.
    11. Reliability and cost (Days 3, 29): FMEA of the top risks, BOM, cost estimate and life-cycle note.
    12. Drawings: general arrangement, gear and shaft details with tolerances, fits and finish, calculation file.

    Design review checklist

    • Every element checked for its governing criterion — strength, fatigue, wear, deflection, heat, life?
    • Every calculated size rounded up to a standard size, and rechecked?
    • Every bearing seat, gear bore and coupling bore with a fit, finish and geometric tolerance?
    • Fillets specified at all shoulders; keyways placed away from peak moment?
    • Assembly sequence possible? Can the bearings be removed for service?
    • Oil level, breather, drain, sight glass, lifting points, guards?
    • FMEA top items addressed; spare parts list made?
    • Calculation file complete, with sources of every data value?
