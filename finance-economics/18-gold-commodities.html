Level 1 · For school students

Commodities are the basic raw materials of life: things that are the same wherever they come from, like gold, oil, wheat and copper.

1What is a commodity?

A commodity is a basic material that is the same no matter who produces it: one gram of pure gold or one barrel of a given crude oil is like any other. That's why they are traded worldwide at one price.

3How oil reaches your plate

India buys almost 90% of its crude oil from abroad. When a war or a blocked sea route pushes oil prices up:

  1. Diesel and petrol cost more (or the government and oil companies absorb it for a while).
  2. Trucks charge more to carry vegetables, milk and goods.
  3. Fertilisers, plastics and airline tickets get costlier.
  4. India pays more dollars for oil, so the rupee weakens (Topic 05).
  5. Prices of many things rise: inflation (Topic 01).

4Why do commodity prices jump around?

  • Supply shocks: wars, floods, droughts, mine strikes or blocked shipping lanes cut supply suddenly.
  • Demand: when the world economy booms, factories need more oil and copper.
  • Fear: in scary times, people and central banks buy gold as a safe store of value.
  • Hard to change quickly: a new mine or oil field takes years, so prices move a lot before supply catches up.

2Calculator: jewellery vs coin vs ETF

GST of 3% applies when buying jewellery and coins (and on making charges); ETFs have no GST. Taxes on gains are not included. Hold gold for its role in a portfolio, not because it always rises; it fell for years after 2012.

3Gold and tax

  • Physical gold, gold mutual funds: held over 24 months → long-term gain taxed at 12.5% (no indexation); shorter → added to income.
  • Gold ETFs: held over 12 months → 12.5% long-term rate.
  • Sovereign Gold Bonds: interest taxable; gains at maturity tax-free; gains on early sale on the exchange are taxable.
  • Import duty of 15% since May 2026 is built into Indian prices, so Indian gold trades above the world price converted at the exchange rate.

4How much gold?

Many planners suggest 5–15% of investments in gold, as a diversifier. It often rises when stocks fall or the rupee weakens, but it pays no income and can fall for years (2012–2018 in rupee terms was flat to down). Household gold already held as jewellery counts towards this, so most Indian families already have plenty.

Gold loans (Topic 16) let you borrow against jewellery at 9–18%. They're useful in emergencies, but if prices fall you may be asked to top up or face auction.

5Oil, food and your budget

  • Oil: India imports about 85–90% of its crude. A $10 a barrel rise adds roughly $12–15 bn a year to the import bill. The crude import bill jumped about 56% year-on-year in April–July 2026 as the Hormuz crisis lifted prices.
  • Pump prices include large central and state taxes, so the government can cushion shocks by cutting duties, at a cost to the budget (Topic 08).
  • Food commodities: edible oils and pulses are partly imported; wheat, rice and sugar prices are managed through stocks, export bans and minimum support prices (Topic 10).
  • Commodity trading: MCX and NCDEX offer futures, which are leveraged and risky. Most individuals should not speculate in them.

6Commodities and the country

  • Gold and oil together are India's two largest import bills, so their prices drive the current account deficit and the rupee (Topic 05). That's why the government raised gold duty in May 2026 while oil was high.
  • Indian households are estimated to hold over 25,000 tonnes of gold, one of the world's largest private stocks, largely unproductive. Gold monetisation schemes have had limited success.
  • Energy transition: copper, lithium and rare earths are the "new oil". India is building critical-mineral partnerships to secure supply.

Level 3 · Review for experts

Futures pricing and the term structure, exhaustible resources, gold's drivers, oil shocks and macroeconomics, and India's commodity exposure.

1Cost of carry and the term structure: calculator

F(T) = S · e^{(r + u − y)·T} r = interest rate, u = storage cost, y = convenience yield F > S: contango (ample stocks) F < S: backwardation (scarcity, high y)

Theory of storage (Kaldor; Working; Brennan): the convenience yield rises as inventories fall. A supply crisis like the 2026 Hormuz disruption typically produces steep backwardation. For gold, y ≈ 0 and stocks are huge, so gold futures sit in mild contango near r + storage (lease rates aside).

2Exhaustible resources and supercycles

  • Hotelling (1931): the scarcity rent on an exhaustible resource rises at the rate of interest. Empirically, technology and discoveries swamp this, and real prices of most minerals show no long-run uptrend.
  • Prebisch–Singer: a secular decline in commodity terms of trade relative to manufactures; contested, with long supercycles (Erten and Ocampo) driven by large demand shocks (US post-war, China 2000s).
  • Supply inelasticity in the short run and long investment lags generate cobweb-like boom-bust cycles in capacity.

3What drives gold?

  • Real interest rates: gold pays no yield, so its opportunity cost is the real rate. Historically gold moved inversely with US TIPS yields, a relationship that weakened after 2022 as central-bank buying took over.
  • Official sector: after the 2022 freezing of Russian reserves, emerging-market central banks have bought about 1,000 t a year (2022–24) and kept buying heavily in 2026, citing sanctions risk and diversification away from the dollar.
  • Safe haven and the currency channel: rupee gold returns equal dollar gold returns plus rupee depreciation, plus duty changes.
  • Bubbles and momentum: the 2025–January 2026 surge and the roughly 25% fall since show momentum and positioning effects that fundamentals do not fully explain.

4Oil shocks and the macroeconomy

  • Hamilton: most post-war US recessions were preceded by oil price spikes, with asymmetric effects (increases hurt more than decreases help).
  • Kilian (2009): distinguishes supply shocks, aggregate demand shocks and precautionary demand shocks. Supply-driven spikes, like the 2026 Gulf disruptions, are the most harmful to importers.
  • For India: higher crude worsens the current account (roughly 0.3–0.5% of GDP per $10/bbl), the fiscal position (if taxes are cut) and inflation (direct fuel plus pass-through). The RBI faces a supply-shock dilemma (Topic 02).
  • Policy buffers: Strategic Petroleum Reserves (about 5.3 million tonnes), diversified sourcing (Russia, US, Africa, Latin America), rupee trade arrangements, and the energy transition.

5Commodity policy in India

  • Gold import duty as a balance-of-payments tool: raised to 10% in 2013 and about 15% in 2022, cut to 6% in July 2024 (smuggling had surged), raised back to 15% in May 2026. Smuggling responds to the duty gap.
  • Food: stock limits, export bans and MSP operations stabilise domestic prices but distort trade and farmer incentives (Topic 10).
  • Derivatives: SEBI regulates commodity derivatives. Agricultural futures in several contracts have been suspended since 2021, which is debated as hurting price discovery.

6Review checklist: open questions

  1. Is the 2026 central-bank gold bid structural, and what does it imply for real-rate models?
  2. Does a 15% duty cut India's gold imports durably, or mainly shift them to smuggling?
  3. How large should India's strategic oil reserves be, given Hormuz exposure?
  4. Can household gold be monetised productively (gold-backed lending, deposit schemes)?
  5. Should agricultural futures be revived, and with what safeguards?
