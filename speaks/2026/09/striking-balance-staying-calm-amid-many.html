Striking a Balance: Staying Calm Amid the Many Emotions Around Us

4 Sep 20268 min read

Life is a continuous process of communication.

Communication does not happen only through words. It happens through expressions, gestures, silence, movement, expectations, behaviour, and even presence.

A fish in an aquarium swims toward us when we approach the tank. A pet dog waits near the door, wags its tail, or looks at us in a particular way. Without speaking a single word, it communicates excitement, hunger, affection, fear, or expectation.

Human relationships communicate at an even deeper level.

Our spouse may express happiness, disappointment, concern, affection, anger, or expectations. Our children communicate their needs, fears, excitement, frustrations, and dreams. Parents, relatives, friends, neighbours, and office colleagues continuously interact with us, each carrying their own emotional world.

Every interaction creates an emotional signal.

And consciously or unconsciously, we receive it.

Different Relationships, Different Emotional Intensities

Not every relationship affects us equally.

A casual remark from a stranger may disappear from our mind within minutes. The same words spoken by someone very close to us may remain in our thoughts for several days.

This happens because emotional influence is often proportional to emotional closeness.

The happiness of our children can immediately elevate our mood. Their disappointment may disturb us. Appreciation from a colleague can energise us, while criticism from someone we respect can make us question ourselves.

Our emotional state therefore keeps moving throughout the day.

A pleasant conversation lifts us.

An argument pulls us down.

Recognition at work creates excitement.

An unexpected problem creates anxiety.

A kind word creates warmth.

An unanswered message may create unnecessary doubt.

In this constantly changing emotional environment, the challenge is not to avoid emotions. That would neither be practical nor desirable.

The challenge is to remain centred while experiencing them.

The Two Sources of Emotional Disturbance

Our emotional state is broadly influenced by two forces: what happens around us and what happens within us.

External influences include the behaviour of people, workplace pressures, family situations, financial matters, traffic, news, social interactions, unexpected events, success, failure, appreciation, criticism, and countless other circumstances.

But an equally powerful influence comes from within.

Sometimes nothing has actually happened, yet our mind creates an entire emotional situation.

We imagine.

We forecast.

We assume.

We interpret.

We predict.

We replay old conversations.

We construct future possibilities.

A simple delay in someone's response may quickly become:

“Perhaps something is wrong.”

“They may be unhappy with me.”

“Maybe the situation has changed.”

“What if this leads to something worse?”

Within a few minutes, imagination can create anxiety around an event that has not even occurred.

The external world provides one signal. The mind sometimes creates ten additional signals around it.

This is why emotional balance requires us to manage not only circumstances but also our interpretation of circumstances.

Facts, Feelings, and Stories

One useful habit is to separate three things whenever we become emotionally disturbed:

What actually happened?

What am I feeling about it?

What story am I creating around it?

Suppose a colleague does not respond to a message.

The fact is simple: there has been no response yet.

The feeling may be disappointment or concern.

But the story created by the mind could be much larger:

“He is deliberately ignoring me.”

“He disagrees with me.”

“There must be some problem.”

The story may eventually prove correct, but until evidence exists, it remains an assumption.

Much emotional suffering comes not from facts, but from the stories we attach to them.

Learning to distinguish between the two is a powerful form of emotional discipline.

Being Sensitive Without Becoming Emotionally Controlled

Emotional balance should never mean becoming insensitive.

A person who does not care about anyone will naturally experience fewer emotional disturbances from relationships. But that is not emotional maturity. It is emotional distance.

The better goal is to remain sensitive while developing stability.

We should listen when our spouse expresses concern.

We should notice when our children need attention.

We should understand the expectations of colleagues.

We should respond to the needs of people around us.

But their emotions need not automatically become our emotions.

Someone else's anger does not necessarily require our anger.

Someone else's anxiety does not require our anxiety.

Someone else's excitement does not require an impulsive decision from us.

We can acknowledge another person's emotional state without surrendering our own mental balance.

That difference is subtle but powerful.

The Pause Between Stimulus and Response

Much of emotional maturity lies in a very small space: the gap between what happens and how we respond.

When something triggers us, our first reaction is often automatic.

Anger immediately wants to speak.

Fear immediately wants to withdraw.

Excitement immediately wants to act.

Ego immediately wants to defend.

Anxiety immediately wants certainty.

But not every emotion requires immediate action.

Sometimes the most intelligent response is simply to create a small pause.

A few seconds before replying.

A short walk before taking a decision.

A night's sleep before reacting to a major issue.

A few deep breaths before continuing an argument.

Time allows emotional intensity to reduce and clarity to increase.

Many mistakes in relationships and workplaces happen not because people lack intelligence, but because emotions act faster than judgement.

Emotional Balance Is Not Emotional Flatness

A balanced person is not someone who remains at the same emotional level every moment.

That is neither possible nor necessary.

Life should make us happy.

Achievements should excite us.

Love should move us.

Loss should make us sad.

Injustice may make us angry.

Uncertainty may create concern.

These emotions are part of being human.

Balance means that emotions are experienced without allowing any single emotion to permanently occupy the driver's seat.

Happiness should not make us careless.

Success should not make us arrogant.

Anger should not make us destructive.

Fear should not paralyse us.

Sadness should not make us lose perspective.

Excitement should not make us impulsive.

The objective is not suppression but regulation.

Maintaining an Inner Reference Point

If our emotional state depends entirely on external circumstances, peace becomes extremely fragile.

Someone praises us—we are happy.

Someone criticises us—we are disturbed.

Something succeeds—we feel confident.

Something fails—we begin doubting ourselves.

Someone agrees with us—we feel comfortable.

Someone disagrees—we become defensive.

Our emotional state then becomes like a boat without an anchor, constantly pushed by every passing wave.

A stable life requires an internal reference point.

It may come from values, purpose, self-awareness, discipline, faith, perspective, or simply a clear understanding of what truly matters.

When our inner reference point is strong, external events still affect us, but they do not completely define us.

Do Not Believe Every Thought

One of the most useful lessons in emotional balance is surprisingly simple:

Not every thought produced by the mind deserves to be believed.

The mind continuously generates possibilities.

Some are useful.

Some are warnings.

Some are memories.

Some are fears.

Some are pure imagination.

A thought such as “Something may go wrong” is not the same as something actually going wrong.

A forecast is not reality.

An assumption is not evidence.

An imagined conversation is not an actual conversation.

Learning to observe thoughts without immediately accepting them as truth reduces enormous emotional turbulence.

Relationships Need Emotional Space

Every relationship also requires the right amount of emotional space.

Too much distance weakens relationships.

Too much emotional dependence can also create instability.

Healthy closeness allows affection without control, concern without constant interference, communication without domination, and care without losing individuality.

Even among family members, emotional needs differ.

One person may need discussion.

Another may need silence.

One may express affection openly.

Another may communicate through actions.

One may recover quickly from disagreement.

Another may need time.

Understanding these differences prevents us from expecting everyone to think, feel, and react exactly as we do.

Calmness Is a Form of Strength

In difficult situations, the calmest person often becomes the strongest person in the room.

Not because that person feels nothing, but because emotion has not overtaken judgement.

Calmness improves listening.

It improves decision-making.

It prevents unnecessary escalation.

It gives others confidence.

And perhaps most importantly, it allows us to see situations as they are rather than as our emotions temporarily portray them.

This quality becomes especially important when responsibilities increase. A parent, leader, manager, teacher, or decision-maker may be surrounded by many emotional signals simultaneously.

Everyone may expect something.

Everyone may have concerns.

Every issue may appear urgent.

The ability to remain composed becomes a form of leadership.

Creating Emotional Equilibrium

Emotional balance is built gradually through small habits.

Listen before reacting.

Separate facts from assumptions.

Allow emotions to settle before making important decisions.

Recognise what is within your control and what is not.

Avoid repeatedly forecasting negative outcomes without evidence.

Do not carry every workplace emotion home.

Do not carry every family disagreement into the workplace.

Give yourself periods of silence, movement, exercise, reflection, prayer, reading, or simple solitude.

Most importantly, regularly ask:

“Am I responding to reality, or am I responding to something my mind has imagined?”

That single question can prevent many unnecessary emotional storms.

The Art of Remaining Centred

Life will never become emotionally silent.

People around us will continue to communicate.

Our family will have expectations.

Our children will experience changing emotions.

Our colleagues will bring different personalities and pressures.

Circumstances will fluctuate.

Success and disappointment will alternate.

And our own mind will continuously generate thoughts about yesterday and tomorrow.

We cannot control all these signals.

But we can gradually improve the way we receive, interpret, and respond to them.

Perhaps emotional maturity is ultimately this:

To care deeply without becoming overwhelmed.

To listen without absorbing every emotion.

To think about the future without living in fear of imaginary futures.

To experience happiness without losing grounding.

To face difficulties without losing composure.

And to remain connected to the world while still maintaining a quiet centre within ourselves.

That quiet centre is not the absence of emotion.

It is the balance that allows us to experience every emotion without being carried away by it.
