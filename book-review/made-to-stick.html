Made to Stick

Chip Heath and Dan Heath · first published 2007 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Ideas that people understand, remember and act on share six learnable traits, so making a message stick is a skill rather than a gift.

Summary

Chip and Dan Heath set out to explain why some ideas survive and spread, such as urban legends and proverbs, while carefully prepared messages from managers and teachers are quickly forgotten. Their answer is that sticky ideas share six qualities, gathered into the acronym SUCCESs.

The main obstacle is what they call the curse of knowledge. Once we know something well, we can no longer imagine not knowing it, so we speak in abstractions that make sense only to us. The six principles are tools for escaping that trap.

Simple means finding the core of an idea and stating it compactly, the way military orders spell out the intent behind a mission so people can act sensibly when plans break down. Simplicity is about priority, not dumbing down.

Unexpected means breaking a pattern to win attention, then opening a gap in what people know so curiosity keeps them listening until the gap is filled.

Concrete means using specific, sensory language and real examples. Abstract statements slide away, while images and tangible cases give memory many hooks to grip.

Credible means helping people believe the idea. Besides experts, the authors suggest vivid details, statistics put in human scale, and claims the audience can test for themselves.

Emotional means making people care. A single person in need moves us far more than a large number, and messages gain force when they connect to self-interest or to the identity people hold.

Stories make people act, because they work as mental rehearsal and inspiration at once. The authors highlight plots about overcoming challenges, building connections and creative breakthroughs, and urge readers to spot good stories already around them.

The book closes by stressing that none of this needs special talent. Anyone who checks a message against the six principles, and fights the curse of knowledge, can make ideas clearer, more memorable and more likely to change what people do.

315 words

One-line takeaway: Before you share an important idea, strip it to its core and make it simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional and told as a story.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What is the 'curse of knowledge' described in the book?

    Answer B. Experts struggle to see things from a beginner's view, so they communicate in ways others cannot follow.

  2. What does the acronym SUCCESs stand for?

    Answer A. The six principles are Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional and Stories.

  3. In the book, what does making an idea 'simple' mainly involve?

    Answer C. Simplicity means identifying the single most important point and expressing it in a compact form.

  4. Why do the authors point to the military practice of stating the intent behind orders?

    Answer D. A clear statement of intent tells people what matters most so they can adapt when circumstances change.

  5. How does the 'Unexpected' principle hold attention over time?

    Answer B. Surprise grabs attention, and a knowledge gap keeps people engaged until it is filled.

  6. Why are concrete ideas easier to remember than abstract ones?

    Answer C. Specific images and tangible examples connect to more things in memory, so they are retained better.

  7. Which is an example of building credibility without relying on an expert?

    Answer A. The authors suggest testable claims, vivid details and human-scale statistics as sources of credibility.

  8. According to the book, what tends to move people more to help?

    Answer D. People respond more strongly to one individual than to big numbers, which can dull emotion.

  9. Why do the authors say stories help ideas stick and drive action?

    Answer B. Stories let listeners simulate how to act and motivate them to do so.

  10. What is the book's view on whether making ideas stick requires special talent?

    Answer C. The authors argue that checking messages against the six principles lets anyone improve them.
