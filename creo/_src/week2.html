    Why datums

    Every Creo part starts with three default planes (RIGHT, TOP, FRONT) and a coordinate system. Good models hang their important features from these and from extra datums, not from faces of other features. A face can disappear when a feature changes; a datum plane defined from RIGHT at 60 mm does not.

    DatumToolTypical constraints
    PlaneModel ▸ PlaneOffset from a plane, Through an axis + Angle to a plane, Through two edges, Normal to an edge, Tangent to a cylinder
    AxisModel ▸ AxisThrough a cylinder, two planes' intersection, two points, Normal to a plane through a point
    PointModel ▸ PointOn a surface with offsets, on an edge at a ratio, at curve/surface intersections, Offset coordinate system
    Coordinate systemModel ▸ Coordinate SystemThree planes, two edges, or offset from another csys
    On-the-fly datums. While inside a feature, open the Datum menu at the right end of the dashboard (Model tab ▸ Datum while the feature is active) to make a plane or axis just for that feature. It is stored inside the feature and stays hidden — tidy, but harder to reuse.
    Coordinate systems matter more in Creo than in many other systems: mass properties, Mechanism, CNC, export to analysis and the "Default" assembly constraint all use them.

    Do it in Creo

    1. New part datum_drill. Extrude a 100 × 60 × 20 block on TOP.
    2. Plane → pick RIGHT → Offset 70 → name DTM_END (Properties tab). The new plane is outside the block — datums can be anywhere.
    3. Axis → pick FRONT and RIGHT with Ctrl → Through both → the intersection axis A_MAIN.
    4. Plane → pick A_MAIN (Through) + Ctrl-pick TOP (Angle 30°) → DTM_30. Sketch a circle on DTM_30 and extrude it to the next surface: an angled boss.
    5. Point → pick the top face → set two offsets 25 and 15 from RIGHT and FRONT → PNT_DRILL.
    6. Coordinate System → pick the three faces at one corner → orient X along the long edge. Name it CS_MACHINING.
    7. Turn datum display on and off with the Datum Display Filters; put the datums on a layer (View ▸ Layers ▸ New Layer ▸ add items) and hide the layer.

    Lab: datum planes in 3D

    Choose how a datum plane is defined and watch it move as the part changes.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Name datums (DTM_BEARING_A) — in big models you will select them in the tree, not on screen.
    • A datum plane's size on screen means nothing; drag its handles or set Display size if it is in the way.
    • For shafts, put the shaft axis on the intersection of two default planes; then every turned feature references the same axis.
    • Avoid datums based on rounds or chamfers — they change shape most often.
    • The yellow/brown side of a datum plane is its positive side (the flip arrow for offsets). Check it before typing an offset.

    Practice

    1. Model a pipe bracket with a Ø30 bore whose axis is 45° to the base and 50 above it. Make the axis first as a datum, then the boss on it.
    2. Add a coordinate system at the bore centre with Z along the bore axis.
    3. Change the angle to 60° with one edit. Nothing else should need fixing.

    Sweeps in Creo

    SweepModel ▸ Sweep. Pick or sketch the origin trajectory first, then click Create or edit sweep section. Creo puts you in a sketch at the start of the trajectory, looking down it.
    Constant / Variable sectionConstant keeps the section shape. Variable lets the section follow extra trajectories or relations along the path.
    trajparA parameter that goes from 0 at the start to 1 at the end of the trajectory. Use it in section relations: sd5 = 20 + 10*trajpar tapers the section.
    Helical SweepModel ▸ Sweep ▸ Helical Sweep. Sketch the helix profile (the outline the coil follows, with an axis), set the pitch, then sketch the section (the wire). Left- or right-handed.
    Self-intersection is the classic sweep failure: a section bigger than the trajectory's tightest bend radius, or a helical sweep whose wire is thicker than the pitch, folds over itself. Rule: section half-size < smallest path radius; wire Ø < pitch.

    Spring rate of a compression spring: k = G·d⁴ / (8·D³·n) with G ≈ 79 300 MPa for spring steel, d wire, D mean coil Ø, n active coils.

    Do it in Creo

    1. New part handle. Sketch on FRONT a path: line 60 up, R20 arc, line 120 across, R20 arc, line 60 down (a U-handle). OK.
    2. Model ▸ Sweep: the sketch is the trajectory. Click Create or edit sweep section. Sketch a Ø16 circle at the crosshair. Finish.
    3. Edit Definition: switch to Variable section, edit the section and add a relation Tools ▸ Relations inside the section: sd# = 16 + 6*sin(trajpar*180) (use the diameter's sd number). Finish: the handle swells in the middle.
    4. New part spring. Sweep ▸ Helical Sweep ▸ References ▸ Define the helix sweep profile on FRONT: a vertical geometry centreline and a line 20 to its right, 60 long. OK.
    5. Pitch 8. Click Create or edit sweep section, sketch a Ø3 circle on the profile start. Finish: Ø43 outside spring, 7.5 coils.
    6. Close the ends: Edit Definition ▸ Pitch panel ▸ add points with pitch 3 at both ends (variable pitch). Grind the ends flat with an extrude cut.
    7. Try pitch 2.5 with the 3 mm wire. Read the failure message, then undo.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • The trajectory's start point sets where the section is sketched. Right-click a vertex ▸ Start Point to move it.
    • Tangent trajectories sweep cleanly; sharp corners in the trajectory need a section small enough to turn the corner, or use Merge ends options.
    • Cosmetic threads (Day 11) are better than modelled helical threads for bolts — modelled threads are heavy and rarely needed on drawings.
    • Springs in assemblies: make the free length a parameter and drive it with a relation from the assembly (Day 12) to show compressed length.
    • Swept Blend (Day 10) is the tool when the section must change shape along the path.

    3. Model an O-ring groove path around a 100 × 60 R15 rectangle with a 2.5 × 1.8 section, as a swept cut.

    Three kinds of blend

    BlendModel ▸ Shape ▸ Blend. Parallel sections at distances from each other. Sketch each section in the Sections panel.
    Rotational BlendSections arranged at angles about an axis — like a curved duct or a horn.
    Swept BlendModel ▸ Sweep ▸ Swept Blend. Sections placed at points along a trajectory — the most flexible.
    Boundary BlendThe surface tool for blending curves in two directions (Day 25).
    The two rules. (1) Every section must have the same number of entities: a circle blending to a square needs the circle divided into four arcs. (2) The start points (the arrow on each section) must match, or the blend twists. Right-click a vertex ▸ Start Point. A point can be a blend vertex (right-click ▸ Blend Vertex) when one section needs one entity fewer.

    Transition options: Straight (ruled faces between sections) or Smooth (curved through all sections). Volume of a straight blend between two areas A₁, A₂ at distance h (similar shapes) ≈ h/3·(A₁ + A₂ + √(A₁A₂)).

    Do it in Creo

    1. New part transition. Model ▸ Shape ▸ Blend ▸ Sections ▸ Define: sketch on TOP a 100 × 100 square centred on RIGHT/FRONT. OK.
    2. Section 2: offset 80 from section 1 ▸ Sketch: circle Ø70 centred.
    3. The circle has one entity, the square four: use Divide to split the circle at 45°, 135°, 225°, 315° (draw two centrelines at 45° to help).
    4. Check the start arrows: both should be at matching corners. Move one with right-click ▸ Start Point. Finish.
    5. Edit Definition ▸ Options ▸ Smooth and add a third section (Ø50 at 140) to see a curved transition.
    6. Shell the transition 3 mm, removing both end faces. You have a square-to-round duct.
    7. Swept Blend: sketch a 90° arc path R100. Sweep ▸ Swept Blend, section 1 at the start a 60 × 40 rectangle, section 2 at the end a Ø50 circle divided into four. Finish.

    Lab: blend in 3D

    Blend between two or three sections, twist them and compare the volume calculated slice by slice.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Twisted, crossed faces nearly always mean mismatched start points.
    • Sketch sections with the same orientation references so Creo picks matching start points by default.
    • For a hopper with a lip or flange, blend the main body and add the flanges as separate extrudes.
    • When a blend fails, simplify: two sections first, straight transition, then add complexity.
    • Check the result with Analysis ▸ Inspect Geometry ▸ Curvature if appearance matters (Day 25).

    Practice

    1. Model a dust hood: 300 × 200 rectangle blending to Ø100 over 150 height, shell 2, flange 20 at the bottom.
    2. Model a ventilation elbow with Swept Blend: 150 × 100 rectangle to 150 × 50 along a 90° R150 path.
    3. Compute the volume of the dust hood with Mass Properties and compare with the h/3 formula (before shelling).

    Parameters and relations

    Dimension symbolsEvery dimension has a symbol (d0, d1…). Tools ▸ Switch Dimensions shows them. Rename a dimension in its Properties to OD so relations read like English.
    ParametersTools ▸ Parameters. Your own named values: MATERIAL_GRADE (string), N_BOLTS (integer), HAS_KEYWAY (yes/no), MASS_LIMIT (real). They also fill drawing title blocks and BOMs.
    RelationsTools ▸ d= Relations. Equations that run in order every regeneration: PCD = OD - 40. The left side is driven — you cannot type it on screen any more.
    Functionssqrt, abs, floor, ceil, min, max, sin/cos/tan in degrees, pi, IF … ELSE … ENDIF, comparisons == <> < > & |. Comments start with /*.
    Order. Relations run top to bottom; a parameter must be given a value before a line uses it. Part relations run during regeneration; you can also choose to run some after regeneration (for mass, using the Post Regeneration list) or put relations in a feature or a sketch (sd symbols).

    Pattern counts are dimensions too: in relations they look like P12. Example: P12 = floor(LENGTH / 40) + 1.

    Do it in Creo

    1. Model a flange: revolve OD 160 × 12 thick, bore Ø80, one Ø11 hole at Diameter placement PCD 120, axis-patterned 6.
    2. Tools ▸ Switch Dimensions. Select each dimension ▸ Properties ▸ Name: OD, THK, BORE, PCD, BOLT_D. The pattern count shows as P#.
    3. Tools ▸ Parameters ▸ add N (Integer) = 6.
    4. Tools ▸ d= Relations, type:
      /* flange rules
      PCD = OD - 40
      BORE = OD / 2
      P15 = N (use your pattern's symbol)
      IF N > 8
        BOLT_D = 9
      ENDIF
    5. Click Verify (the check button) then OK. Regenerate.
    6. Change OD to 200 and N to 10 from the Parameters dialog or by editing OD on screen. Regenerate: PCD, bore, bolt size and count follow.
    7. Try to edit PCD on screen — Creo says it is driven by a relation.
    8. Add a string parameter DESCRIPTION = "Flange OD" + a relation DESCRIPTION = "FLANGE OD" + itos(OD) (itos = integer to string).

    Edit the relations below — Creo-style syntax, IF/ENDIF, functions in degrees. The flange redraws as you type and warns when holes break out or overlap.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Name dimensions before writing relations. d17 = d4 - 40 is unreadable in six months.
    • Do not drive the same dimension from two places (a relation and a family table) — the last one wins and nobody knows which.
    • Trig functions use degrees: sin(30) = 0.5.
    • Use /* comments to say why, not what.
    • To switch whole features on and off by rule, use a family-table feature column (Day 13) or Pro/PROGRAM (Tools ▸ Program), where an IF … END IF around a feature's ADD … END ADD block includes or skips it.

    Generic and instances

    A family table is a spreadsheet inside a part (or assembly). The generic is the model you built; each row is an instance — a separate part as far as assemblies and drawings are concerned, but all stored in one file.

    Dimension columnsValues for named dimensions (d1, LENGTH…). An asterisk * means "same as the generic".
    Feature columnsY = present, N = suppressed in that instance (a chamfer, a keyway, a cross hole).
    Parameter columnsDESCRIPTION, PART_NO, MATERIAL per instance — for BOMs and title blocks.
    Component columnsIn assembly family tables: which part or instance to use, or Y/N to include it.
    Verify. Tools ▸ Family Table ▸ Verify Instances regenerates every instance and reports which fail. Always verify after editing the generic — an instance can fail even when the generic is fine.

    Relations or family table? Relations: a rule applied to whatever values you type. Family table: a fixed list of approved sizes, each with its own part number. Many companies use both: the table picks the size, relations derive the rest.

    Do it in Creo

    1. Model a hex bolt generic M10 × 30: hex head 16 A/F × 6.4, shank Ø10 × 30, 1 × 45° end chamfer, cosmetic thread 26 long. Name the dimensions D, L, S, K.
    2. Tools ▸ Family Table → click Add/delete table columns → Dimension → pick D, L, S, K on screen; Feature → pick the chamfer; Parameter → DESCRIPTION. OK.
    3. Click Insert instance four times. Name them M6X20, M8X25, M12X40, M16X50 and fill in the values. Put N in the chamfer column for M12X40 to see suppression.
    4. Click Verify. All should say Success.
    5. Select M8X25 ▸ Open. It opens as its own window, title M8X25<BOLT>.
    6. Use Patternize (Edit ▸ Patternize in the table) to create M10X35 … M10X60 in steps of 5 automatically.
    7. Save. One file bolt.prt holds every instance; assemblies can call any of them.

    Lab: family table

    Pick a row to see that instance. Edit cells, and set the chamfer column to Y or N.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Instance names become part names — follow your numbering system (e.g. ISO4017-M8X25).
    • Do not delete a column or reorder the generic's features casually: every instance changes.
    • Large tables are easier in Excel: the table has an Edit with Excel button.
    • Replace one instance with another in an assembly: right-click the component ▸ Replace ▸ Family Table.
    • Instances are read-only geometry; to change their shape, change the generic or the table.
    • Instance accelerator files (.xpr) speed up opening instances; regenerate them after table changes.

    Units and materials

    File ▸ Prepare ▸ Model Properties shows units and material in one place.

    UnitsChange unit system: Convert dimensions (a 25.4 mm part becomes 1 in — same size) or Interpret dimensions (25.4 mm becomes 25.4 in — 25 times bigger!). Nearly always Convert.
    MaterialPick from the material library (.mtl files) or your company folder (pro_material_dir). The material carries density, E, ν, yield, thermal data and appearance.
    DensityCheck it: steel 7.85 × 10⁻⁹ tonne/mm³ = 7850 kg/m³. A wrong unit in a .mtl file makes everything 1000× wrong.
    Mass PropertiesAnalysis ▸ Mass Properties. Volume, surface area, density, mass, centre of gravity and inertia about a coordinate system and at the centre of gravity.
    Saved analysis. In Mass Properties choose Feature instead of Quick: an analysis feature goes into the tree and creates parameters (mass, cog). Relations and drawing tables can then read them, and they update every regeneration.

    Do it in Creo

    1. Open the flange from Day 12. File ▸ Prepare ▸ Model Properties ▸ Material ▸ change. Pick a steel. Check density in the material definition.
    2. Analysis ▸ Mass Properties → coordinate system PRT_CSYS_DEF → Preview. Read mass, volume and COG.
    3. Compare the mass with your hand calculation: ρ × (π/4 (OD² − bore²) − holes) × THK.
    4. Switch the analysis to Feature, name it MASS_FLANGE, OK. Look at the tree.
    5. Add a relation (Post Regeneration): WEIGHT_KG = MASS:FID_MASS_FLANGE (Creo offers the parameter from the Insert Parameter list). Regenerate and read WEIGHT_KG in Parameters.
    6. Change units: Model Properties ▸ Units ▸ Change to inch-lbm-second ▸ Convert. Check the OD reads 6.299 in. Change back.
    7. Assign a different material to see mass change; change only the appearance colour and see mass does not.

    Lab: mass calculator

    Pick a shape, dimensions and material; compare the lab's mass and inertia with Creo's Mass Properties.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Keep a company material folder with checked densities; point pro_material_dir to it (Day 26).
    • Bought-out parts (motors, bearings) often come as simplified shapes: override their mass with a parameter rather than trusting the geometry.
    • Assembly mass properties use each part's material; assign materials before trusting assembly mass.
    • Inertia "at the centre of gravity" is what rotating-machine calculations need (e.g. coupling and shaft J); "about the csys" is for when you rotate about that axis.
    • Units are per model. A mm part in an inch assembly is fine — Creo converts — but drawings show each model's own units unless told otherwise.

    Practice

    1. Assign steel to the shaft from Day 5 and aluminium 6061 to the pulley. Record both masses and the polar inertia of the pulley about its axis.
    2. Check the shaft mass by hand (sum of cylinders). Difference should be under 1 %.
    3. Add a WEIGHT_KG parameter to the bolt family generic from Day 13 and verify it is correct for two instances.
