Corona Treatment and Surface Energy: Dyne Levels, Treatment Dose and Adhesion

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
CoronaSurface energyAdhesionFilms

Polyethylene and polypropylene have low surface energy, so inks and adhesives bead up instead of wetting and bonding. Corona treatment uses a high-voltage discharge in air to oxidise the surface, adding polar groups that raise surface energy. The level is checked with dyne liquids or contact angle, and it decays with time and with additives migrating to the surface.

Key takeaways

  • A liquid wets a surface when the surface energy exceeds the liquid's surface tension by about 8–10 mN/m.
  • Untreated PE and PP are around 29–31 mN/m; printing usually needs ≥ 38 and lamination/coating ≥ 40–42 mN/m (dyn/cm).
  • Treatment dose (W·min/m²) = power ÷ (line speed × treated width).
  • Treatment decays over days, faster with slip agents and warm storage; treat in line or re-treat before printing.
  • Over-treatment can cause blocking, odour and weak boundary layers; more is not always better.

2. Treatment dose and power

Dose (W·min/m²) = P (W) / [ v (m/min) · w (m) ] P (kW) = dose · v · w / 1000

Typical doses: 10–20 W·min/m² for BOPP, 20–50 for PE, depending on additives and target level. Example: width 1.3 m, speed 150 m/min, dose 30 W·min/m² → 5.85 kW generator output.

3. Testing and decay

  • Dyne pens / test inks (ASTM D2578, ISO 8296): the highest liquid that stays as a continuous film for about 2 s gives the level.
  • Contact angle: lower water contact angle → higher surface energy; repeatable and quantitative.
  • Decay: measure at production and before converting; slip agents (erucamide) and antistats bloom to the surface and lower the level.
  • Back-side treatment: unwanted treatment on the reverse causes blocking and set-off; check both sides.
