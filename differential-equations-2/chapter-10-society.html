Economic and social models are rougher than physical laws — people are not particles — but the same mathematics applies: balances, feedbacks, thresholds and cycles. Two new ideas appear: stochastic equations, where a random term dW is added and we track distributions rather than single paths; and equations used to price things rather than predict them.

91Black–Scholes equation

PDElinearparabolicfinanceBlack, Scholes and Merton 1973 (Nobel 1997)
∂V/∂t + ½·σ²·S²·∂²V/∂S² + r·S·∂V/∂S − r·V = 0
European call: C = S·N(d₁) − K·e−rT·N(d₂), d₁ = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)T]/(σ√T), d₂ = d₁ − σ√T

V option value · S share price · K strike · T time to expiry · σ volatility · r risk-free rate · N normal distribution

Hold an option and continuously trade the share to cancel its risk; the hedged portfolio must earn the risk-free rate. That argument removes the investor's expected return from the problem and leaves a PDE — which, by a change of variables, is the heat equation (Part I, Day 12). Volatility plays the role of diffusivity: more uncertainty, more option value.

Share ₹100, strike ₹100, 1 year, r = 5 %, σ = 20 %. Value of a call option?d₁ = (0 + 0.07)/0.2 = 0.35, d₂ = 0.15, N(d₁) = 0.637, N(d₂) = 0.560 C = 100 × 0.637 − 100 × e^(−0.05) × 0.560 = 63.7 − 53.2 = ₹10.45 Raise σ to 40 %: C = ₹18.0 — the option is worth more when the future is less certain.
Where you meet itOptions and derivatives pricing, corporate finance (real options in project appraisal), insurance, risk management (the "Greeks" are its derivatives).

92Geometric Brownian motion

SDElinearstochasticSamuelson 1965; Osborne 1959
dS = μ·S·dt + σ·S·dW E[S(t)] = S₀·eμt, median = S₀·e(μ − σ²/2)t

μ drift (expected return) · σ volatility · dW Brownian increment (random, variance dt)

The standard model of share prices: the percentage change in each instant is a drift plus a random shock. Prices stay positive and are log-normally distributed. A surprising consequence: the typical (median) outcome grows more slowly than the average, by σ²/2 per year — the "volatility drag" that makes high-volatility investments disappoint most holders even when their average is good.

μ = 12 % a year, σ = 30 %, over 10 years. Mean and median growth?Mean: e^(1.2) = 3.32× Median: e^((0.12 − 0.045) × 10) = e^(0.75) = 2.12× Half of all paths end below 2.12× even though the average is 3.32× — a few very lucky paths pull the mean up.
Where you meet itShare-price models and Monte-Carlo risk simulation, the Black–Scholes derivation, commodity prices, population growth with environmental noise, Brownian motion of particles.

93Solow growth model

ODEnonlinearfirst orderSolow 1956 (Nobel 1987) · Part I, Day 3: Equilibria and bifurcation
dk/dt = s·kα − (n + δ)·k steady state k* = (s/(n + δ))1/(1−α), output y* = k*α

k capital per worker · s saving (investment) rate · α capital share (≈ ⅓) · n population growth · δ depreciation

Investment adds capital, depreciation and a growing workforce dilute it. Because returns to capital diminish (α < 1), every economy converges to a steady state where investment just replaces wear and dilution. Saving more raises the level of income but not its long-run growth — only technology does that. The "golden rule" saving rate (s = α) maximises consumption.

s = 30 %, n = 1 %, δ = 5 %, α = ⅓. Steady capital and output per worker? What if saving rises to 40 %?k* = (0.30/0.06)^(1.5) = 5^1.5 = 11.2, y* = 11.2^(1/3) = 2.24 s = 40 %: k* = (6.67)^1.5 = 17.2, y* = 2.58 — output up 15 %, but growth returns to zero once there.
Where you meet itDevelopment economics and growth accounting, policy debates on saving and investment, capital-intensive industries, any process with diminishing returns and steady depreciation (machine fleets, infrastructure).

94Vasicek interest-rate model (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck)

SDElinearstochasticVasicek 1977; Ornstein and Uhlenbeck 1930
dr = a·(b − r)·dt + σ·dW E[r(t)] = b + (r₀ − b)·e−at, long-run s.d. = σ/√(2a)

r short interest rate · b long-run mean · a speed of mean reversion · σ volatility

A first-order lag (Part I, Day 2) driven by noise. Rates wander but are pulled back towards a long-run level; the pull strength a sets how fast. The same Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process models the velocity of a Brownian particle, sensor drift, temperature anomalies and mean-reverting commodity spreads.

a = 0.5 per year, b = 6 %, r₀ = 10 %, σ = 1 %. Expected rate after 2 years? Long-run spread?E[r(2)] = 6 + 4·e^(−1) = 7.47 % Long-run standard deviation = 1/√(2 × 0.5) = 1 % → 95 % of the time between about 4 % and 8 %.
Where you meet itInterest-rate and bond pricing, currency and commodity spreads, sensor noise and drift models, the velocity of particles in a fluid, neuron membrane noise.

95Bass diffusion of innovations

ODEnonlinearfirst orderFrank Bass 1969
dF/dt = (p + q·F)·(1 − F) peak adoption at t* = ln(q/p)/(p + q)

F fraction of the market that has adopted · p innovation (adoption from advertising) · q imitation (word of mouth)

Some people adopt a new product spontaneously (p), most because others have (q·F). When q ≫ p the curve is a logistic S with a sharp sales peak; when p is large it is closer to exponential saturation. Fitted to the first few years of sales it forecasts the peak — used for TVs, mobile phones, solar panels and electric vehicles.

Typical consumer-durable values p = 0.03, q = 0.38 per year. When do sales peak? What share has adopted by then?t* = ln(0.38/0.03)/0.41 = 2.54/0.41 = 6.2 years F(t*) = (q − p)/(2q) = 0.35/0.76 = 46 % — sales peak just before half the market has bought
Where you meet itProduct launch forecasting, technology adoption (EVs, UPI payments, solar), spread of farming practices, social-media growth, capacity planning for new plants.

96Lanchester's combat laws

ODElinearsystemLanchester 1916; Osipov 1915
dx/dt = −a·y dy/dt = −b·x conserved: b·x² − a·y² = constant (the square law)

x, y force strengths · a, b effectiveness of y against x and of x against y

In aimed-fire combat each side's losses are proportional to the size of the other side. The conserved quantity shows that fighting strength goes as effectiveness × numbers²: doubling numbers is worth quadrupling quality. It explains concentration of force — Nelson's plan at Trafalgar — and is used, with care, in business strategy for market battles.

Equal effectiveness, 40 units against 30. Who wins, and how many survive?Square law: x_final = √(40² − 30²) = √700 = 26.5 units survive The larger force loses only about a third of its strength while destroying the smaller one entirely. To draw, the smaller side would need (40/30)² = 1.78 times the effectiveness.
Where you meet itMilitary operations research, war-gaming, competitive strategy (market share battles), and as a lesson in why concentration beats dispersal in any contest with simultaneous engagement.

97Goodwin growth cycle

ODEnonlinearsystemRichard Goodwin 1967 · Part I, Day 10: Nonlinear dynamics
dv/dt = v·[ (1 − u)/σ − (α + β) ] du/dt = u·[ −γ + ρ·v − α ]
equilibrium v* = (α + γ)/ρ, u* = 1 − σ(α + β)

v employment rate · u wage share of output · σ capital–output ratio · α productivity growth · β labour-force growth · γ, ρ wage response (Phillips curve)

Predator–prey in economics. High profits (low wage share) mean high investment and rising employment; high employment strengthens workers and raises wages; rising wages squeeze profits and investment; employment falls; wages weaken — and the cycle repeats. The wage share plays predator, employment prey. A classic of business-cycle theory.

σ = 3, α = 2 %, β = 1 %, γ = 0.6, ρ = 0.7. Equilibrium employment and wage share?v* = (0.02 + 0.6)/0.7 = 0.886 → 88.6 % employment u* = 1 − 3 × 0.03 = 0.91 → wages take 91 % of output at the centre of the cycle Cycles about these values have periods of several years (lab).
Where you meet itBusiness-cycle and macroeconomic theory, labour economics, illustrating feedback loops in organisations and markets.

98Lorenz equations

ODEnonlinearsystemchaosEdward Lorenz 1963 · Part I, Day 11: Chaos
x′ = σ(y − x) y′ = x(ρ − z) − y z′ = xy − βz σ = 10, β = 8/3, ρ = 28

x convection speed · y, z temperature differences · ρ heating (Rayleigh ratio)

The equation that launched chaos theory, met in Part I, Day 11. It belongs in any list of the hundred most useful because it changed how we think about prediction: weather forecasting, ensemble forecasts and the "butterfly effect" all trace back to it. It also models lasers, waterwheels and dynamos.

At ρ = 28, where are the two non-trivial equilibria? Are they stable?C± = (±√(β(ρ − 1)), ±√(β(ρ − 1)), ρ − 1) = (±8.49, ±8.49, 27) They lose stability at ρ = σ(σ + β + 3)/(σ − β − 1) = 24.74 < 28 → unstable: the flow orbits around them without settling, hopping between the two lobes.
Where you meet itWeather and climate predictability, ensemble forecasting, lasers (Haken), chaotic waterwheels and dynamos, chaos-based encryption, teaching sensitivity to initial conditions.

99Rössler system

ODEnonlinearsystemchaosOtto Rössler 1976
x′ = −y − z y′ = x + a·y z′ = b + z·(x − c) classic a = b = 0.2, c = 5.7

One nonlinear term (zx) only · c controls the route to chaos

Rössler designed the simplest possible chaotic flow: a spiral in the x–y plane that occasionally lifts off and folds back (the z term) — a direct picture of "stretch and fold". As c increases the orbit doubles its period again and again (1, 2, 4 … loops) before becoming chaotic, the same Feigenbaum cascade seen in dripping taps and in the Duffing oscillator of Part I.

Follow the period-doubling route with a = b = 0.2.c = 2.5: a single loop (period 1) c = 3.5: two distinct loops (period 2) c = 4.0: four loops (period 4) c = 5.7: chaotic band — a strange attractor with a one-dimensional return map.
Where you meet itA standard test case for chaos analysis (Lyapunov exponents, return maps), models of chemical oscillators, and teaching the period-doubling route.

100Kuramoto model (synchronisation)

ODEnonlinearsystemYoshiki Kuramoto 1975
dθi/dt = ωi + (K/N)·Σj sin(θj − θi) order parameter r·eiψ = (1/N)·Σ eiθj

θi phase of oscillator i · ωi natural frequency · K coupling · r coherence (0 to 1)

Many oscillators, each with its own natural pace, each nudged towards the others. Below a critical coupling they run independently; above it a synchronised cluster appears suddenly and grows — a phase transition in time. For a Lorentzian spread of half-width γ the threshold is Kc = 2γ and r = √(1 − Kc/K) above it. A fitting end: equation 58's single locking oscillator, multiplied a thousand times.

Natural frequencies spread with half-width γ = 0.5 rad/s (Lorentzian). Critical coupling? Coherence at K = 2?K_c = 2γ = 1.0 At K = 2: r = √(1 − 1/2) = 0.71 — about 70 % coherent The London Millennium Bridge (2000): pedestrians' steps locked to the bridge's sway once the crowd exceeded a critical number — Kuramoto in action.
Where you meet itPower-grid stability (generators as coupled oscillators), fireflies and crickets, heart pacemaker cells, circadian clocks, laser arrays, neuronal rhythms, synchronised metronomes, applause.
