    Transient plus steady state

    Add a harmonic force to the oscillator:

    m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = F₀·cos ωt

    Because the equation is linear (Day 2), the solution is the free response (which carries the initial conditions and dies away at the rate ζωn) plus one particular solution that follows the force for ever. After a few time constants 1/(ζωn) only that steady state remains, and it is a cosine at the forcing frequency ω — not at ωn.

    Amplitude and phase: the complex trick

    Replace cos ωt by ejωt (its real part) and guess x = X·ejωt. Every derivative becomes a multiplication by jω:

    (k − m·ω² + j·c·ω)·X = F₀ → X = F₀ / (k − mω² + jcω)
    With r = ω/ωn: |X|·k/F₀ = 1 / √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²) φ = arctan( 2ζr / (1 − r²) )
    RegionAmplitudePhase lagWhat controls it
    r ≪ 1 (slow)≈ F₀/k — the static deflection≈ 0°Stiffness
    r = 1 (resonance)F₀/(k·2ζ) — the static deflection × Q, with Q = 1/(2ζ)exactly 90°Damping
    r ≫ 1 (fast)≈ F₀/(m·ω²) — falls as 1/ω²≈ 180°Mass

    The peak is at r = √(1 − 2ζ²), just below ωn. The half-power bandwidth — the width of the peak where the amplitude is 1/√2 of maximum — is Δω ≈ 2ζωn, a second way to measure damping from a frequency sweep.

    Undamped resonance and beats

    With c = 0 and ω = ωn exactly, the guess X·ejωt fails (it is already a free solution). The particular solution becomes x = (F₀/(2mωn))·t·sin ωn·t: the amplitude grows linearly without limit. Near but not at resonance, starting from rest gives x = (2F₀/(m(ωn² − ω²)))·sin((ωn − ω)t/2)·sin((ωn + ω)t/2): a fast oscillation inside a slow envelope — beats, with beat period 2π/|ωn − ω|. You hear them when two guitar strings are almost in tune, and you see them on a rig when a shaft speed sits near a structural mode.

    Transmissibility and isolation

    The force passed to the foundation goes through both the spring and the damper. Its ratio to the applied force is the transmissibility:

    TR = √(1 + (2ζr)²) / √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²)

    All curves cross TR = 1 at r = √2. Below √2 the mount amplifies; above it the mount isolates. And above √2 more damping makes isolation worse — designers use just enough damping to get through resonance at start-up and shut-down.

    Transfer functions, poles and zeros

    With zero initial conditions, output over input in the s-domain is a ratio of polynomials — the transfer function:

    G(s) = Y(s)/U(s) e.g. first order: K/(τs + 1) second order: ωn² / (s² + 2ζωn·s + ωn²)

    Roots of the denominator are poles; roots of the numerator are zeros. Each pole p contributes a term ept to the response. So the s-plane is a map of behaviour:

    • Left half-plane (Re p < 0): decays → stable. Further left = faster.
    • Right half-plane: grows → unstable.
    • Imaginary part = oscillation frequency. On the jω axis: oscillates for ever.
    • For a pole pair at σ ± jω: ωn = distance from the origin, ζ = cos of the angle from the negative real axis.
    Overshoot to a step: Mp = e−πζ/√(1−ζ²) peak time tp = π/ωd 2 % settling ts ≈ 4/(ζωn) = 4/|σ|

    Put s = jω into G and you get the frequency response of Day 6. Time response, frequency response and pole positions are three views of the same object.

    Convolution, the impulse response and the final value theorem

    The inverse transform of G(s) itself is the impulse response g(t). Because a product in s is a convolution in time, the response to any input is y(t) = ∫₀ᵗ g(τ)·u(t − τ) dτ — the output is the input "smeared" by the system's memory. The final value theorem limt→∞ y(t) = lims→0 s·Y(s) holds only if y actually settles, i.e. all poles of s·Y are in the left half-plane; apply it to an unstable system and it gives a confident, wrong answer.

    Feedback moves poles

    Take a slow plant G = K/(τs + 1) with K = 2, τ = 5 s, and close a proportional loop with gain Kp = 4:

    Closed loop Y/R = KpK / (τs + 1 + KpK)Open-loop pole: s = −1/τ = −0.2 → time constant 5 s Closed-loop pole: s = −(1 + 8)/5 = −1.8 → time constant 0.56 s (9× faster) Steady-state error to a step = 1/(1 + K_p·K) = 1/9 = 11 % (an integral term removes it)

    Control design is, at heart, the art of putting poles where you want them. Push the gain too far on a higher-order plant and a pole pair crosses into the right half-plane: the loop oscillates.

    • The slowest (right-most) stable poles dominate the response: they are the last to decay.
    • A system with any pole in the right half-plane is unstable even if that pole's term starts tiny — it will win eventually.

    Many equations, one matrix

    Two masses connected by three springs (wall – k₁ – m₁ – k₂ – m₂ – k₃ – wall). Newton for each mass:

    m₁·x₁″ = −k₁x₁ + k₂(x₂ − x₁)
    m₂·x₂″ = −k₂(x₂ − x₁) − k₃x₂
    Matrix form: M·x″ + K·x = 0, M = [[m₁, 0], [0, m₂]], K = [[k₁ + k₂, −k₂], [−k₂, k₂ + k₃]]

    The off-diagonal −k₂ is the coupling: it is why pushing one mass moves the other. First-order systems look the same: two tanks in series, a two-compartment drug model or a predator–prey pair near equilibrium all become x′ = A·x.

    Eigenvalues: directions that do not turn

    For a matrix A, an eigenvector v is a direction that A only stretches: A·v = λ·v. The stretch factor λ is the eigenvalue. Find them from

    det(A − λI) = 0 → for 2×2: λ² − (trace)·λ + det = 0

    Then the general solution of x′ = A·x is a sum of independent pieces, each moving along one eigenvector at its own rate:

    x(t) = c₁·eλ₁t·v₁ + c₂·eλ₂t·v₂

    The eigenvectors decouple the system: in eigen-coordinates, each equation stands alone. That single idea underlies modal analysis, principal components, Google's PageRank and quantum mechanics.

    Natural frequencies and mode shapes

    For M·x″ + K·x = 0 guess x = φ·cos ωt. Then (K − ω²M)·φ = 0, which has a non-zero φ only if

    det(K − ω²M) = 0 → n natural frequencies ω₁ < ω₂ < …, each with a mode shape φᵢ

    A mode is a pattern in which every part moves at the same frequency, in step (or exactly opposite), with fixed amplitude ratios. Start the system in a mode shape and it stays in it for ever. Any other start is a mix of modes, each ringing at its own frequency — and the mix generally does not repeat.

    Orthogonality, and why modes are independent

    Because M and K are symmetric, mode shapes are orthogonal with respect to both: φᵢᵀ·M·φⱼ = 0 and φᵢᵀ·K·φⱼ = 0 for i ≠ j. Substituting x = Σ qᵢ(t)·φᵢ and multiplying by φⱼᵀ splits the coupled system into independent single-degree-of-freedom oscillators qⱼ″ + ωⱼ²·qⱼ = (forces projected on φⱼ). With proportional damping each gets its own ζⱼ. Every finite-element vibration analysis does exactly this for millions of degrees of freedom, then keeps only the few modes in the frequency range of interest.

    The trace–determinant map

    The characteristic equation λ² − τ·λ + Δ = 0 depends only on τ = trace = a + d and Δ = det = ad − bc. So one plane with τ across and Δ upwards classifies every 2 × 2 linear system:

    Δ < 0 → saddle Δ > 0 and τ² > 4Δ → node Δ > 0 and τ² < 4Δ → spiral
    τ < 0 → stable τ > 0 → unstable τ = 0, Δ > 0 → centre
    The parabola Δ = τ²/4 separates nodes from spirals.

    The oscillator again. x″ + 2ζωn·x′ + ωn²·x = 0 in state form has A = [[0, 1], [−ωn², −2ζωn]], so τ = −2ζωn and Δ = ωn². Increasing damping moves the point left along a horizontal line: centre (ζ = 0) → spiral sink (ζ < 1) → crosses the parabola at ζ = 1 → stable node. Day 5's three cases are three regions of this map.

    Structural stability: which pictures survive small changes

    Nudge the matrix entries slightly. Saddles, nodes and spirals keep their type — they sit in open regions of the map. Centres (the τ = 0 line), degenerate nodes (on the parabola) and lines of equilibria (Δ = 0) do not: any perturbation changes them. That is why a real "undamped" system always turns out to spiral slightly in or out, and why centres in a linearisation tell you nothing certain about the nonlinear system (Day 10).

    Nullclines and equilibria

    For x′ = f(x, y), y′ = g(x, y) the x-nullcline is the curve f = 0 (flow is purely vertical there) and the y-nullcline is g = 0 (flow purely horizontal). Equilibria are where they cross. Between nullclines the arrows keep the same general direction, so sketching the two curves and one arrow per region already gives a rough phase portrait — no computer needed.

    Linearisation: the Jacobian

    Near an equilibrium (x*, y*) replace f and g by their tangent planes:

    J = [[∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y], [∂g/∂x, ∂g/∂y]] evaluated at (x*, y*) → η′ ≈ J·η

    Classify J with Day 9's map. The Hartman–Grobman theorem says the nonlinear picture near the point looks like the linear one provided no eigenvalue has zero real part (a "hyperbolic" equilibrium). Saddles, nodes and spirals carry over; centres do not — the nonlinear terms decide whether the orbits close, spiral in or spiral out.

    Pendulumθ′ = ω, ω′ = −sin θ − c·ωHanging equilibria (spirals), inverted ones (saddles). With c = 0 the energy ½ω² − cos θ is conserved: closed swings inside the separatrix, continuous spinning outside.
    Predator–prey (Lotka–Volterra)x′ = x − 0.5xy, y′ = 0.25xy − p·yA centre at (4p, 2) surrounded by closed cycles: prey peak, then predators peak a quarter-cycle later. A hidden conserved quantity keeps the orbits closed.
    Van der Polx′ = y, y′ = μ(1 − x²)y − xNegative damping at small amplitude, positive at large: every start converges to one closed orbit — a limit cycle.
    SIR epidemicS′ = −βSI, I′ = βSI − γIInfections grow only while S > γ/β. R₀ = β/γ; the epidemic turns when S falls to 1/R₀.
    Limit cycles and the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem

    In the plane, a trajectory that stays in a closed bounded region containing no equilibrium must approach a closed orbit. So if you can draw a "trapping" ring that the flow enters everywhere on its edges, and the only equilibrium inside the hole is a source, there must be a limit cycle in the ring. This is why chaos is impossible in two-dimensional autonomous systems: trajectories cannot cross, so in the plane they have nowhere to go except to equilibria or cycles. A limit cycle is usually born in a Hopf bifurcation, where a spiral sink's eigenvalues cross the imaginary axis (Van der Pol at μ = 0).

    Worked example

    Damped pendulum θ″ + 0.25·θ′ + sin θ = 0 as θ′ = ω, ω′ = −sin θ − 0.25ωEquilibria: ω = 0 and sin θ = 0 → θ = 0, ±π, ±2π … J = [[0, 1], [−cos θ, −0.25]] At θ = 0: J = [[0, 1], [−1, −0.25]] τ = −0.25, Δ = 1 → λ = −0.125 ± 0.992i → spiral sink (hanging) At θ = π: J = [[0, 1], [ 1, −0.25]] Δ = −1 < 0 → λ = 0.883, −1.133 → saddle (inverted)
    Predator–prey at p = 0.75Equilibrium: y′ = 0 → x* = 0.75/0.25 = 3; x′ = 0 → y* = 1/0.5 = 2 J(3, 2) = [[1 − 0.5·2, −0.5·3], [0.25·2, 0.25·3 − 0.75]] = [[0, −1.5], [0.5, 0]] λ = ±√(−0.75) = ±0.866i → linear centre; cycles of period ≈ 2π/0.866 = 7.3 time units
    Where you meet thisLimit cycles are self-sustained oscillations: heart beats, a clock's escapement, a violin bow, brake squeal and stick-slip on machine slides, wheel shimmy, aero-elastic flutter, oil whirl in journal bearings, and valve chatter. Nonlinear multistability: a Duffing-type rig mount that jumps between two vibration amplitudes, latch-up in electronics, and the two possible states of a bistable switch.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Always sketch nullclines first — they catch algebra slips in finding equilibria.
    • A centre in the linearisation is a warning, not an answer. Look for a conserved quantity or simulate.
    • A limit cycle's amplitude is set by the nonlinearity, not by the initial conditions — that is the key test to tell it from a linear oscillation in measured data.
    • In SIR, the peak of infections happens when S drops to 1/R₀, well before most people are infected; the total ever infected depends on R₀ alone.
