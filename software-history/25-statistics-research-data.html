Statistics and research data: from SPSS to R, JASP and Python

Test results mean little until you know how much they would vary if you ran the test again. Statistics turns scattered measurements into conclusions with stated confidence. For decades that meant hand calculation and printed tables; then came mainframe packages (SPSS, SAS), PC programs (Minitab, Stata), the free language R, and point-and-click open-source tools that bring good statistics to every researcher.

01The requirement: honest conclusions from noisy data

In 1908 William Sealy Gosset, a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, needed to judge barley and hop quality from only a few samples. Writing as "Student", he published the t-distribution for small samples. R. A. Fisher's Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925) gave researchers analysis of variance, significance tests and printed tables, and his work at Rothamsted shaped the design of experiments. For half a century, statistics meant mechanical calculators and those tables.

03The open-source revolution

At Bell Labs in 1976, John Chambers and colleagues created the S language for interactive data analysis (commercialised as S-PLUS). In 1993 Ross Ihaka and Robert Gentleman at the University of Auckland wrote a free S-like language, R; version 1.0 appeared in 2000. Its package archive (CRAN) now has more than 20,000 packages; RStudio (2011, the company renamed Posit in 2022) made it friendly. In parallel, Python gained NumPy, SciPy, pandas (2008), statsmodels and Jupyter notebooks — the tools used in Episode 19.

For people who prefer menus: PSPP (GNU, a free SPSS replacement), JASP (University of Amsterdam, 2016; includes Bayesian tests) and jamovi (2017) give a spreadsheet-like interface built on R, with results that update as you change options.

04Try it: is the difference real?

t-test. Bearing temperatures (°C) after 100 hours with the old and new grease. Welch's t-test asks whether the difference in means is larger than the scatter would explain. Edit the numbers (separated by spaces or commas).

Linear regression. Pairs of x and y on each line — here, vibration level (mm/s) against bearing load (kN). The fit gives the slope with its uncertainty and R².

A p-value is not the probability that your idea is true. In 2016 the American Statistical Association warned against treating p < 0.05 as a bright line. Report the size of the effect and its confidence interval, check the data plot, and think about whether the difference matters in engineering terms.

05Collecting the data: forms and surveys

Research data increasingly arrives through online forms. LimeSurvey (open source, 2003), SurveyMonkey (1999), Google Forms (2008), Microsoft Forms (2016), KoboToolbox and ODK (open-source field data collection on phones, widely used in development and health studies) all export to CSV for analysis. For engineering, data comes from DAQ systems and loggers (Episode 19), in CSV, TDMS or HDF5 files.

06Version explorer

Blue chips: commercial packages. Amber chips: open-source tools.

07Free but not open

  • Student and trial editions of SPSS, Minitab and JMP are time-limited; JMP Student Edition is free for students.
  • SAS OnDemand for Academics is free in the cloud for teaching and learning.
  • GraphPad Prism and Origin offer viewers and trials; OriginLab has a free "Origin Viewer".
  • Open alternatives — R, Python, JASP, jamovi, PSPP — are free for any use, including commercial work.

09Lessons for engineers

  1. Plot first. One outlier or a drifting sensor can make any test "significant".
  2. Plan the sample size before the test, not after — more runs give tighter answers.
  3. Script your analysis. An R or Python script, or an SPSS syntax file, lets you and others repeat it exactly.
  4. Statistical significance is not engineering significance. A 0.2 °C difference may be "real" and still not matter.
