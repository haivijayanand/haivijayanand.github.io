01The requirement: tools every engineer can own

In the 1980s and 1990s, numerical work meant FORTRAN on mainframes or Unix workstations, or commercial packages: MATLAB (1984), Mathcad, LabVIEW (1986), NASTRAN and ANSYS for FEA, AutoCAD and Pro/ENGINEER for design (Episode 09). Licences were per seat and per year, tied to dongles or licence servers. Students lost access when they graduated; small labs and laboratories in developing countries could afford few seats; and results locked in proprietary file formats could not be checked by others.

04GNU Octave and Scilab: MATLAB-style computing

  
GNU Octave began in 1988 when John W. Eaton wrote software for a chemical-reactor design course at the University of Wisconsin; version 1.0 appeared in 1994. It is named after Octave Levenspiel, a chemical-engineering professor known for fast back-of-envelope calculations. Its language is largely compatible with MATLAB, so many .m scripts run unchanged; packages add signal processing, control and statistics. Scilab came from France's INRIA and ENPC (first release 1994) with its own similar language and Xcos, a block-diagram simulator comparable to Simulink.
