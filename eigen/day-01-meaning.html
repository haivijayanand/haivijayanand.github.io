What an Eigenvector Is

A matrix moves every vector somewhere else — except a few special directions that it only stretches. Those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are the eigenvalues. Today you find them by eye, by dragging, and by thinking about stretching, shearing, rotating and reflecting.

◆Today

  • Read A v = λ v as a sentence: "A does nothing to v except scale it by λ"
  • Find eigenvectors of simple transformations by geometry alone
  • Know what λ > 1, 0 < λ < 1, λ = 0 and λ < 0 mean physically
  • See why a rotation has no real eigenvectors and a shear has only one

1A matrix is a machine that moves vectors

Multiply a 2 × 2 matrix by a vector and you get a new vector. Do it for every vector in the plane and the whole plane is transformed: squares become parallelograms, circles become ellipses. Take

A = [ 2 1 ; 1 2 ]

and feed it a few vectors:

xA xWhat happened
(1, 0)(2, 1)stretched and turned by 26.6°
(0, 1)(1, 2)stretched and turned the other way
(1, 1)(3, 3)exactly 3 × the input — not turned at all
(1, −1)(1, −1)exactly 1 × the input — untouched

Most vectors get knocked off their line. Two directions do not. Those directions are the eigenvectors of A, and the factors 3 and 1 are its eigenvalues.

Definition. A non-zero vector v is an eigenvector of the square matrix A, with eigenvalue λ, when
A v = λ v
In words: A acting on v gives back v, only scaled by λ. "Eigen" is German for "own" or "characteristic" — these are the matrix's own directions.

Two things follow at once. Any multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector (A(2v) = 2Av = 2λv), so it is really a direction, a whole line through the origin. And the zero vector is excluded, because A·0 = λ·0 for every λ and would tell us nothing.

2Lab: hunt for the directions that do not turn

Drag the blue vector x around. The orange dashed arrow is A x. Watch the angle between them: when it drops to 0° (or 180°) you are sitting on an eigenvector, and the length ratio is the eigenvalue. Press P to sweep x round the circle automatically, X to stop. Then try the other presets.

Notice the black ellipse — the image of the unit circle. For the symmetric preset its longest and shortest radii lie exactly along the eigenvectors, and their lengths are the eigenvalues 3 and 1. That is no accident; Day 4 is about it.

3What the eigenvalue tells you

λAlong the eigenvector the matrix…Typical meaning
λ > 1stretchesgrowth, amplification, an unstable direction when repeated
λ = 1leaves it alonean equilibrium, a steady state, a rotation axis
0 < λ < 1shrinksdecay, damping, forgetting the starting point
λ = 0flattens it to nothinginformation lost; the matrix is singular (det A = 0)
λ < 0flips it round and scalesreflection, an alternating sequence
complex λno real direction survivesrotation, oscillation (Days 2 and 6)

Two quick rules you can check in the lab: the eigenvalues add up to the trace (a + d) and multiply to the determinant (ad − bc). For [2 1; 1 2]: 3 + 1 = 4 = 2 + 2 and 3 × 1 = 3 = 4 − 1. The determinant is the factor by which areas change, so it is natural that it equals the product of the stretches.

4Eigenvectors you can see without algebra

Example 1 — reflection in the line y = x: A = [0 1; 1 0]Vectors ON the mirror line, e.g. (1, 1), stay put → λ = +1 Vectors PERPENDICULAR to it, e.g. (1, −1), flip to (−1, 1) → λ = −1 Every other vector is turned. Check: trace 0 = 1 + (−1), det −1 = 1 × (−1).
Example 2 — projection onto the x-axis (a shadow from overhead light): A = [1 0; 0 0](1, 0) already lies on the axis → A(1, 0) = (1, 0), λ = 1 (0, 1) points straight up → A(0, 1) = (0, 0), λ = 0 λ = 0 means a whole direction is crushed: the projection cannot be undone, det A = 0.
Example 3 — shear (a deck of cards pushed sideways): A = [1 1; 0 1]Horizontal vectors slide along themselves: A(1, 0) = (1, 0), λ = 1. Any vector with a vertical part is pushed sideways and turned. Only ONE eigen-direction for a 2 × 2 matrix → a "defective" matrix (Day 2).
Example 4 — rotation by 36.9°: A = [0.8 −0.6; 0.6 0.8]Every vector is turned by 36.9°, so no real direction is kept. The eigenvalues are complex: 0.8 ± 0.6i = e^(±i·36.9°). Their size |λ| = 1 says "no stretching", their angle is the rotation angle.
In three dimensions a rotation DOES have a real eigenvector: its axis. Euler's rotation theorem says that any orientation of a rigid body can be reached from another by a single rotation about one fixed axis — and that axis is the eigenvector of the 3 × 3 rotation matrix with λ = 1. Robotics, spacecraft attitude control and camera calibration extract the rotation axis exactly this way.

5Why anyone cares

If you know the eigenvectors, a complicated matrix becomes simple: along each eigen-direction it is just multiplication by a number. Write any vector as a mix of eigenvectors, and A acts on each part separately. Apply A a hundred times and each part is multiplied by λ¹⁰⁰ — the parts with |λ| > 1 explode, those with |λ| < 1 die away, and the largest |λ| takes over. That single idea runs through the whole course:

Stress in a partThe stress matrix has three eigenvectors: the principal directions, where there is no shear. A crack opens along the largest eigenvalue (Day 4).
VibrationEach eigenvector is a mode shape — a pattern the structure vibrates in on its own — and each eigenvalue gives a natural frequency (Day 5).
StabilityA system x′ = Ax is stable exactly when every eigenvalue has a negative real part. Aircraft, rotors, control loops (Day 6).
Long-run behaviourMarkets, weather and populations settle onto the eigenvector with λ = 1 or the largest λ (Day 3).
DataThe eigenvectors of a covariance matrix are the directions in which the data varies most — the principal components (Day 7).
RankingGoogle's PageRank is the eigenvector with λ = 1 of a matrix built from the links of the web (Day 8).
Where you meet it in a test labOn a gearbox test rig the three accelerometer axes, the two proximity probes of a shaft and the stress gauges of a rosette all give you a vector at each instant. The "natural" axes in which these vectors look simplest — principal stresses, major axis of the shaft orbit, the direction of maximum vibration — are always eigenvectors of some symmetric matrix built from the readings.
Why eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are independent

Suppose v₁ and v₂ have different eigenvalues λ₁ ≠ λ₂ and c₁v₁ + c₂v₂ = 0. Apply A: c₁λ₁v₁ + c₂λ₂v₂ = 0. Subtract λ₂ times the first equation: c₁(λ₁ − λ₂)v₁ = 0. Since λ₁ ≠ λ₂ and v₁ ≠ 0, c₁ = 0, and then c₂ = 0. So they point in genuinely different directions. The same argument, by induction, shows that n eigenvectors with n different eigenvalues are independent — they form a basis, which is what makes them so useful.
