The Five Dysfunctions of a Team

Patrick Lencioni · first published 2002 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Teams fail not for lack of talent or strategy but because of five linked behavioural problems, starting with a lack of trust, that leaders can learn to fix.

Summary

Patrick Lencioni tells most of this book as a business fable. A struggling Silicon Valley technology company has talented people, money and a promising product, yet keeps falling behind. A new chief executive, Kathryn, concludes that the real problem is the senior team: capable individuals who do not behave as a team. Through a series of off-site meetings she patiently changes how they work together.

After the story, Lencioni sets out his model directly: five dysfunctions that build on each other like layers of a pyramid.

The base is an absence of trust. He means the willingness to be open about mistakes, weaknesses and the need for help. Without that, people guard themselves and waste energy on appearances.

Low trust leads to fear of conflict. Team members avoid honest, passionate debate about ideas and settle for polite, guarded discussion. Lencioni argues that productive conflict over issues, not personalities, is healthy and necessary.

Without real debate there is a lack of commitment. People who never voiced their views do not truly buy into decisions, even when they seem to agree. Clarity and buy-in matter more than consensus.

Weak commitment produces avoidance of accountability. If people are unsure what was agreed, they hesitate to challenge peers whose performance or behaviour hurts the team. The most effective pressure, he says, comes from colleagues rather than the boss.

At the top sits inattention to results. When accountability is missing, people put personal status, careers or their own departments ahead of the team's shared goals.

Lencioni offers practical tools for each layer, such as sharing personal backgrounds, discussing behavioural profiles, confirming decisions at the end of meetings and making results public. The leader must go first: showing vulnerability, encouraging debate, insisting on clarity and keeping everyone focused on collective outcomes.

Teamwork, he concludes, is rare not because it is complicated but because it demands discipline and courage.

312 words

One-line takeaway: Build trust through openness, welcome honest debate, commit clearly, hold one another accountable, and put the team's results above personal interests.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. Which dysfunction forms the foundation of Lencioni's pyramid?

    Answer C. The model starts with trust, because every other dysfunction grows out of people guarding themselves.

  2. What kind of trust does Lencioni say a team needs most?

    Answer A. He focuses on vulnerability-based trust, where people stop protecting their image and admit their shortcomings.

  3. How does Lencioni view conflict within a team?

    Answer D. He argues that honest, issue-focused debate leads to better decisions and stronger buy-in.

  4. Why do teams that avoid debate struggle with commitment?

    Answer B. Without having weighed in, members may appear to agree but do not really commit.

  5. According to the book, commitment requires:

    Answer B. Lencioni says people can commit to a decision they disagreed with if their views were heard and the decision is clear.

  6. Where does Lencioni say the most effective accountability comes from?

    Answer D. He holds that pressure from respected colleagues is stronger and more immediate than pressure from the boss.

  7. What does 'inattention to results' mean in the model?

    Answer A. The top dysfunction is about members pursuing individual or departmental interests instead of collective outcomes.

  8. In what form is most of the book written?

    Answer C. Lencioni teaches the model mainly through a story about a new CEO rebuilding a dysfunctional executive team.

  9. Which practice does Lencioni suggest for strengthening commitment?

    Answer D. Confirming what was decided ensures everyone leaves with the same understanding and can communicate it consistently.

  10. What is the leader's most important role in overcoming the dysfunctions?

    Answer A. The leader must model openness, encourage debate and keep the team focused on results for others to follow.
