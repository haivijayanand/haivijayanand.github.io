Bolted Joints: Preload, Tightening Torque, Joint Stiffness and Separation

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
BoltsPreloadVDI 2230Fasteners

A bolted joint works by clamping. The bolt is stretched like a stiff spring and the parts are compressed; external loads mostly change the clamp force rather than the bolt force, as long as the joint stays closed. Getting the preload right, and knowing how uncertain it is, is the heart of joint design.

Key takeaways

  • Tensile stress area As = (π/4)(d − 0.9382p)² for metric threads.
  • Target preload is usually 70–90 % of proof load (proof stress × As): 75 % for re-usable joints.
  • Torque T = K·Fi·d, with nut factor K ≈ 0.2 dry and 0.12–0.16 lubricated. Most torque is lost to friction.
  • Torque tightening scatters preload by ±25–30 %; angle or yield control and direct tension methods are far better.
  • With joint stiffness factor C, the bolt sees only C × external load; the joint separates at Fi/(1 − C).

2. Torque and preload

T = K · Fi · d K ≈ 0.20 dry steel, 0.15–0.16 oiled, 0.10–0.12 with MoS₂ paste

Typically about 50 % of the tightening torque overcomes friction under the head or nut, 40 % thread friction, and only about 10 % actually stretches the bolt. Small changes in friction change preload a lot.

3. The joint diagram

C = kb / (kb + km) joint stiffness factor (typically 0.1–0.4) Bolt force Fb = Fi + C · P Clamp force Fm = Fi − (1 − C) · P Separation P_sep = Fi / (1 − C)

Because C is small, most of the external load reduces clamp force rather than raising bolt force, which is why a properly preloaded bolt survives fatigue loads that would break an unpreloaded one.

Loss of preload

Embedding (settling of surface roughness, typically a few µm per interface), gasket creep, thermal differences and vibration-induced loosening all reduce preload. Use hardened washers, adequate grip length (long bolts lose less preload to embedding), and locking methods suited to transverse vibration.
