Belts are the cheapest, quietest way to carry power between shafts that are some distance apart, and they slip instead of breaking when overloaded. Learn the belt tension ratio, centrifugal tension, the speed for maximum power, and how to select V-belts from a catalogue.

    Geometry

    Open drive: θ (small pulley) = π − 2·asin((D − d) / 2C)
    Length L = 2C + (π/2)(D + d) + (D − d)² / (4C)
    Crossed drive: θ = π + 2·asin((D + d)/2C); L = 2C + (π/2)(D + d) + (D + d)²/(4C)

    The smaller pulley has the smaller angle of lap and slips first; it decides the capacity. Keep θ ≥ 120° (V-belts) and centre distance between about D and 3(D + d).

    Tensions and power

    Flat belt: (T₁ − Tc) / (T₂ − Tc) = eμθ Centrifugal tension Tc = m·v² (m = mass per metre)
    V-belt (groove angle 2β): replace μ by μ/sin β — wedge action, 2.5–3× the grip
    Power P = (T₁ − T₂)·v = (T₁ − m·v²)·(1 − e−μθ)·v
    Maximum power when Tc = T₁/3, i.e. v = √(Tmax / 3m)

    Centrifugal tension does no work but uses up belt strength. Above the optimum speed, the power falls; at v = √(Tmax/m) the belt carries nothing. Practical V-belt speeds: 5–30 m/s, best about 20–25 m/s.

    Selecting V-belts

    V-belts are not designed from first principles in practice — they are selected from the maker's or IS 2494 tables:

    1. Design power = rated power × service factor (1.0–1.2 fans and light conveyors; 1.3–1.5 compressors, machine tools; 1.6–2.0 crushers, mills).
    2. Choose the section from design power and small-pulley speed: A (13 mm), B (17), C (22), D (32) — or narrow wedge SPZ, SPA, SPB, SPC (more power per belt).
    3. Choose pulley diameters (not below the section minimum: A 75, B 125, C 200 mm) and the ratio.
    4. Calculate the pitch length; pick the nearest standard length and recalculate C.
    5. Read power per belt from the table; correct for angle of lap (Fθ) and length (FL).
    6. Number of belts = design power / (rated power per belt × Fθ × FL), rounded up.

    Belt types, materials and cost

    BeltStrengthsLimits

    Belt drives are usually the cheapest solution up to a few hundred kW. Their running cost is in tension maintenance and belt replacement — a drive with a slightly bigger pulley and fewer belts often costs less over its life.

    V-belt drive: pulleys 150 / 400 mm, C = 800 mm, 1440 rpm, μ = 0.3, belt allowable tension 500 N, m = 0.19 kg/mθ = π − 2·asin(250/1600) = 162°; L = 2483 mm (pick the nearest standard length) μ′ = 0.3/sin 20° = 0.877; e^(μ′θ) = 11.95 v = π × 0.15 × 1440/60 = 11.31 m/s; Tc = 0.19 × 11.31² = 24 N P per belt = (500 − 24)(1 − 1/11.95) × 11.31 = 4.93 kW Maximum power would be at v = √(500/0.57) = 29.6 m/s: 9.05 kW — a bigger driver pulley would use the belt better.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Set tension by the deflection method (force to deflect the span 16 mm per metre) and re-tension after the first day's running-in.
    • Replace V-belts in matched sets — one new belt in an old set takes all the load.
    • Shaft load from a belt drive is about T₁ + T₂ — often larger than the gear forces in a gearbox. Size bearings for it.
    • Guard every belt drive (Factories Act); design the guard to allow tensioning without removal.

    Roller chains

    Roller chains (IS 2403 / ISO 606) are named by pitch: 08B = 12.7 mm (½ in), 10B = 15.875, 12B = 19.05, 16B = 25.4 mm; "-2" and "-3" are duplex and triplex strands. The B series is the European/Indian form; the A series (ANSI 40, 50, 60, 80) has the same pitches with slightly different dimensions.

    Pitch Ø of sprocket D = p / sin(180°/z)
    Chain speed v = z·p·n / 60 000 m/s
    Length in pitches Ln = 2a/p + (z₁ + z₂)/2 + ((z₂ − z₁)/2π)² · p/a → round to an even number
    Design power = power × service factor × tooth factor; compare with the maker's rating chart

    The polygonal effect

    A chain wraps the sprocket as a polygon, not a circle. As each link engages, the chain's effective radius swings between R and R·cos(180°/z): the chain speed pulses and the chain rises and falls. This causes vibration, noise and impact of rollers on teeth.

    Speed variation = 1 − cos(180°/z) z = 11: 4.0 % z = 17: 1.7 % z = 25: 0.8 %

    Rules: at least 17 teeth on the small sprocket (19–25 at high speed); odd tooth numbers with an even number of links spread wear; ratio up to about 6:1; centre distance 30–50 pitches; slack side at the bottom.

    Lubrication and wear

    Chains wear at the pin–bush joint; the pitch grows. Replace a chain at 1.5–3 % elongation, or it rides up the teeth and jumps. Lubrication type depends on speed: manual or drip below ~3 m/s, oil bath or disc 3–12 m/s, forced spray above. Most chain failures are lubrication failures.

    Wire ropes

    A rope such as 6 × 37 FC has six strands of 37 wires round a fibre core: flexible, for cranes and hoists. 6 × 19 is stiffer and more abrasion-resistant; 6 × 7 for haulage. Steel core (IWRC) is stronger and crushes less on drums.

    Direct stress σd = (W + rope weight) / A
    Bending stress over a sheave σb = Er · dw / D (Er ≈ 84 GPa for the rope, dw = wire Ø, D = sheave Ø)
    Acceleration adds W·a/g; starting with slack rope adds impact
    Factor of safety = breaking load / (sum of loads) — IS 3177 cranes: 5–8; lifts by code

    The sheave-to-rope diameter ratio D/d strongly controls rope life: keep D ≥ 25–30 d for 6 × 37 ropes, more for long life.

    Materials and cost

    Chains: plain carbon/alloy steel, case-hardened pins and bushes; stainless or nickel-plated for food and wet duty; self-lubricating (sintered bush) where oiling is impossible. Sprockets: C45 with induction-hardened teeth for the small sprocket, cast iron for large driven sprockets. A chain drive costs more than a belt drive but less than gears for long centres; its running cost is lubrication and chain replacement. Wire ropes are consumables: inspect, discard by broken-wire count (IS 3973 / ISO 4309), and budget for replacement.

    Chain selection procedure

    1. Choose z₁ (≥ 17) and z₂ from the ratio.
    2. Design power = transmitted power × service factor (1.0–1.5) × multi-strand factor.
    3. From the maker's rating chart, choose pitch and strands at the small sprocket speed.
    4. Find chain length in links (even) and the exact centre distance; provide adjustment.
    5. Check chain speed and lubrication regime; check the breaking-load factor of safety (≥ 7–10 incl. dynamic loads).
    6. Design sprockets: tooth form from the standard, hub and bore.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Never use fewer than 9 teeth even on slow drives; below 17 expect noise and wear.
    • A duplex chain does not carry exactly twice a simplex — use the maker's multi-strand factor (≈ 1.7).
    • Vertical drives need tensioners — the lower sprocket loses contact as the chain stretches.
    • For ropes, reverse bends (S-shapes over two sheaves) cut life by half or more.

    The law of gearing

    For a constant speed ratio, the common normal at the point of contact of two teeth must always pass through one fixed point on the line of centres — the pitch point. Two curves do this well: cycloids (clocks) and involutes (nearly every power gear). The involute is the path of the end of a taut string unwound from a circle — the base circle.

    Why involutes winThe line of action is a straight line tangent to both base circles, so the force direction is constant. A small change in centre distance does not spoil the ratio. One straight-sided rack tool cuts all tooth numbers.
    Pressure angle φThe angle between the line of action and the common tangent at the pitch point. Standard 20°; 14½° (old, quieter, weaker); 25° (stronger, noisier).

    Proportions (IS 2535 / ISO 53, full depth)

    Pitch Ø d = m·z Circular pitch p = π·m Base Ø db = d·cos φ Base pitch pb = π·m·cos φ
    Addendum ha = m Dedendum hf = 1.25 m Tip Ø da = m(z + 2) Root Ø df = m(z − 2.5)
    Centre distance a = m(z₁ + z₂)/2 Speed ratio i = z₂/z₁ = n₁/n₂

    Preferred modules (IS 2535, first choice): 1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20, 25 mm — an R10 series again (Day 1). Two gears mesh only if they have the same module and pressure angle.

    Contact ratio

    The contact ratio is the average number of tooth pairs in contact. It must be above 1, or there will be moments with no tooth carrying the load.

    ε = [ √(ra1² − rb1²) + √(ra2² − rb2²) − a·sin φ ] / (π·m·cos φ)

    Spur gears: 1.4–1.8 is typical. Above 1.2 is acceptable; above 2 (high-contact-ratio gearing) runs very quietly. Helical gears add overlap (Day 23).

    Interference and undercut

    If the tip of the gear reaches below the pinion's base circle, it digs into the pinion's non-involute root — interference. A generating cutter removes that material instead — undercut — weakening the tooth.

    Minimum teeth on a pinion meshing with a rack: zmin = 2 / sin² φ → 20°: 17.1 (18); 14½°: 32
    With a gear of ratio u = z₂/z₁: zmin = 2/((1+2u)·sin²φ) · (u + √(u² + (1+2u)·sin²φ))

    Profile shift (x·m, cutter moved out) cures undercut on small pinions, balances tooth strength between pinion and gear, and lets you hit an exact centre distance. In practice a pinion with 12–17 teeth and a positive shift is normal.

    Materials, manufacture and cost

    Gears are generated by hobbing or shaping (any tooth number with one tool), then shaved or ground for accuracy. Accuracy grades IS 3681 / ISO 1328: 10–12 rough, 7–8 general machinery (hobbed), 5–6 ground automotive and gearbox test-rig gears, 3–4 master gears. Each grade finer costs significantly more; a ground, case-carburised gear can cost 3–5× a hobbed through-hardened one — but carries 2–3× the power in the same size.

    m = 4, z₁ = 18, z₂ = 36, φ = 20°d₁ = 72, d₂ = 144, a = 108 mm r_b1 = 33.83, r_b2 = 67.66, r_a1 = 40, r_a2 = 76 √(40² − 33.83²) = 21.34; √(76² − 67.66²) = 34.61; a·sin φ = 36.94 Length of contact = 19.02 mm; p_b = 11.81 mm → ε = 1.61 z_min for this ratio = 15 (rack: 18) → 18 teeth are free of interference.

    Procedure for gear geometry

    1. From the ratio, choose z₁ (≥ 18 for 20° without shift; fewer with profile shift) and z₂.
    2. Choose a module (from strength, Day 22) and the pressure angle.
    3. Calculate d, da, df, db and centre distance; adjust z or shift for a required a.
    4. Check contact ratio ≥ 1.2 (aim for 1.4+).
    5. Check interference / undercut; apply profile shift if needed.
    6. Specify on the drawing: m, z, φ, x, accuracy grade, span or tooth-thickness measurement, backlash.

    • Backlash is needed for oil film and thermal growth: about 0.03–0.05 × m, made by thinning teeth or adjusting centre distance.
    • Never mix 14½° and 20° gears, or module and diametral pitch gears.

    Loads on a tooth

    Pitch-line velocity v = π·d₁·n₁ / 60 000 m/s
    Tangential load Ft = P / v = 2T / d
    Effective load Peff = Cs·Ft / Cv (Cs service factor; Cv velocity (dynamic) factor)
    Barth: Cv = 3/(3+v) cut gears; 6/(6+v) hobbed; 5.6/(5.6+√v) precision ground

    Bending: the Lewis equation

    In 1892 Wilfred Lewis treated the tooth as a cantilever of uniform strength — a parabola inscribed in the tooth, touching at the root. The geometry collapses into one factor Y that depends only on the tooth number and form.

    Beam strength Sb = σb · b · m · Y (σb allowable bending stress; b face width ≈ 8–12 m)
    20° full depth: Y ≈ 0.484 − 2.87/z
    Design: Sb ≥ n · Peff

    Because the pinion has fewer teeth (smaller Y), it is weaker unless its material is stronger. Compare σb·Y for pinion and gear: the smaller product is the weaker member to design.

    Pitting: Buckingham's wear strength

    Tooth surfaces roll and slide under high Hertzian contact stress. After millions of cycles, small pits form at the pitch line and grow until the tooth profile is destroyed.

    Wear strength Sw = b · Q · d₁ · K
    Q = 2·z₂/(z₂ + z₁) (external gears) K = 0.16 (BHN/100)² MPa (steel on steel, 20°)
    Hertz contact stress σH = 0.591·√( Ft·E·(i+1) / (b·d₁·i·sin 2φ) ) — compare with surface endurance ≈ 2.76·BHN − 70 MPa

    Pitting resistance depends on hardness, so surface hardening (induction, carburising, nitriding) is the main lever. Bending depends on core strength and root fillet — module helps most there.

    Modern methods

    Lewis and Buckingham are excellent for first sizing and for understanding. Final rating uses ISO 6336 (or AGMA 2001 / IS 4460), which adds factors for load distribution across the face (KHβ), dynamics (Kv), application (KA), the real root stress (YF, YS), size, lubrication, and life. A spur gear rating tool built to ISO 6336 Method B is the right next step for any gear you will actually make.

    Spur gear design procedure

    1. Choose ratio, tooth numbers (Day 21), service factor, materials and hardness.
    2. Decide the weaker member (σb·Y smaller) for bending.
    3. Estimate the module from Lewis with an assumed Cv; round to a standard module.
    4. Recalculate v, Cv and Peff; check beam strength.
    5. Check wear strength (or Hertz stress). If short, raise hardness, then face width, then module/diameter.
    6. Rate the final design to ISO 6336 / AGMA; specify accuracy grade, backlash and lubrication.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Face width beyond about 12 m (or 1–1.2 d₁) does not help — misalignment loads only one end.
    • Crown or lead-correct the teeth on wide or heavily loaded gears so the load stays central.
    • Most gearbox failures start from lubrication or bearing problems, not tooth strength — check both.
    • Do not stop at Lewis for a production gear; the factors it leaves out can be ±50 %.

    Helical gears

    The teeth are cut at a helix angle ψ (typically 15–30°) to the axis. Contact starts at one end of a tooth and spreads across the face, so load is shared by more teeth and the mesh is smooth and quiet.

    Normal module mn (the cutter) and transverse module mt = mn / cos ψ
    Pitch Ø d = mn·z / cos ψ tan φn = tan φt · cos ψ
    Virtual number of teeth zv = z / cos³ψ (use for Y in the Lewis equation)
    Overlap ratio εβ = b·sin ψ / (π·mn) — aim for ≥ 1

    A pair of meshing parallel-shaft helical gears has equal helix angles of opposite hand. A double-helical (herringbone) gear has both hands side by side; the thrusts cancel.

    Forces on helical gears

    Ft = 2T / d Fr = Ft · tan φn / cos ψ Fa = Ft · tan ψ

    The axial thrust must be taken by a locating bearing (angular contact, tapered roller, or a deep groove ball bearing for light thrust). Its direction depends on the hand of helix and the direction of rotation — sketch it for each shaft. On a countershaft with two helical gears, choose hands so the thrusts oppose and partly cancel.

    Bevel gears

    Bevel gears connect intersecting shafts, usually at 90°. The teeth lie on pitch cones whose apexes meet at the shaft intersection.

    Pitch-cone angle tan γ₁ = z₁ / z₂ (at 90°) Virtual teeth zv = z / cos γ
    At the mean diameter: Ft = 2T / dm
    Pinion: Fr = Ft · tan φ · cos γ₁ Fa = Ft · tan φ · sin γ₁ (pushes away from the cone apex)
    The pinion's axial force equals the gear's radial force, and vice versa
    TypeFeatures
    Straight bevelSimple, low speed (< 5 m/s), noisy
    Spiral bevelCurved teeth, smooth, high load and speed; thrust direction depends on hand and rotation
    ZerolCurved teeth with zero spiral angle — forces like straight bevel
    HypoidOffset axes; vehicle rear axles; high sliding, needs EP (hypoid) oil

    Materials and cost

    Helical gears cost little more than spur gears to hob, so they are the default in industrial gearboxes. The cost is in the bearings and housing that must carry thrust. Spiral bevel gears need special generators (Gleason, Klingelnberg) and are usually bought as matched, lapped sets — always replace them as a pair. Case-carburised 20MnCr5 or 17CrNiMo6 is standard for automotive and helicopter bevel sets.

    Worked example

    Helical pinion: T = 120 N·m, d = 80 mm, φn = 20°, ψ = 20°Ft = 2 × 120 000 / 80 = 3000 N Fr = 3000 × tan20° / cos20° = 1162 N Fa = 3000 × tan20° = 1092 N Resultant 3397 N. The thrust (36 % of Ft) needs an angular-contact or tapered roller bearing at one end. Same pinion as straight bevel with γ = 25°: Fr = 3000 × 0.364 × 0.906 = 990 N, Fa = 461 N.

    Procedure

    1. Choose ψ (15–25° single helical; 30–40° double) and hand of helix.
    2. Find d, zv, overlap ratio; size by Lewis/Buckingham with zv and Cv (strength methods as Day 22, with the higher helical capacity).
    3. Calculate Ft, Fr, Fa for each gear; sketch their directions for the given rotation.
    4. Find bearing reactions in two planes, including the moment Fa·d/2.
    5. Arrange bearings: one locating (takes Fa) and one floating.
    6. For bevel gears, add mounting distance and adjustment shims to set the contact pattern.

    Lab: gear force resolver

    Choose spur, helical, bevel or worm and see the three force components at the pitch point. These are the inputs to Days 13 and 25.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • The axial force on a helical gear also creates a moment Fa·d/2 on the shaft — forgetting it gives wrong bearing loads.
    • Herringbone gears need one shaft free to float axially so the two halves share load.
    • Set bevel gear contact pattern with marking compound and shims during assembly — it is never right "as machined".

    Geometry

    Ratio i = z₂ / z₁ (z₁ = starts on the worm, 1–4)
    Worm pitch Ø d₁ = q·m (q = diameter factor, 8–16 standard) Wheel Ø d₂ = m·z₂
    Lead angle tan λ = z₁ / q Centre distance a = m(q + z₂)/2
    Sliding velocity vs = π·d₁·n₁ / (60 000·cos λ)

    The worm is like a screw (Day 12); the wheel is a helical gear with a throat that wraps the worm, so contact is along a line. Single-start worms give the highest ratio and the lowest efficiency.

    Efficiency and self-locking

    η (worm driving) = (cos φn − μ·tan λ) / (cos φn + μ·cot λ)
    η (wheel driving) = (cos φn − μ·cot λ) / (cos φn + μ·tan λ) — ≤ 0 means self-locking
    Friction for bronze on hardened steel falls with sliding speed: μ ≈ 0.08 at 0.1 m/s to ≈ 0.02 at 10 m/s

    Efficiency rises with lead angle (more starts) and with sliding speed (lower μ). Single-start, low-speed worms may be self-locking — but vibration can still let them creep. Never rely on a worm as the only brake on a hoist.

    Thermal rating

    All the lost power becomes heat in the oil. Worm drives are usually limited by temperature, not by tooth strength.

    Heat generated Q = Pin·(1 − η)
    Heat dissipated Q = h·A·(toil − tair) (h ≈ 10–20 W/m²K still air; 25–40 with a fan on the worm shaft)
    Keep the oil below about 80–90 °C (rise ≤ 50–60 °C)

    Materials and cost

    Worm: case-carburised and ground steel (20MnCr5, 16MnCr5) or hardened 40Cr4. Wheel: phosphor bronze (centrifugally cast rim on a cast-iron or steel hub to save bronze), aluminium bronze for heavy slow drives, cast iron only for light, slow, hand drives. Use EP gear oils compatible with bronze (some sulphur additives attack it) or PAG synthetics for best efficiency. Worm gearboxes are cheap per unit of ratio, but over years their energy loss can cost more than a helical-bevel box that is 95 % efficient (Day 29).

    Worked example

    2-start worm, q = 10, m = 6, wheel 40 teeth, 1440 rpm, 7.5 kW in, φn = 20°λ = atan(2/10) = 11.31°; d₁ = 60, d₂ = 240, a = 150 mm, ratio 20:1 vs = π × 60 × 1440/60 000/cos11.31° = 4.61 m/s → μ ≈ 0.022 η = (0.940 − 0.022 × 0.2)/(0.940 + 0.022 × 5) = 89.2 % Heat = 7.5 × 0.108 = 0.81 kW; housing 1.2 m², h = 15 → oil rise 45 °C ✓ Back-driving efficiency 88 % — this drive is NOT self-locking.

    Design procedure

    1. From the ratio, choose starts z₁ and wheel teeth z₂ (≥ 28–30 to avoid undercut at 20°).
    2. Choose q and module; find λ, diameters and centre distance (round a to a standard value).
    3. Find sliding velocity, μ and efficiency; decide whether self-locking is wanted.
    4. Check the wheel teeth for bending (Lewis, with zv) and wear (bronze wear factor).
    5. Check thermal capacity; add fan, fins or a cooler if needed.
    6. Find forces (Day 23) and size worm shaft and bearings — the worm shaft takes large thrust.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Worm drives lose efficiency at start-up (low sliding speed, high μ) — motors need extra starting torque.
    • Run in new worm sets at part load: the bronze conforms and efficiency improves by a few per cent.
    • The worm usually goes below the wheel (splash lubrication) unless speed is very high.
    • Check the worm shaft deflection — it is long and slender, and deflection spoils the contact.
