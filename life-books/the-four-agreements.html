The Four Agreements

Don Miguel Ruiz · first published 1997 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Much of our unhappiness comes from beliefs we absorbed as children without choosing them, and four simple personal promises can help us replace those beliefs and live with more freedom, honesty and peace.

Summary

Don Miguel Ruiz, who trained as a surgeon in Mexico before turning to the teachings of his family's Toltec tradition, wrote this short book about how we can free ourselves from needless suffering. His starting point is that, as children, we are taught what to believe about ourselves and the world long before we can question it. He calls this process domestication. We learn to chase approval, fear punishment, and carry an inner judge that keeps scolding us for falling short.

Ruiz suggests that these early lessons work like agreements we never chose. Many of them are harsh: that we are not good enough, not lovable, or must be perfect. To change our lives, he says, we can make four new agreements and practise them daily.

The first is to be impeccable with your word. Words are powerful; they can heal or wound, so speak honestly, avoid gossip, and do not use language against yourself or others. The second is not to take anything personally. What people say and do mostly reflects their own beliefs and moods, not your worth, so you need not carry their opinions as your burden.

The third is not to make assumptions. Much conflict at home grows from guessing what others think or want. Instead, ask questions and say clearly what you need. The fourth is always to do your best, knowing that your best changes from day to day. When you are tired or unwell, your best is smaller, and that is fine. Doing your best keeps you away from both guilt and harsh self-judgment.

Ruiz admits these agreements are hard to keep at first. Old habits are strong, and we will slip. The point is to begin again each time, gently, until the new way becomes natural. For families, the book offers a shared language: kinder words, fewer hurt feelings, clearer conversations, and room for everyone to try without needing to be perfect.

320 words

One-line takeaway: This week, pick one of the four agreements, write it on a card by the kitchen table, and at dinner each evening let every family member share one moment when they kept it or found it hard.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What does Ruiz call the process by which children absorb beliefs from the people and culture around them?

    Answer B. Ruiz uses the word domestication for the way we are trained into beliefs before we can choose them.

  2. Which tradition does Ruiz draw on for the ideas in the book?

    Answer D. The book presents its agreements as practical lessons from ancient Toltec teachings.

  3. What is the first of the four agreements?

    Answer A. Ruiz places speaking with honesty and care first because words shape our lives so strongly.

  4. According to the book, why should we not take things personally?

    Answer C. Ruiz argues that what others do is a projection of their own world, not a verdict on our worth.

  5. What does Ruiz recommend instead of making assumptions?

    Answer C. Clear questions and honest requests prevent the misunderstandings that guesses create.

  6. What does the book say about doing your best?

    Answer A. Ruiz stresses that your best varies with circumstances, and doing it frees you from guilt and self-judgment.

  7. Which inner figure does Ruiz describe as constantly criticising us by the rules we absorbed?

    Answer D. He describes an inner judge that punishes us against the beliefs we were taught.

  8. Under the first agreement, which habit does Ruiz especially warn against?

    Answer B. He sees gossip as a way words spread hurt and false beliefs among people.

  9. How does Ruiz suggest handling the times we break one of the agreements?

    Answer A. He expects slips and encourages starting over gently until the new habit takes root.

  10. Why does Ruiz say the old childhood agreements can cause suffering?

    Answer D. He argues that unchosen, fear-based beliefs keep us feeling unworthy and unhappy.
