3D CAD: from Sketchpad to CATIA, Pro/ENGINEER and SolidWorks

Episode 09 told how AutoCAD put 2D drafting on the PC. Mechanical design, though, is about solid parts that must fit, move and be made. 3D solid modelling began in aircraft and car companies on mainframes, was transformed by parametric "feature-based" modelling in 1988, and reached every design office with SolidWorks in 1995. Today the model is the master: drawings, FEA, machining and inspection all come from it.

01The requirement: one model that everything comes from

A 2D drawing is a set of views a human must interpret. It cannot check whether parts collide, compute mass, feed a CNC machine directly or be analysed by FEA. Aircraft and car makers in the 1960s–70s, with thousands of parts and complex curved surfaces, needed a single, exact 3D definition of every part.

02Four eras of 3D CAD

1. Mainframe pioneers (1963–1980)

Sketchpad (1963) let Ivan Sutherland draw on a screen with a light pen, with constraints between lines — an idea that returned 25 years later. General Motors and IBM built DAC-1; Lockheed's CADAM (1960s–70s) ran on IBM mainframes. Computervision sold turnkey CAD systems. In France, Avions Marcel Dassault developed CATI (1977) for fighter design; renamed CATIA, it was spun off as Dassault Systèmes in 1981 and marketed by IBM. McDonnell Douglas's Unigraphics and SDRC's I-DEAS (1982) followed.

2. Solid modelling on workstations (1980s)

Research at Cambridge (Shape Data, Romulus) and elsewhere produced boundary representation (B-rep) solid modelling: a part is stored as faces, edges and vertices that enclose a volume. Expensive Unix workstations ran these systems. Boeing designed the 777 (first flight 1994) entirely in CATIA — the first airliner developed without a physical mock-up.

3. Parametric, feature-based modelling (1988 →)

Samuel Geisberg founded Parametric Technology Corporation (PTC) in 1985. Pro/ENGINEER (1988) built parts from features — extrude, revolve, hole, fillet — driven by dimensions and stored as a history tree. Change a dimension and the model regenerates. Every major system adopted the idea.

4. Windows mid-range and the cloud (1995 →)

SolidWorks (founded by Jon Hirschtick in 1993, released 1995) brought parametric solids to ordinary Windows PCs at a fraction of the price; Dassault bought it in 1997. Intergraph's Solid Edge (1995, now Siemens) and Autodesk's Inventor (1999) competed. Cloud-native Onshape (2015, Hirschtick again; bought by PTC in 2019) and Fusion 360 (2013) run in the browser.

03Try it: a parametric flange

The flange is defined by a feature tree and a few driving dimensions. Change a parameter and the part regenerates; impossible values fail with an error, as in real CAD. Untick a feature to suppress it.

05Under the hood: kernels and exchange formats

Most CAD programs license a geometric modelling kernel that does the hard mathematics of Boolean operations, fillets and surfaces: Parasolid (1988, Cambridge; now Siemens — used by NX, SolidWorks, Solid Edge, Onshape), ACIS (1989, Spatial; now Dassault), CATIA's own CGM, PTC's Granite, and the open-source Open CASCADE (used by FreeCAD).

06Try it: exact geometry vs triangles

STEP stores a hole as an exact cylinder. STL approximates it with flat facets; the export tolerance (chord height) sets how many. Too coarse and a printed bearing seat is polygonal; too fine and files become huge.

09Lessons for engineers

  1. Model design intent. Dimension from the features that matter functionally (bearing bore, mounting face), so changes behave sensibly.
  2. Keep the feature tree clean. Name features; avoid referencing fillets and edges that may disappear.
  3. Archive in STEP too. Native files depend on one vendor's version; STEP AP242 is the long-term record.
  4. The model is not the part. Tolerances, surface finish and inspection still need drawings or model-based definition.
