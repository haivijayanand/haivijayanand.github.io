1What is a business?

A business is any activity in which people make or buy goods, or provide services, and sell them to others to earn a profit.

Think of Meena, who sets up a lemonade stall outside her building on a hot Sunday. She buys lemons, sugar and cups, makes lemonade, and sells each glass for ₹20. If she sells enough, she ends the day with more money than she started with. That extra money is her profit. Meena is running a business.

🛒 CustomersPeople who pay for what the business sells. No customers, no business.
📦 ProductSomething you can touch (a good, like lemonade) or something done for you (a service, like a haircut).
🎲 RiskMeena might not sell enough lemonade. Then she loses money. Every business owner takes this risk.
💰 ProfitMoney earned from sales minus money spent. Profit is the reward for taking the risk.

1Business, profession or employment?

People earn a living in three ways. A business has five features that set it apart:

1 · ExchangeGoods or services are sold for money; growing vegetables only for your own kitchen is not business.
2 · RegularitySelling your old scooter once is not business; buying and selling scooters every month is.
3 · Profit motiveThe main aim is to earn more than you spend.
4 · Risk and uncertaintyDemand, prices, rules and competitors change. The owner bears the loss if things go wrong.
5 · CapitalMoney must be invested before any income comes in.

3How big is your business? India's MSME classes

Since 1 April 2025, a manufacturing or service unit is classed by both its investment in plant, machinery or equipment and its annual turnover. If either figure crosses a limit, the unit moves up a class. Exports are left out of turnover. Being an MSME brings priority-sector loans, collateral-free credit guarantees, the 45-day payment rule for buyers and government tender benefits.

1Why do firms exist?

If markets allocate resources efficiently through prices, why is so much activity organised by command inside firms? Coase (1937) answered: using the market has costs: finding a supplier, negotiating, writing and enforcing contracts. A firm exists where organising a transaction internally is cheaper than contracting for it. It grows until the cost of one more internal transaction equals the cost of buying it in the market.

Williamson sharpened this into transaction cost economics. A transaction is more likely to be brought inside the firm when it has:

Asset specificityInvestments worth much less in any other use (a die made for one customer, a plant next to one mine). This invites hold-up.
UncertaintyContracts cannot spell out every contingency (bounded rationality).
FrequencyRecurring transactions spread the fixed cost of a governance structure.

Grossman–Hart–Moore property-rights theory adds that ownership of assets confers residual control rights, which shape investment incentives when contracts are incomplete.

2Make or buy? A transaction-cost toy model

Compare the yearly cost of making a component in-house with buying it. Buying carries a market price plus transaction costs that rise with asset specificity (expected hold-up loss). Making carries a governance cost (bureaucracy, weaker incentives) but avoids hold-up.

Make vs buy

The model is a teaching device, but the logic is real: as specificity rises, the hold-up cost of buying rises, and the switch-over point moves toward making. This is why carmakers own their body-panel dies but buy standard bolts.

4Legal and statistical definitions in India

"Business" is defined broadly for tax: the Income-tax Act includes any trade, commerce, manufacture or any adventure or concern in the nature of trade. For GST, "business" includes any trade or similar activity whether or not for profit, plus transactions incidental to it, so even a one-off sale of a business asset can be a taxable supply. The MSME law uses the composite investment-and-turnover test above; the Companies Act has its own "small company" test (paid-up capital up to ₹10 crore and turnover up to ₹100 crore since 1 December 2025). These definitions do not line up, so a firm can be "medium" for MSMED purposes and "small" under company law.

Open questions: how to define the boundary of platform firms (are gig workers inside or outside the firm?), and whether the four Labour Codes in force since 21 November 2025, which bring gig and platform workers into social security, shift that boundary.
