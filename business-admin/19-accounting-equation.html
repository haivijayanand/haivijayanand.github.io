The Accounting Equation

Everything a business owns was paid for by someone: either by people it owes (liabilities) or by its owner (capital). That simple fact, the accounting equation, is the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping and of the balance sheet. Pick your level.

Level 1 · For school students

What a business owns always equals what it owes to others plus what it owes to its owner.

1Owns = owes

Assets (what the business owns) = Liabilities (what it owes to others) + Capital (what it owes to the owner)

Kavitha puts ₹5,00,000 of her savings into Kaveri Stores. Now the shop owns ₹5,00,000 cash, and it owes Kavitha ₹5,00,000. If it borrows ₹1,00,000 from a bank, it owns ₹6,00,000 and owes ₹1,00,000 to the bank and ₹5,00,000 to Kavitha. Always equal!

3Why profit increases capital

Revenue increases what the owner is owed; expenses reduce it; drawings take it out. So

Assets = Liabilities + Opening capital + Revenue − Expenses − Drawings Closing capital = Opening capital + Net profit − Drawings

For Kaveri Stores in April (after month-end adjustments in Chapter 26): opening capital ₹5,00,000 + net profit ₹31,500 − drawings ₹10,000 = ₹5,21,500.

Level 3 · For experts — a review

The equation's algebra, its link to profit, and using it to audit and reconstruct accounts.

1Solve for the missing figure

The equation is an identity, so any one unknown can be recovered: useful for incomplete records (single-entry businesses), estimating profit from a statement of affairs, or checking a balance sheet.

Statement of affairs method

2The equation under Ind AS

Equity is a residual: assets minus liabilities, not independently measured. It includes share capital, other equity (securities premium, retained earnings, other comprehensive income reserves). Total comprehensive income = profit + OCI; OCI items (e.g., remeasurement of defined benefit plans, FVOCI equity investments) bypass profit but still change equity, so

ΔEquity = Profit + OCI + Owner contributions − Distributions (clean-surplus relation)

The clean-surplus relation underpins the residual income valuation model (Ohlson 1995): value = book equity + PV of future (earnings − cost of equity × opening book). Violations of clean surplus (items going straight to equity) weaken the link.

3Matrix view

Each transaction is a vector that sums to zero across the signed accounts (assets positive, liabilities and equity negative). A trial balance is the sum of all vectors. This is why a transaction that touches only one account is impossible in double entry, and why the equation cannot detect compensating errors or a wrong-account posting (Chapter 22).
