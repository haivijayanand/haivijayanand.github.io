Reliability, DFMA and Life-Cycle Cost

A design is judged by how long it works and what it costs over its whole life, not just its price. Learn reliability with the Weibull distribution, series and parallel systems, FMEA, design for manufacture and assembly, value engineering, and life-cycle cost with discounting.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: Reliability and cost of ownership❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Use the Weibull distribution and know what the shape factor β tells you
  • Calculate reliability of series and redundant systems
  • Run a simple FMEA and rank risks
  • Apply DFMA and value engineering to cut cost
  • Compare designs by life-cycle cost (present value)

2Reliability

Reliability R(t) is the probability that a part works without failure up to time t. Mechanical lives are well described by the Weibull distribution:

R(t) = exp(−(t/η)β) η = characteristic life (63.2 % have failed) Mean life = η·Γ(1 + 1/β)
β < 1: infant mortality (early failures, poor assembly) β ≈ 1: random (constant failure rate) β > 1: wear-out (fatigue β ≈ 1.5–3)
Series system: R = R₁·R₂·…·Rₙ Parallel (redundant): R = 1 − (1 − R₁)(1 − R₂)…

A gearbox is a series system — two gears, four bearings, two seals, oil: each must work. Ten parts at 99 % give only 90 %. Redundancy (a standby pump, two brakes on a lift) raises reliability for critical functions. Rolling bearing L10 (Day 25) is the Weibull life at R = 0.9 with β ≈ 1.1–1.5.

3FMEA

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis lists, for each part, how it can fail, what happens, and why, then scores Severity, Occurrence and Detection (1–10 each). Their product, the Risk Priority Number (or the newer AIAG-VDA action priority), ranks what to fix first.

4DFMA and value engineering

Day 3 introduced DFMA. Value engineering asks of every feature: what is its function (verb + noun: "locate bearing", "transmit torque"), and what is the cheapest way to perform it? Typical wins: replace a machined spacer by a circlip; combine a bracket and a housing into one casting; replace a keyed hub with a taper-lock bush; use a standard gear motor instead of a custom gearbox.

5Life-cycle cost

LCC = purchase + installation + Σ (energy + maintenance + downtime)ₜ / (1 + r)ᵗ − residual value
Energy cost per year = (output kW / η) × hours × tariff

For anything that runs many hours — motors, pumps, gearboxes, compressors — energy dominates: a motor's purchase price is often only a few per cent of what it costs to run over ten years. A 5 % efficiency gain usually pays back within one or two years. For low-use equipment (a manual hoist, a test fixture), purchase price and reliability matter more.

6Worked example

Reliability: six parts in series, each Weibull β = 1.5, η = 20 000 h, mission 5000 hOne part: R = exp(−(5000/20 000)^1.5) = exp(−0.125) = 88.2 % Six in series: 0.882⁶ = 47 % — less than half the units survive 5000 h without any failure Mean life of one part = 20 000 × Γ(1.667) = 18 060 h Cost: drive A ₹60 000 at 88 %, drive B ₹85 000 at 93 %, 15 kW output, 4000 h/yr, ₹8/kWh, 10 % discount Energy A = 15/0.88 × 4000 × 8 = ₹5.45 lakh/yr; B = ₹5.16 lakh/yr → saves ₹29 000/yr B repays its extra ₹25 000 in the first year; over 10 years its NPV is ₹1.6 lakh lower.

7Procedure

  1. Write the reliability target (e.g. 95 % over the warranty) and the mission profile.
  2. Break the machine into a reliability block diagram (series/parallel).
  3. Assign life data to each block (bearing L10, seal life, gear rating, field data).
  4. Run an FMEA; act on the highest-risk items (design change first, inspection last).
  5. Apply DFMA/value engineering to remove parts and cost.
  6. Compare final options by life-cycle cost, not by price.

9Pro tips and common mistakes

  • "Mean time between failures" hides the shape: an MTBF of 20 000 h with β = 3 is very different from β = 1.
  • Field data beats handbooks. Log every failure with hours, cause and photo.
  • Include downtime cost — a ₹2000 seal that stops a production line for a shift costs lakhs.

10Practice

  1. A machine has 12 parts each 99.5 % reliable over a year. What is the system reliability? How many parts can it have for 95 %?
  2. Write a five-line FMEA for a belt drive.
  3. In the lab, find the efficiency at which option B stops paying back within 10 years.
