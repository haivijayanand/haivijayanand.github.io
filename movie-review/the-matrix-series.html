The Matrix (1999) imagined that the everyday world is a computer simulation built by machines that farm human bodies for energy. A hacker called Neo is pulled out of it, trained to bend its rules, and comes to believe he is “the One” who can end the war.

Two sequels shot back to back, Reloaded and Revolutions, expanded this into a war for the last human city, Zion, and released six months apart in 2003. Eighteen years later Lana Wachowski returned alone with Resurrections, a film that is partly a sequel and partly a commentary on sequels themselves.

The first film is a near-perfect machine. The rest of the series is the sound of that machine trying — sometimes brilliantly, sometimes clumsily — to understand what it means.

The Matrix

1999 · Review 05a

Plot

Thomas Anderson is a programmer by day and a hacker called Neo by night. Morpheus offers him a choice of pills and wakes him into the real world: around 2199, a ruined Earth where machines grow humans in pods for energy and keep their minds pacified inside a simulation — the Matrix. Neo is trained to bend the simulation’s rules, the crew visit the Oracle, and the crew member Cypher betrays them to the machines.

Neo is killed by Agent Smith inside the Matrix — then revives, now able to see and rewrite its code, and destroys Smith.

Numbers

Budget $63 million — a big risk on two little-known directors and untested effects. Shot in 118 days (March–August 1998), mostly at Fox Studios, Sydney. Opened with $27.8 million and grossed about $467 million worldwide, fourth-highest of 1999. Won four Oscars.

The Matrix Reloaded

2003 · Review 05b

Plot

Six months on, Neo and Trinity are together, and machine Sentinels are tunnelling towards Zion. Neo, Morpheus and Trinity hunt for the Source, the machines’ core, hoping to end the war. On the way they meet the Merovingian, an old program who hoards secrets, his wife Persephone, and the Keymaker.

At the Source, the Architect tells Neo that “the One” is part of the system’s design: he can reboot the Matrix and save Zion’s future, or save Trinity and let the Matrix crash. He chooses Trinity, brings her back after she is shot, then stops Sentinels in the real world with his mind — and collapses into a coma.

Numbers

Budget $127–150 million. Shot back to back with Revolutions from March 2001 to August 2002. Opened with $91.7 million — then the second-biggest opening ever — and grossed about $742 million, the highest in the series. It held the record for highest-grossing R-rated film until Deadpool in 2016.

The Matrix Revolutions

2003 · Review 05c

Plot

Neo’s mind is trapped in Mobil Ave, a limbo between the Matrix and the machine world, controlled by the Merovingian’s Trainman. Trinity, Morpheus and Seraph force his release. The Oracle warns that Agent Smith, now a self-copying virus, threatens both worlds. While the Sentinels storm Zion’s docks, Neo and Trinity fly to the Machine City.

Trinity dies in the crash. Neo offers the machines a deal — he will stop Smith in exchange for peace. Inside the Matrix he lets Smith absorb him, and the machines destroy every Smith through Neo’s body. Neo seems to die; the war ends, and the Architect agrees that humans who want to leave the Matrix will be set free.

Numbers

Budget estimated at $110–150 million. Shot in Sydney: Fox Studios, the disused tunnels of St James station (subway scenes) and the Royal Botanic Garden (the Oracle–Architect ending). Released on 5 November 2003 in 108 territories at the same time — the first live-action film released together in regular and IMAX theatres. Grossed about $427 million, roughly half of Reloaded.

The Matrix Resurrections

2021 · Review 05d

Plot

Sixty years after Revolutions, Thomas Anderson is a celebrated video-game designer — who cannot tell his dreams from reality. Trinity lives as Tiffany, a suburban mother with no memory of her past. A young captain, Bugs, and her ship the Mnemosyne pull Neo out of a new Matrix, helped by a reprogrammed Morpheus.

They learn that the Analyst — Neo’s own therapist — rebuilt both Neo and Trinity and kept them close but apart, because the tension between them generates more energy for the machines. Neo and Trinity break free together and set out to remake the Matrix.

Numbers

Budget $190 million. Filming began in San Francisco on 4 February 2020 under the code name “Project Ice Cream”, moved to Studio Babelsberg in Germany, was stopped by COVID-19 on 16 March 2020 and wrapped in November 2020. Released in cinemas and on HBO Max the same day; it grossed only about $160 million and is widely counted a box-office failure.

Technology & craft

Few film series have changed visual-effects history as much. Each film pushed a different technique.

Bullet time (1999)

A ring of still cameras was fired one after another around an actor, and optical-flow software filled in the frames between them. The result moves the viewpoint around a frozen or slowed moment — an effective rate of about 12,000 frames per second. Manex Visual Effects rendered many shots on a cluster of FreeBSD computers.

Paper before pixels

Comic artists Geof Darrow and Steve Skroce drew a 600-page shot-by-shot storyboard, which helped convince Warner Bros. to back an untested visual style.

Kung fu training

From October 1997 the cast trained for about four months in martial arts and wire work under Yuen Woo-ping. Keanu Reeves had just had spinal fusion surgery and could not kick for two of those months.

Colour as code

Scenes inside the Matrix carry a green tint, a nod to old monochrome computer screens; the real world is graded blue. The famous falling “digital rain” uses the same green.

Universal Capture (2003)

For Reloaded, the ESC team recorded actors’ faces in fine detail so fully digital doubles of Reeves and Hugo Weaving could act alongside the real ones — the “virtual cinematography” behind the Burly Brawl, where Neo fights a hundred Smiths. That fight alone took 27 days to film and cost about $40 million. Final effects were rendered with Mental Ray.

A freeway built from scratch

For Reloaded’s chase, the producers built a 1.5-mile freeway on the old runways of Naval Air Station Alameda in California. General Motors donated over 300 cars, all wrecked. About 97% of set materials were recycled afterwards.

IMAX on day one

Revolutions opened in 108 territories at the same moment and was the first live-action film released in regular and IMAX cinemas together.

Filming through a pandemic

Resurrections was halted by COVID-19 four days after moving to Germany. Lana Wachowski thought of abandoning it; the cast persuaded her to finish. Its effects team earned a BAFTA nomination.

Ideas as raw material

The first film drew on Hong Kong action films, anime such as Ghost in the Shell, Baudrillard’s Simulacra and Simulation, Plato’s cave, Descartes and William Gibson’s Neuromancer.

Music

For the trilogy, Don Davis wrote an orchestral and choral score, mixed with synthesiser, that plays on the films’ ideas of mirrors and reflections. The 1999 companion album added heavy electronic and rock tracks from Rammstein, Rage Against the Machine, The Prodigy and Marilyn Manson, which defined the film’s late-90s sound.

On Reloaded and Revolutions, Davis worked with the electronic group Juno Reactor on the action scenes. For Resurrections, Lana Wachowski turned to her long-time collaborators Johnny Klimek and Tom Tykwer.

Budget & box office

Budget vs worldwide gross (US$ million)

■ Budget   ■ Worldwide gross. For Reloaded and Revolutions the bar shows the upper budget estimate.

The curve tells the story of the franchise. The original turned $63 million into about $467 million. Reloaded was the commercial peak at about $742 million. Revolutions, released only six months later, earned roughly half that.

Resurrections cost the most — $190 million — and earned the least, about $160 million. It was released on HBO Max the same day it reached cinemas during the pandemic, and co-financier Village Roadshow later sued Warner Bros. over that decision.

Emotional arc across the series

Intensity (0–10) at key moments, scored for this review. Dashed lines separate the four films.

Where the emotions come from

The red pill. The first film’s great feeling is the shock of waking up — the suspicion that the ordinary world is fake, followed by the thrill of learning you can break its rules.
Becoming the One. Neo’s death and return in 1999 is pure myth: a resurrection scene that turns an action film into a story about belief.
The Architect’s room. In Reloaded, Neo learns his “prophecy” was a control system. Choosing Trinity over the plan is the series’ first real act of free will.
Trinity’s death. Revolutions earns its sadness here, in one of the quietest scenes of the trilogy, as she tells Neo she loves him.
A sacrifice without victory. Neo does not defeat Smith; he lets Smith take him. The trilogy ends in peace, not triumph.
A personal sequel. Lana Wachowski wrote Resurrections after losing both parents and a close friend; bringing Neo and Trinity back was, she said, a comfort. Its warmth comes from that grief.

How to watch

  • Must see: The Matrix (1999). It stands on its own.
  • Watch as one long film: Reloaded and Revolutions back to back — they were shot together and play better that way.
  • Watch last, as an epilogue: Resurrections, ideally knowing it is a personal, meta film rather than a return to 1999-style action.
  • Optional: The Animatrix and the game Enter the Matrix fill in gaps between films.

Awards & legacy

Awards

  • The Matrix: four Oscars — Visual Effects, Film Editing, Sound, Sound Effects Editing
  • BAFTAs for Sound and Special Visual Effects; Saturn Awards for Best Science Fiction Film and Best Director
  • Added to the US National Film Registry in 2012
  • Resurrections: BAFTA nomination for Special Visual Effects

Influence

  • Made “bullet time” a standard tool of films, games and adverts
  • Brought Hong Kong wire-fu choreography into Hollywood
  • Turned the “red pill” into an everyday phrase
  • Helped shape the modern effects-driven blockbuster

Readings & future

  • Read in terms of Gnosticism, Christianity and Buddhism; Lilly Wachowski has called the first film an allegory of gender transition
  • A fifth film is in development, with Drew Goddard writing and directing
