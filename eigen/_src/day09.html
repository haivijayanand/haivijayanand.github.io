Let the number of masses go to infinity and the matrix becomes a differential operator: −y″ = λy. Its eigenvalues are the buckling loads of columns, the harmonics of a guitar string, the decay rates of heat and the energy levels of atoms. Chop it back into a finite grid and it is a matrix eigenproblem again — which is how every FE and quantum-chemistry code solves it.
  • Recognise boundary-value eigenproblems: −y″ = λy with end conditions
  • Compute Euler buckling loads and effective lengths as eigenvalues
  • Explain standing waves, harmonics and quantised energy levels as eigenfunctions
  • Turn the continuous problem into a tridiagonal matrix and judge the accuracy of its eigenvalues

    1From matrices to functions

    A taut string is a chain of infinitely many tiny masses. The stiffness matrix of Day 2, [2 −1 0; −1 2 −1; 0 −1 2]/h², is the finite-difference version of minus the second derivative: (−yᵢ₋₁ + 2yᵢ − yᵢ₊₁)/h² ≈ −y″(xᵢ). So the continuous eigenproblem is

    −y″(x) = λ y(x), y(0) = y(L) = 0

    The "eigenvectors" are now functions — eigenfunctions. The solutions that vanish at both ends are

    yₙ(x) = sin(nπx/L), λₙ = (nπ/L)², n = 1, 2, 3, …

    Infinitely many eigenvalues, each eigenfunction with one more half-wave than the last, all mutually orthogonal (∫ yₘyₙ dx = 0) — the continuous version of the Day 4 and Day 5 theorems. Expanding a function in these eigenfunctions is exactly a Fourier sine series.

    2Buckling: the load at which a new shape becomes possible

    A straight column under axial load P can also sit in equilibrium in a bent shape if

    EI y″ + P y = 0 ⟺ −y″ = (P/EI) y

    That is our eigenproblem with λ = P/EI. Below the first eigenvalue only the straight shape exists; at P = λ₁EI a bent shape becomes possible and the column buckles. The eigenvalue is the critical load, the eigenfunction the buckled shape.

    Example 1 — steel rod Ø20 mm, length 1 m, pinned at both endsI = πd⁴/64 = π·0.02⁴/64 = 7.85 × 10⁻⁹ m⁴, E = 200 GPa → EI = 1571 N·m² λ₁ = (π/L)² = 9.87 m⁻² → P_cr = π²EI/L² = 9.87 × 1571 = 15.5 kN Second mode (one extra half-wave) needs 4 × that, 62 kN — only reached if mid-span is braced.
    Example 2 — same rod as a flagpole (fixed base, free top)Boundary conditions change the eigenvalues: shape = 1 − cos(πx/2L), λ₁ = (π/2L)² P_cr = π²EI/(4L²) = 3.88 kN — a quarter of the pinned value Effective length 2L. Fixed–fixed gives L/2 and 4× the pinned load: P = 62 kN.

    The effective-length factors (2, 1, 0.7, 0.5) printed in every steel code are nothing but the square roots of ratios of eigenvalues for different end conditions. FE programs do "linear buckling analysis" by solving K φ = λ K_G φ, where K_G is the geometric stiffness from the applied load — a generalised eigenproblem just like Day 5's.

    3Strings, harmonics and heat

    Example 3 — a guitar string (L = 0.65 m, tension T = 70 N, μ = 0.4 g/m)Wave equation μ y_tt = T y_xx; separate y = φ(x)cos ωt → −φ″ = (μω²/T) φ Eigenvalues (nπ/L)² → fₙ = (n/2L)√(T/μ) = n × (1/1.3)√(175 000) = n × 321.8 Hz Harmonics 322, 644, 965, 1287 Hz … — every mode is a whole multiple of the first. That is why a string sounds like a clear note. A drum's circular-membrane eigenvalues (zeros of Bessel functions) are NOT whole multiples, so a drum has no clear pitch.

    Heat conduction gives the same eigenfunctions with a different time behaviour. Temperature in a slab is a sum of eigenmodes, each decaying as e^(−λₙαt). The higher modes (fine detail) die out fast; after a short time only the first mode remains, decaying with time constant L²/(π²α). For a 20 mm steel plate (α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s) that is 0.02²/(9.87 × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵) = 3.4 s — the time scale of quenching and of a thermocouple's response.

    4Quantum mechanics: energy levels are eigenvalues

    The time-independent Schrödinger equation is an eigenproblem for the energy:

    −(ħ²/2m) ψ″ + V(x) ψ = E ψ (Hamiltonian H, so H ψ = E ψ)

    Only certain energies E — the eigenvalues — allow a wavefunction that stays finite. That is why atoms emit light only at sharp colours: the energy levels are quantised because they are eigenvalues.

    Example 4 — an electron in a 1 nm "box" (a quantum dot, a dye molecule)V = 0 inside, infinite walls → our sine eigenfunctions Eₙ = n²h²/(8mL²) = n² × 0.376 eV E₁ = 0.376 eV, E₂ = 1.50 eV, E₃ = 3.38 eV Jump 1 → 2 absorbs 1.13 eV → wavelength 1240/1.13 = 1100 nm (near infra-red). Smaller box → bigger gaps → bluer light: why small quantum dots glow blue and large ones red.

    For a parabolic well V = ½kx² (a chemical bond as a spring) the eigenvalues are evenly spaced, Eₙ = (n + ½)ħω — the vibrational ladder seen in infra-red spectroscopy of molecules. For two wells side by side (the ammonia molecule, whose nitrogen can sit above or below its hydrogens) the lowest two eigenvalues are very close; their tiny gap is the frequency of the ammonia maser, 23.87 GHz.

    5Lab: solve them all as one matrix eigenproblem

    Pick a problem and a number of grid points N. The lab builds the N × N tridiagonal matrix, finds its eigenvalues and eigenvectors (Jacobi's method, Day 10) and compares them with the exact values. Watch the error fall by four when you double N, and watch the higher modes need more points.

    Example 5 — how accurate is the grid?The matrix eigenvalues are known exactly: λₖ(h) = (4/h²) sin²(kπh/2L) For small h: λₖ(h) ≈ (kπ/L)² [1 − (kπh/L)²/12] Pinned column, N = 30 (h = 1/31 m): error in λ₁ = −(π/31)²/12 = −0.086 % Mode 4 with the same grid: −(4π/31)²/12 = −1.37 % — 16 × worse. FE meshes are sized the same way: enough elements per half-wave of the highest mode you need.
