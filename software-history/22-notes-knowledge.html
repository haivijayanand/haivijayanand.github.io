Notes and personal knowledge: from the Memex to Obsidian

Engineers and researchers collect knowledge for decades — test observations, formulas, supplier details, lessons from failures — and then cannot find it when needed. Note-taking software promised a "second brain". Its history runs from a 1945 thought experiment and a German sociologist's 90,000 index cards to OneNote, Evernote, Notion, and a new generation of linked, local Markdown notes.

01The requirement: remember, find and connect

In 1945 Vannevar Bush, who led US wartime research, imagined the Memex: a desk that stored a person's books, records and notes on microfilm and let them create "associative trails" between items. It was never built, but it inspired hypertext, the web and personal knowledge software. Meanwhile, the German sociologist Niklas Luhmann kept a Zettelkasten ("slip box") of index cards, each with a number and references to related cards; he credited it with his very large output of books and papers.

02Three eras of note software

1. Notebooks on the PC (1987–2007)

Apple's HyperCard (1987) let anyone build linked card stacks. Lotus Agenda (1988) was a free-form personal database. Microsoft OneNote (Office 2003) copied the paper notebook — notebooks, section tabs, pages where you can type, draw or paste anywhere — and became popular with pen-based tablet PCs. It became free in 2014 and remains part of Microsoft 365.

2. The cloud notebook (2008–2018)

Evernote (launched 2008) offered "remember everything": notes synced across phone and PC, a web clipper, and text search inside photos. It reached hundreds of millions of sign-ups, then lost users through feature bloat, price rises and slow apps. The Italian company Bending Spoons bought it in 2022–23, moved its operations to Europe, cut the free plan and rebuilt it; version 11, rolled out from 19 January 2026, added AI search and assistants. Google Keep (2013) took the simple sticky-note role; Apple Notes improved steadily.

3. Linked and block-based notes (2016 →)

Notion (2016) made pages out of blocks — text, tables, boards, databases — for teams and individuals. Roam Research (2019) popularised two-way links and daily notes. Obsidian (2020) brought the same ideas to plain Markdown files in a folder on your own disk, with plugins and a graph view; Logseq (2020) and Joplin (2017) offered open-source equivalents. The idea of the Zettelkasten — small notes, densely linked — became a popular method again.

03Try it: linked notes and backlinks

Write notes and link them with [[Note name]], as in Obsidian, Logseq or Roam. The graph shows notes as points and links as lines; the panel lists backlinks — which notes mention the selected one — something a paper notebook can never show you.

06Owning your notes

  • Lock-in is real. When Evernote cut its free plan in 2023, many long-time users found that moving years of notes was hard. Before committing a decade of knowledge, check that you can export everything, with attachments, in an open format.
  • Local files are durable. A folder of Markdown files can be read by any text editor (Episode 16), searched, backed up and kept under version control with Git.
  • Official information stays in official systems. Work-related or classified material should not be copied into personal cloud note apps; use the tools your organisation approves.
  • AI features read your notes. Evernote, Notion and OneNote now offer AI assistants; check where the processing happens and what the terms allow.

08Lessons for engineers

  1. Write notes for your future self. Record why, not just what — the reason a test was stopped matters years later.
  2. Link, don't just file. A note on a bearing failure should link to the rig, the supplier and the analysis.
  3. Choose formats that outlive the app. Markdown and PDF will be readable long after today's note apps.
  4. Tools don't think for you. Luhmann's system worked because he wrote and linked every card himself.
