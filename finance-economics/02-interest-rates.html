Interest Rates

Level 1 · For school students

If you have ever lent a friend a pencil and asked for it back, you already know half of this.

1What is interest?

Interest is the extra money you pay for borrowing money, or the extra money you earn for lending or saving it. The interest rate says how much extra, as a percent per year.

Think of interest as rent for money. If you rent a bicycle for a day, you pay rent and give the bicycle back. If you borrow ₹1,000 for a year at 10%, you give back the ₹1,000 plus ₹100 rent. That ₹100 is the interest.

When you saveYou put ₹1,000 in a bank. The bank uses your money to give loans to others and pays you, say, ₹70 a year for it. You are the lender, so you earn interest.
When you borrowYour family takes a loan to buy a scooter. They pay back the loan plus interest every month. Now they are the borrower, so they pay interest.
Why should anyone pay rent for money?Because ₹100 today is more useful than ₹100 next year. Prices rise (remember Topic 01, inflation), and the lender gives up the chance to use that money now and takes a risk that it may not come back.

3How does a bank make money?

A bank is a middle-man for money. It pays savers a lower rate and charges borrowers a higher rate. The difference pays staff salaries, rent and the cost of loans that are never repaid, and what is left is the bank's profit.

Saver getsabout 6–7% a year on a fixed deposit
Borrower paysabout 8–10% on a home loan, more on a personal loan, and much more on a credit card
The gapis called the spread. It is how the bank earns its living.

4Who decides the interest rate?

Each bank sets its own rates, but they all follow one main rate: the repo rate, which is set by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). The repo rate is the interest the RBI charges banks when they borrow from it for a short time.

Think of it as the price of a key ingredient. If the cost of milk goes up, every tea stall raises its price. When the RBI raises the repo rate, banks raise the interest they charge on loans, and slowly on deposits too. In October 2026 the RBI raised the repo rate from 5.25% to 5.50%.

5Why does the RBI move rates up or down?

⬆ Raise rates — to cool pricesWhen prices rise too fast, the RBI makes borrowing costlier. People buy fewer cars and houses on loan, shops sell a little less, and price rises slow down. This is why the RBI raised rates when inflation was climbing in 2026.
⬇ Cut rates — to warm up the economyWhen business is slow and jobs are few, the RBI makes loans cheaper. Families and factories borrow and spend more, and the economy speeds up.
🎚️ Like a regulator on a fanTurning it up or down is easy. The hard part is that the effect takes many months to show, so the RBI has to guess where the economy is heading, not just where it is today.

6Interest in your life

  • Piggy bank vs bank account: money in a piggy bank earns nothing. In a savings account it earns a little interest, and the bank keeps it safe.
  • Start early: because of compounding, ₹1,000 saved at age 10 grows much more by age 40 than ₹1,000 saved at age 30. Time is the secret ingredient.
  • Borrow carefully: compounding also works against you on debt. An unpaid credit-card bill can grow very fast.
  • Education loans: many students borrow for college and repay after they get a job. Interest is the cost of getting the education earlier.

2From RBI's decision to your EMI: transmission

1 · RBI moves repoThe Governor announces the decision; overnight money-market rates move the same day.
2 · Bond yields and T-bills reactOften before the decision, because markets price in what they expect.
3 · Repo-linked loans resetAt the next reset date, within three months. The bank raises your EMI, or more often keeps it the same and stretches the tenure.
4 · MCLR loans and deposits followOver several months, as banks' cost of funds changes.
5 · The economy respondsBorrowing, spending and investment slow or speed up, and the effect on inflation shows up over the following year or so.

3Loans: the EMI calculator, and what a rate hike does

EMI = P · r · (1+r)^n / ((1+r)^n − 1) r = annual rate ÷ 12, n = months
  • Early EMIs are mostly interest. The chart shows how each year's payments split between interest and principal. Prepaying in the early years saves the most interest.
  • When rates rise, banks usually extend the tenure instead of raising the EMI. The calculator shows how many extra months that adds; if the tenure cannot stretch far enough, the EMI has to rise.
  • Credit cards often charge around 3–3.75% a month, which works out to well over 40% a year once compounded. Revolving a card balance is the most expensive borrowing most people ever do.

4Savings: getting the interest right

  • Compounding frequency matters. 7% compounded quarterly gives an effective annual yield of 7.19%. Compare deposits on their effective annual yield, not on the headline rate.
  • Real return = nominal − inflation − tax drag. With inflation at 6% (Topic 01), a 7% FD taxed at 30% loses purchasing power.
  • Lock-in vs flexibility. When rates are rising, short deposits let you reinvest at higher rates later. When rates are near a peak, locking in a longer tenure keeps the higher rate. A ladder (deposits maturing in different years) spreads this timing risk.
  • Small savings schemes (PPF, SCSS) pay administered rates and offer tax benefits, so they can beat bank FDs after tax even at similar headline rates.

5Rates and markets: bonds, shares, gold and the rupee

Bonds — the see-sawWhen interest rates rise, the prices of existing bonds fall, because new bonds pay more. Long-term bonds and long-duration debt funds fall the most. When rates fall, they gain.
SharesHigher rates make borrowing costlier for companies and make safe deposits more attractive than risky shares, so markets often weaken when rate hikes are expected. Banks may benefit if lending rates rise faster than deposit rates.
GoldGold pays no interest, so its appeal falls when real interest rates rise and grows when real rates are low or negative.
The rupeeHigher Indian rates relative to US rates attract foreign investors and support the rupee. When the Fed raises rates, money tends to flow out of India.

6What interest rates do to the country

Inflation controlRates are the RBI's main tool for keeping inflation near 4%. The October 2026 hike responded to inflation that was rising and spreading beyond food.
Growth and jobsHigher rates slow housing, vehicles and business investment. The RBI balances this against inflation; it still projects FY 2026–27 growth at about 7.1%.
Government borrowingInterest on public debt is one of the largest items in the Union Budget. Higher yields raise the cost of every new government bond.
Savers vs borrowersRising rates help retirees who live on interest income and hurt indebted households and firms. Falling rates do the reverse. Every rate decision redistributes money between groups.
Financial stabilityRates held too low for too long can inflate asset bubbles. Rates raised too fast can expose weak banks and borrowers.

7The global picture

  • The Fed sets the world's base rate. The dollar dominates trade and finance, so US rates affect borrowing costs everywhere. In September 2026 the Fed raised its target range to 3.75–4.00%, its first hike since 2023, then held in October; two members wanted another hike.
  • Long-term US yields are high. The US 10-year Treasury yield was around 5% in late October 2026. That raises mortgage costs in the US and pulls global capital towards dollar assets.
  • Spillovers to India. When the gap between Indian and US rates narrows, foreign portfolio money can leave and the rupee weakens. One reason the RBI gave for its hike was to support the rupee and limit outflows.
  • History in brief. US rates reached about 20% in 1980–81 under Paul Volcker to break 1970s inflation. After 2008 they sat near zero for years, and Europe and Japan even tried negative rates. In 2022–23, rates rose at the fastest pace in four decades.

8Sensible habits when rates move

  • Know your loan's benchmark (repo-linked or MCLR) and its reset date.
  • After a rate hike, check whether your tenure has been quietly extended. Asking the bank to raise the EMI instead can save a lot of interest.
  • Clear credit-card balances first; no investment reliably earns 40% a year.
  • Compare deposits on effective yield after tax, and stagger maturities.
  • Hold debt funds whose duration matches your time horizon.

9Check yourself

Level 3 · Review for experts

A refresher on rate theory, the term structure, bond mathematics, the operating framework and the international transmission of rates.

1Definitions and conventions

Fisher (exact): (1 + i) = (1 + r)(1 + π^e) → i ≈ r + π^e Effective annual: EAR = (1 + i/m)^m − 1 continuous: EAR = e^i − 1 Discount factor: d(t) = 1 / (1 + y_t)^t present value: PV = Σ CF_t · d(t)
  • Ex-ante vs ex-post real rate. The ex-post real repo rate today is about 5.50 − 6.09 ≈ −0.6%. The relevant policy measure is ex-ante: repo minus expected inflation over the next year. If inflation is expected to fall back towards 4–5%, the ex-ante real rate is positive.
  • Quoting conventions. Indian G-secs pay half-yearly coupons and are quoted as semi-annual yields; T-bills are quoted on a discount basis; money-market rates use act/365. Mixing conventions is a common source of small but real errors.

2Theories of the interest rate

TheoryCore ideaAssociated with
Time preference / productivityInterest reconciles impatience with the productivity of capital. In the Ramsey rule, r = ρ + θg.Böhm-Bawerk, Fisher, Ramsey
Loanable fundsThe real rate clears saving and investmentClassical economists, Wicksell
Liquidity preferenceThe rate is the price of giving up liquidity; it is set in the money marketKeynes
Natural rate (r*)The real rate consistent with output at potential and stable inflation. Policy is tight if r > r*.Wicksell; Laubach–Williams; Holston–Laubach–Williams

Estimates of r* fell steadily from the 1990s to 2020 in advanced economies, reflecting demographics, a global saving glut, lower productivity growth and demand for safe assets. Whether r* has risen since the pandemic, through fiscal deficits, investment in the energy transition and AI capital spending, is a live debate. A US 10-year yield near 5% suggests markets think it has. RBI research has put India's r* in a range of roughly 1–2% in real terms, with wide uncertainty bands.

3Term structure and bond mathematics

Expectations hypothesis + term premium: y_n ≈ (1/n) Σ E_t[i_{t+k}] + TP_n Modified duration: D_mod = D_mac / (1 + y/m) ΔP/P ≈ −D_mod·Δy + ½·C·(Δy)²
  • Shape. A normal upward slope reflects a positive term premium. An inverted curve, with short rates above long ones, signals expected rate cuts and has preceded most US recessions. It is a less reliable signal in India, where the curve is shaped heavily by government supply and liquidity.
  • Term premium. It turned negative in the QE era and has rebuilt since 2022 as fiscal supply grew and inflation became more uncertain.

4India's operating framework

  • The corridor. SDF (floor, 5.25%), repo (5.50%) and MSF/Bank Rate (ceiling, 5.75%): a symmetric ±25 bp corridor. The SDF, introduced in 2022, is uncollateralised, which frees the RBI from needing government securities to absorb surplus liquidity.
  • Operating target: the Weighted Average Call Rate (WACR). Liquidity is managed with variable-rate repo and reverse-repo auctions, open market operations, FX swaps and changes to the CRR, so that the WACR stays close to the repo.
  • Liquidity versus the rate. A large liquidity surplus can push overnight rates towards the SDF, an effective easing even when the repo is unchanged. Liquidity conditions and the stance therefore need to be read together.
  • Stance language. "Calibrated tightening" was last used in October 2018, when no further hike actually followed. The 2026 decision on the stance passed 4–2, while the hike itself was unanimous.

The EBLR regime moved much of the burden of transmission onto borrowers' EMIs, which made transmission faster but larger for households. Asymmetric deposit repricing compresses bank margins in easing cycles and widens them in tightening cycles.

6Beyond the policy rate

  • Effective lower bound. Negative rates were used by the ECB (2014–2022), the BoJ (2016–2024), the SNB, Sweden's Riksbank and Denmark. The evidence shows modest stimulus, with side-effects on bank profitability.
  • Quantitative easing and tightening work mainly through the term premium and signalling. With large balance sheets, central banks control short rates by paying interest on reserves (the Fed's IORB is 3.90% from 29 October 2026) rather than through scarce reserves.
  • Forward guidance moves the expected path, and therefore long yields, without changing the policy rate.
  • Macroprudential tools (risk weights, loan-to-value caps) can target credit booms more precisely than the policy rate.

7International dimension

Uncovered interest parity: i − i* ≈ E[Δs] (+ risk premium) Covered interest parity: F/S = (1 + i)/(1 + i*)
  • The trilemma, or a dilemma. With an open capital account, monetary independence requires a flexible exchange rate. The Rey "global financial cycle" view argues that US monetary policy drives global risk appetite regardless of the exchange-rate regime, which reduces the trilemma to a dilemma.
  • The 2026 configuration. Fed tightening (3.75–4.00%) with US 10-year yields around 5% narrows the India–US differential. The RBI's October hike partly aimed to cushion portfolio outflows and rupee pressure. Carry-trade flows are sensitive both to this gap and to expected currency volatility.
  • Policy divergence and FX reserves. Emerging-market central banks combine rate moves with FX intervention and reserves as a buffer; the IMF's Integrated Policy Framework formalises this mix.

8Review checklist: open questions

  1. Has r* risen after the pandemic, and is the rise global (through fiscal supply and the term premium) or specific to each economy?
  2. Should an inflation-targeting central bank react to a supply-led price rise with an ex-post negative real rate, or look through it?
  3. Does the EBLR regime improve welfare, given that it shifts interest-rate risk to households?
  4. How large is the "liquidity channel" in India relative to the repo signal?
  5. Do small savings rates impede deposit transmission, and should they be formula-linked to G-sec yields more strictly?
  6. How should emerging markets respond when the Fed tightens during a domestic supply shock?
