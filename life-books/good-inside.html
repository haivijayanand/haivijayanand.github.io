Good Inside

Becky Kennedy · first published 2022 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Children and parents are good at their core, so hard behaviour is a sign of a skill still being learned, and parents help most by leading with calm, firm boundaries while staying warmly connected.

Summary

Becky Kennedy is a clinical psychologist and mother of three. Her book starts from one belief: every child is good inside, and so is every parent. When a child hits, lies or screams, that behaviour does not reveal who they are. It shows a feeling they cannot yet manage or a skill they have not yet built.

This shift changes the parent's job. Instead of asking how to stop a behaviour fast, we ask what is going on underneath it. Kennedy suggests looking for the most generous reading of a child's actions. A child who grabs a toy may be tired or anxious, not selfish. Seeing that does not excuse the behaviour, but it lets us respond with help rather than shame.

Kennedy pairs warmth with firmness. She describes the parent as a sturdy leader who stays calm, decides and holds limits even when a child is upset. A boundary, in her view, is something the parent does, not something the child must do. Rather than demanding a child stop throwing blocks, a parent can calmly move the blocks away.

Feelings are welcome, even when actions are not. She often notes that two things can be true at once: a child can be sad about bedtime, and bedtime still happens. Naming and allowing big emotions helps children learn to tolerate frustration and disappointment, which matters more for their future than being happy all the time.

Repair is another central idea. Every parent yells or loses patience sometimes. What counts is coming back afterwards, owning our part and reconnecting. Kennedy argues it is never too late to repair, and that doing so teaches children that relationships can survive hard moments.

She also offers guidance for deeply feeling children, whose reactions are especially intense and who need extra steadiness. Throughout, she reminds parents to treat themselves with the same kindness, since a calm parent grows from self-understanding, not perfection.

317 words

One-line takeaway: This week, the next time your child melts down, hold a calm boundary on what you will do, name the feeling you see, and later return for a short repair talk about what happened.

  1. What is the core belief at the heart of Good Inside?

    Answer C. Kennedy builds the whole book on the idea that both children and parents are fundamentally good inside.

  2. According to Kennedy, what does a child's difficult behaviour usually signal?

    Answer A. She treats hard behaviour as a window into an unmet need or missing skill, not a verdict on the child.

  3. What does the 'most generous interpretation' of a child's behaviour mean?

    Answer D. It means seeking a compassionate explanation, such as tiredness or worry, before reacting.

  4. How does Kennedy describe the parent's role?

    Answer B. She asks parents to be sturdy leaders who stay calm, decide and hold limits with warmth.

  5. In Kennedy's view, a boundary is best understood as what?

    Answer A. She frames boundaries as the parent's own actions, like moving thrown blocks out of reach.

  6. What does the idea that 'two things can be true' help parents do?

    Answer C. A child can be upset about bedtime and bedtime can still happen; both are honoured.

  7. Why does Kennedy value helping children tolerate frustration?

    Answer D. She sees the ability to cope with hard feelings as more important than being happy all the time.

  8. What should a parent do after yelling or losing patience?

    Answer B. Repair, according to Kennedy, shows children that relationships can survive difficult moments.

  9. What does Kennedy say about when repair can happen?

    Answer C. She stresses that repair is possible at any time, even long after a rupture.

  10. What does Kennedy encourage parents to offer themselves?

    Answer A. She reminds parents that they too are good inside and that calm grows from self-understanding.
