The Book of Joy

Dalai Lama, Desmond Tutu and Douglas Abrams · first published 2016 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Lasting joy does not depend on easy circumstances; it grows from how we train our minds and open our hearts, even in the middle of suffering.

Summary

In 2015 the Dalai Lama and Archbishop Desmond Tutu, two old friends who had each lived through exile, injustice and great hardship, spent a week together in Dharamsala, India, to mark the Dalai Lama's eightieth birthday. Writer Douglas Abrams joined them, asked questions, and shaped their conversations into this book, mixing their answers with scenes of their teasing, laughter and shared meals.

Their starting point is that joy is deeper than happiness. Happiness often rises and falls with events, while joy can remain steady even when life is hard. Both men insist that suffering cannot be avoided, but that our response to it is something we can practise and improve.

The first part of the book looks honestly at the things that block joy: fear and worry, anger and frustration, sadness and grief, loneliness, envy, illness and the fear of death. Rather than pretending these feelings away, the two teachers suggest noticing them early, understanding where they come from, and not letting them take over.

The heart of the book is a set of eight pillars of joy. Four belong to the mind: perspective, which means stepping back to see the bigger picture; humility; humour, especially laughing at ourselves; and acceptance of what we cannot change. Four belong to the heart: forgiveness, gratitude, compassion and generosity.

A thread running through every chapter is that turning toward other people is the surest path to joy. Thinking constantly about ourselves tends to shrink our world and deepen our worries, while caring for others widens it.

The book closes with simple daily practices, such as quiet reflection, counting blessings, and small acts of kindness, so that readers can build these habits into ordinary family life.

282 words

One-line takeaway: This week, end each day by having everyone at home name three things they were grateful for and one kind thing they did or saw someone else do.
