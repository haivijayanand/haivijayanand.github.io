The recipe that turns A v = λ v into something you can solve: rewrite it as (A − λI)v = 0, demand a non-zero solution, and you get det(A − λI) = 0 — a polynomial whose roots are the eigenvalues. Then each eigenvector is found by solving a small set of equations.
  • Derive the characteristic equation det(A − λI) = 0
  • Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors of 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 matrices by hand
  • Use trace and determinant as instant checks
  • Recognise complex, repeated and defective cases
    •

    1From A v = λ v to a polynomial

    Move everything to one side. Because λv = λIv (I is the identity matrix):

    A v − λ I v = 0 ⟹ (A − λI) v = 0

    This is a homogeneous linear system. It always has the boring solution v = 0. It has a non-zero solution only if the matrix A − λI is singular — it squashes some direction to zero — which happens exactly when its determinant vanishes:

    Characteristic equation
    det(A − λI) = 0
    For an n × n matrix the left side is a polynomial of degree n in λ, the characteristic polynomial. It has exactly n roots (counting repeats and complex ones), so an n × n matrix has n eigenvalues.

    For a 2 × 2 matrix A = [a b; c d] it is short enough to remember:

    det [a − λ b ; c d − λ] = (a − λ)(d − λ) − bc = λ² − (a + d)λ + (ad − bc) = λ² − (trace)·λ + det

    So λ = trace/2 ± √(trace²/4 − det). The sign of the discriminant trace²/4 − det decides everything: positive → two real eigenvalues, zero → a repeated one, negative → a complex pair.

    2Worked examples, by hand

    Example 1 — A = [4 1; 2 3]trace = 7, det = 12 − 2 = 10 λ² − 7λ + 10 = 0 → (λ − 5)(λ − 2) = 0 → λ₁ = 5, λ₂ = 2 λ = 5: A − 5I = [−1 1; 2 −2] row 1: −v₁ + v₂ = 0 → v = (1, 1) λ = 2: A − 2I = [ 2 1; 2 1] row 1: 2v₁ + v₂ = 0 → v = (1, −2) Check: A(1, −2) = (4 − 2, 2 − 6) = (2, −4) = 2·(1, −2) ✓

    Notice that the two rows of A − λI are always multiples of each other when λ is an eigenvalue — that is what "singular" means — so one row is enough to read off the eigenvector.

    Example 2 — three masses on springs: A = [2 −1 0; −1 2 −1; 0 −1 2]det(A − λI) = (2 − λ)[(2 − λ)² − 1] − (2 − λ) = (2 − λ)[(2 − λ)² − 2] Roots: λ = 2, and (2 − λ)² = 2 → λ = 2 ∓ √2 λ₁ = 2 − √2 = 0.586 v₁ = (1, √2, 1) all masses in step λ₂ = 2 v₂ = (1, 0, −1) ends opposite, middle still λ₃ = 2 + √2 = 3.414 v₃ = (1, −√2, 1) neighbours opposite Check: 0.586 + 2 + 3.414 = 6 = trace ✓; 0.586 × 2 × 3.414 = 4 = det ✓

    This is the stiffness matrix of three equal masses held between two walls by four equal springs (in units of k). Its eigenvectors are the three ways the chain likes to vibrate. You will animate them on Day 5.

    Example 3 — a spring–mass–damper in state form: A = [0 1; −4 −1](x′ = v, v′ = −4x − v, i.e. m = 1, c = 1, k = 4) trace = −1, det = 4: λ² + λ + 4 = 0 λ = −0.5 ± √(0.25 − 4) = −0.5 ± 1.936 i Real part −0.5 → the motion decays as e^(−0.5t) Imaginary part 1.936 → it oscillates at 1.936 rad/s (period 3.25 s)

    Complex eigenvalues are not a nuisance — they are how a matrix says "oscillate". Day 6 makes this the centre of stability analysis.

    3Lab: the eigen-calculator

    Pick an example or type your own 2 × 2, 3 × 3 or 4 × 4 matrix. The lab builds the characteristic polynomial, finds its roots, computes each real eigenvector and checks |Av − λv|. The plot shows p(λ): its zero crossings are the real eigenvalues. A complex pair shows up as a curve that dips towards zero but never crosses.

    4Shortcuts and special cases

    • Triangular matrices (zeros below or above the diagonal): the eigenvalues are just the diagonal entries, because det(A − λI) is the product of (aᵢᵢ − λ).
    • Trace and determinant: Σλ = trace, Πλ = det, for any size. A zero eigenvalue ⇔ det = 0 ⇔ A cannot be inverted.
    • Powers and inverses: if Av = λv then A²v = λ²v, Aᵏv = λᵏv and A⁻¹v = (1/λ)v. Same eigenvectors, eigenvalues transformed. Shifting also works: (A − σI)v = (λ − σ)v. Day 10's algorithms are built on these three facts.
    • Real matrices have complex eigenvalues in conjugate pairs a ± bi.
    • Symmetric matrices (A = Aᵀ) never have complex eigenvalues — the star of Day 4.
    Repeated eigenvalues can be defective. A = [3 1; 0 3] has λ = 3 twice (algebraic multiplicity 2), but A − 3I = [0 1; 0 0] has only one independent null vector, (1, 0) (geometric multiplicity 1). Not enough eigenvectors to make a basis. Physically this is the borderline "critically damped" case: solutions look like t·e^(λt) instead of pure exponentials. By contrast 3I = [3 0; 0 3] also has λ = 3 twice, but every vector is an eigenvector.
    Why the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial are trace and determinant

    Factor the polynomial by its roots: det(λI − A) = (λ − λ₁)(λ − λ₂)…(λ − λₙ). Expanding, the λⁿ⁻¹ coefficient is −(λ₁ + … + λₙ) and the constant term is (−1)ⁿλ₁λ₂…λₙ. Now expand the same determinant directly: the only term that reaches λⁿ⁻¹ comes from the diagonal product (λ − a₁₁)…(λ − aₙₙ), giving −(a₁₁ + … + aₙₙ) = −trace. Setting λ = 0 gives det(−A) = (−1)ⁿ det A. Matching the two expansions proves Σλ = trace and Πλ = det. The middle coefficient of a 3 × 3 is the sum of the three 2 × 2 principal minors — that is the "10" in Example 2: (4 − 1) + (4 − 0) + (4 − 1).

    A practical warningNever find eigenvalues of a big matrix by building its characteristic polynomial: the roots of a high-degree polynomial are hypersensitive to rounding in its coefficients. Software (LAPACK, NumPy, MATLAB, ANSYS, NASTRAN) works on the matrix directly with the QR or Lanczos algorithms (Day 10). The polynomial is for understanding and for 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 hand work.
