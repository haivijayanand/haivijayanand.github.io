1. The problem

A sheet of paper is 0.1 mm thick. Every fold in half doubles the thickness. How many folds would make the stack reach the Moon, 384,400 km away?

And a second question: why can nobody fold an ordinary sheet more than 7 or 8 times?

2. The answer

42 folds
0.1 mm × 242 ≈ 440,000 km — past the Moon. 41 folds reach only 220,000 km.
≈ 12 folds
is the record (Britney Gallivan, 2002). Each fold uses up paper on the curved edge, and that loss grows like 4n.

3. Working it out

After n folds the thickness is t · 2n. To reach a distance D:

n = log2(D / t) = log2(384,400,000,000 mm / 0.1 mm) ≈ 41.8 → 42 folds

Why the real limit is so low

At each fold the paper wraps round a curve whose radius is the thickness of the stack, and that curved paper is "lost" from the flat length. As a high-school student, Britney Gallivan worked out the minimum length of a strip needed for n folds in one direction:

L = (πt / 6) · (2n + 4) · (2n − 1)

That grows like 4n: every extra fold needs about four times as much paper. With 0.1 mm paper, 12 folds need a strip about 880 m long; she did it with a roll of thin tissue over 1 km long. Forty-two folds would need a strip about 1018 km long — over 100,000 light-years, the width of the galaxy.

5. Fine print

  • Folding in alternate directions (the usual way) gives a slightly different formula but the same 4n growth.
  • MythBusters reached 11 folds in 2007 with a sheet the size of a football pitch, helped by a steamroller.
  • The same exponential growth is why binary search finds one name among a billion in about 30 steps — it is paper folding run backwards.
