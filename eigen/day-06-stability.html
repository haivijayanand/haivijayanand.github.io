Stability of Dynamic Systems

For any linear system x′ = A x the solution is a sum of terms e^(λt)·v. So the eigenvalues decide everything: negative real parts decay, positive ones blow up, imaginary parts oscillate. One rule answers "is it stable?" for control loops, aircraft, rotors, circuits and ecosystems.

1Why eigenvalues decide stability

Try x(t) = e^(λt)v in x′ = Ax: λe^(λt)v = Ae^(λt)v, i.e. Av = λv. So each eigenpair gives one solution, and the general solution is

x(t) = c₁ e^(λ₁t) v₁ + c₂ e^(λ₂t) v₂ + … + cₙ e^(λₙt) vₙ

With λ = σ + iω, e^(λt) = e^(σt)(cos ωt + i sin ωt). The real part σ sets growth or decay, the imaginary part ω sets oscillation.

Stability rule. The equilibrium x = 0 of x′ = Ax is asymptotically stable if and only if every eigenvalue of A has a negative real part. One eigenvalue in the right half-plane is enough for instability. For discrete steps xₖ₊₁ = Axₖ (Day 3) the rule is |λ| < 1 instead.

For a 2 × 2 system the eigenvalues are trace/2 ± √(trace²/4 − det), so the whole zoo of behaviours fits on one map: the trace–determinant plane. Stable ⇔ trace < 0 and det > 0.

3Reading engineering numbers from λ

For an oscillator m x″ + c x′ + k x = 0 in state form x′ = [0 1; −k/m −c/m]x, the eigenvalues are

λ = −ζωₙ ± iωₙ√(1 − ζ²) ωₙ = √(k/m) = √det, ζ = c/(2√(km)) = −trace/(2ωₙ)
Example 1 — m = 1 kg, c = 1 N·s/m, k = 4 N/m (the lab's default)trace = −1, det = 4 λ = −0.5 ± 1.936 i ωₙ = √4 = 2 rad/s, ζ = 1/(2·2) = 0.25, damped frequency ω_d = 1.936 rad/s envelope e^(−0.5t): time constant 2 s, amplitude halves every 1.39 s logarithmic decrement δ = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²) = 1.62 → each peak is e^(−1.62) = 20 % of the previous

Aircraft engineers describe longitudinal motion by two eigenvalue pairs of the 4 × 4 flight-dynamics matrix: the short-period mode (typically λ ≈ −2 ± 2.5i for a light aircraft: a quick, well-damped pitch bob of about 2.5 s period) and the phugoid (λ ≈ −0.01 ± 0.15i: a slow, lightly damped exchange of height and speed with a period of about 40 s). Handling-quality standards are written as limits on these eigenvalues' damping ratios and frequencies.

4Feedback moves eigenvalues

A controller measures the state and pushes back: u = −Kx. Then x′ = Ax + Bu = (A − BK)x. The closed-loop eigenvalues — the poles — are those of A − BK, and choosing K to put them where you want is called pole placement.

Example 2 — balancing an inverted pendulum (linearised, g/L = 2 s⁻²)θ″ = 2θ + u state x = (θ, θ′) Open loop: A = [0 1; 2 0], λ = ±1.414 → a saddle: it falls over, e-folding in 0.71 s Feedback u = −k₁θ − k₂θ′: A − BK = [0 1; 2 − k₁ −k₂] Characteristic equation: λ² + k₂λ + (k₁ − 2) = 0 Want both poles at −2: (λ + 2)² = λ² + 4λ + 4 → k₂ = 4, k₁ = 6 Closed loop: critically damped, settles (within 2 %) in about 3 s. Put the poles at −4 instead and k₁ = 18, k₂ = 8: faster, but needs larger motor torque and amplifies sensor noise — the classic trade-off.
Where you meet itAutopilots and fly-by-wire laws, Segways and rockets (inverted pendulums), servo and dynamometer speed loops, power-system stability studies (eigenvalues of grid models with hundreds of generators), chatter in machining, brake squeal and clutch judder, and the rotor-dynamics stability check of fluid-film bearings: cross-coupled bearing stiffness can push a rotor's whirl eigenvalue into the right half-plane — oil whip.

5Nonlinear systems: linearise, then take eigenvalues

Most real systems are nonlinear, x′ = f(x). Near an equilibrium x*, small deviations obey x′ ≈ J·(x − x*), where J is the Jacobian matrix of partial derivatives ∂fᵢ/∂xⱼ at x*. The eigenvalues of J tell you whether small disturbances die out (Hartman–Grobman theorem, valid unless an eigenvalue sits exactly on the imaginary axis).

Example 3 — fish and sharks (predator–prey with crowding), linearised at coexistenceJ = [−0.1 −0.5 ; 0.2 0] (per month) trace = −0.1, det = 0.1 λ = −0.05 ± 0.312 i → stable spiral: after a disturbance, fish and sharks oscillate with period 2π/0.312 ≈ 20 months, each cycle smaller by e^(−0.05 × 20) ≈ 0.37, settling back to coexistence.
Why stability needs trace < 0 and det > 0 (and the Routh–Hurwitz idea)

For 2 × 2: det = λ₁λ₂ and trace = λ₁ + λ₂. If both λ are real and negative, their product is positive and sum negative. If they are a complex pair σ ± iω, det = σ² + ω² > 0 always and trace = 2σ, so trace < 0 ⇔ σ < 0. Conversely det < 0 forces opposite-sign real roots (a saddle). For larger systems the Routh–Hurwitz criterion tests the characteristic-polynomial coefficients without solving for the roots — historically important, now mostly replaced by computing the eigenvalues directly.
