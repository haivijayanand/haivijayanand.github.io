The Seven Pillars of Business

Every business, however small, must do seven jobs well. Weakness in any one can bring the whole building down. This chapter introduces the seven; the next seven chapters take them one at a time. Pick your level.

Level 1 · For school students

Think of a business as a building. It stands on seven pillars. If one breaks, the roof wobbles.

1The seven pillars

1 · Strategy & leadershipDeciding where to go and leading people there.
2 · MarketingMaking the right people know, want and trust what you sell.
3 · SalesTurning interested people into paying customers.
4 · OperationsMaking the product or giving the service, well and on time.
5 · FinanceLooking after money: costs, prices, cash, profit.
6 · PeopleHiring, training and keeping good workers.
7 · Customer experienceMaking customers happy so they come back.

Level 2 · For working people

What each pillar does, how they hand work to each other, and a quick health check for your business.

1How the pillars hand work to each other

Strategy chooses the target. Marketing brings in interested prospects. Sales converts them. Operations delivers. Customer experience keeps them and brings referrals, which feeds marketing again. Finance and People run underneath everything: no cash or no capable people, and every pillar weakens.

PillarKey questionOne measure to watch
Strategy & leadershipWhere do we play and how do we win?Progress on 3–5 annual goals
MarketingDo the right people know and want us?Qualified leads per month; cost per lead
SalesDo we turn interest into orders?Conversion rate; average order value
OperationsDo we deliver right, first time, on time?On-time-in-full %; defect rate
FinanceDo we earn profit and keep enough cash?Gross margin; cash runway; receivable days
PeopleDo we have the right people, motivated?Attrition; vacancies; training hours
Customer experienceDo customers come back and recommend us?Repeat rate; NPS; complaints resolved

The "seven pillars" are a teaching taxonomy, not a theory. Their value is completeness: small firms usually over-invest in the pillar the founder knows (often operations or sales) and neglect finance and people until a crisis.

2The weakest pillar sets the pace

Goldratt's theory of constraints: a system's throughput equals the capacity of its bottleneck. Improving a non-bottleneck creates only inventory or idle time. Model a month of a small B2B firm:

The five focusing steps: identify the constraint, exploit it (no idle bottleneck time), subordinate everything else to it, elevate it (invest), and repeat, because the constraint moves.
