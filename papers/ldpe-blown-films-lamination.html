LDPE-Based Blown Films for Lamination: Historical Emergence, Process Windows, Additives and Current Trends

Online version: https://haivijayanand.github.io/papers/ldpe-blown-films-lamination.html

Abstract

LDPE-based blown film remains the workhorse sealant web of laminated flexible packaging, because long-chain branching gives it the melt strength, bubble stability and clarity that lamination converters need. Over nine decades the sealant has moved from monolayer LDPE at 80–100 µm to 5–9 layer co-extrusions of LDPE, LLDPE and metallocene PE at 30–60 µm, with sealing starting 20–30 °C lower and dart impact several times higher. This paper traces that history, sets out the process window (blow-up ratio, draw-down, frost line and the temperature at each station of the line), summarises the property requirements of lamination-grade film, and gives a separate review of additives: metallocene resins, fillers, impact modifiers, slip, antiblock, processing aids and stabilisers. It closes with current trends: all-PE recyclable laminates, recycled content and PFAS-free processing aids. Numerical ranges are typical industry values for guidance; resin supplier data sheets and line trials govern any specific set-up.

Keywords: blown film extrusion, LDPE, lamination, blow-up ratio, metallocene polyethylene, polymer additives, flexible packaging, recyclability

1. Introduction

In a laminate, the PE film is the inside web: it seals the pack, carries the product contact and provides most of the toughness, while the outer web (BOPET, BOPP, BOPA, paper or aluminium foil) carries print, stiffness and barrier. Typical structures are PET 12 µm // PE 40–100 µm for snacks, spices and liquids, PET // Al foil // PE for high barrier, and BOPP // PE or MDO-PE // PE for all-PE recyclable packs.

The sealant film must therefore do four jobs at once:

  • Bond — accept adhesive or extrudate with a stable surface energy (≥ 40 dyn/cm at lamination) and no migrating additives at the interface.
  • Seal — start sealing at low temperature, with high hot tack and seal strength through contamination.
  • Survive — resist puncture, flex-cracking and drop impact in the filled pack.
  • Run — have controlled COF, flatness, gauge uniformity and low gels on the laminator and the form-fill-seal (FFS) machine.

LDPE alone does the first and last well; modern film design adds LLDPE and metallocene PE to do the middle two.

2. Historical emergence

LDPE film went from a wartime curiosity to the default sealant in about 30 years [1], [2]; every later step has been about doing the same job with less material and lower sealing energy.

Period Development What it changed for lamination film
1933 ICI (Fawcett and Gibson) obtain polyethylene by high-pressure polymerisation of ethylene A tough, inert, heat-sealable polymer becomes possible
1939–1945 Commercial LDPE at ICI; used mainly for radar cable insulation Capacity built; post-war surplus looks for markets
Late 1940s–1950s Tubular blown film process adopted for LDPE Thin, wide, low-cost film; bags and liners
1950s–1960s Ziegler and Phillips catalysts give HDPE; extrusion coating and early adhesive lamination of cellophane, paper and foil PE becomes the heat-seal layer of laminates
1970s BOPP and BOPET films grow; polyurethane adhesive lamination spreads; gas-phase LLDPE (Unipol, 1977) Two-web PET//PE and BOPP//PE laminates take over; LLDPE adds toughness
1980s 3-layer co-extrusion; LDPE/LLDPE blends; C6 and C8 comonomers Sealant, core and treat-side layers tuned separately
1990s Single-site metallocene PE and plastomers (Exxon Exact 1991; Dow Insite/Affinity/Elite 1993–94); solventless adhesives Low seal initiation, high hot tack, downgauging
2000s 5–7 layer lines, internal bubble cooling, automatic gauge control; EVOH/PA barrier co-ex Faster lines, ±3–5 % gauge, barrier sealants
2010s 9–11 layer lines; machine-direction oriented (MDO) PE Stiff, printable PE replaces PET as outer web
2020s Design-for-recycling guidelines (CEFLEX, APR); EPR and recycled-content rules, including India's Plastic Waste Management Amendment Rules 2022; PFAS-free processing aids All-PE laminates, PCR in core layers, additive reformulation

The common thread is that LDPE never left the film. It moved from being the whole film to being the 10–40 % melt-strength component that keeps the bubble stable while LLDPE and metallocene PE carry the mechanical and sealing duty.

3. The blown film process and blow-up ratio

For lamination-grade LDPE-rich film, a blow-up ratio (BUR) of 2.0–3.0 with the frost line at 2–4 die diameters gives the best balance of MD/TD properties, flatness and bubble stability [2].

Resin is melted in one or more single-screw extruders, filtered, and fed through an adapter into an annular spiral-mandrel die. The emerging tube is inflated with air, cooled by an air ring (and on modern lines by internal bubble cooling, IBC), and solidifies at the frost line. It is then flattened in a collapsing frame, pulled by the primary nip, corona-treated on the bonding side, and wound. Orientation is set between the die lip and the frost line by two independent stretches: radial (blow-up) and axial (draw-down).

3.1 Key geometric ratios

The blow-up ratio, layflat width and draw-down ratio (DDR) are:

  • BUR = Dbubble / Ddie
  • Wlayflat = π · Ddie · BUR / 2 ≈ 1.571 · Ddie · BUR
  • DDR = hdie gap / (tfilm × BUR)

where D is diameter, h the die gap and t the film thickness. For example, a 250 mm die at BUR 2.5 gives a 625 mm bubble and a 982 mm layflat. With a 1.0 mm die gap and 50 µm film, DDR = 1000 / (50 × 2.5) = 8.

3.2 Typical process windows

Parameter LDPE monolayer LDPE/LLDPE/mPE co-ex sealant Effect of increasing it
Blow-up ratio 1.8–3.0 2.0–3.5 More TD orientation, higher TD tear and dart, lower MD tear; above about 3.5 the bubble wanders
Die gap (mm) 0.6–1.2 1.2–2.5 (LLDPE-rich) Less shear and melt fracture, but higher DDR and MD orientation
Draw-down ratio 5–20 10–40 More MD orientation, MD splittiness
Frost line height (× die dia.) 2–4 2–5 More time to relax: clearer, better-balanced film; lower output
Specific output (kg/h per cm die circumference) 0.8–1.5 1.2–2.5 with IBC Needs more cooling; raises gauge variation
Film thickness for lamination (µm) 40–120 30–100 —

Guidance for lamination webs:

  • Balance over strength. A BUR near 2.5 gives near-balanced MD/TD tear and shrink, which keeps the laminate flat and limits tunnelling and curl after curing.
  • Low BUR (< 1.8) gives MD-oriented, splitty film with high MD shrink; it can tunnel on the laminator.
  • LDPE content sets the ceiling. 10–30 % LDPE in an LLDPE/mPE blend is usually enough to hold a stable bubble at BUR 2.5–3.0.
  • Layflat is fixed by laminate width, so BUR and die size are chosen together; a die change is often cheaper than running at an unstable BUR.

4. Temperature profile at each station

LDPE lamination film is typically run with a rising barrel profile from about 160 °C to 200 °C and a melt temperature of 190–215 °C; LLDPE- and mPE-rich layers need 10–25 °C more.

Station LDPE (MFI 0.5–2), °C LLDPE / mLLDPE-rich layer, °C Purpose and notes
Feed throat (water-cooled) 30–50 30–50 Stops bridging and premature melting in the hopper
Barrel zone 1 (feed) 150–170 170–190 Low enough to convey solids; too hot causes surging
Barrel zone 2 (transition) 170–185 190–205 Main melting zone
Barrel zone 3 (metering) 180–195 200–215 Homogenise melt, build pressure
Barrel zones 4–5 185–200 205–220 Final melt conditioning
Screen changer / filter 190–205 205–220 80–150 mesh pack holds back gels and contamination
Adapter and melt pipe 190–205 205–225 Equal to or slightly above barrel exit
Die body and spiral zones 195–210 205–225 Uniform around the circumference; ±2 °C or better
Die lip 195–215 210–230 Hotter lips reduce sharkskin and die lines
Melt temperature (measured) 190–215 205–235 Higher gives clarity and fewer gels, but a weaker bubble
Air ring cooling air 10–20 10–20 Chilled air raises output; frost line moves down
IBC air (inlet) 15–25 15–25 Internal cooling adds 20–50 % output
Frost line (film) ≈ 95–110 ≈ 105–120 Crystallisation; orientation frozen here
Collapsing frame / primary nip (film) 40–60 40–60 Too hot causes wrinkles, blocking, inside-sticking
Corona treater (film) 30–50 30–50 Treat to 42–48 dyn/cm for adhesive lamination
Winder (film) < 35–40 < 35–40 Hot rolls block and lose dyne faster

Practical points for lamination quality:

  • Melt temperature drives clarity and gels. Too cold leaves unmelt gels and haze; too hot oxidises the melt, adds carbonised gels and odour, and consumes antioxidant.
  • Treat-side temperature matters. Corona treatment is most stable on film below 40 °C; dyne decays faster on warm, slip-loaded film.
  • Seasonal drift. In a hot shop (35–40 °C ambient), chilled air holds the frost line; set points that work in winter can give a higher, wandering frost line in summer.
  • Co-extrusion. Adjacent layers should differ by no more than about 20–30 °C at the die to avoid interfacial instability (zig-zag and wave patterns).

These figures are starting points; resin data sheets and a gel-count, dart and dyne trial set the final profile.

5. Lamination-grade film properties and their trend

The biggest shift since the 1980s is in sealing and toughness: seal initiation has dropped from about 110 °C to 75–95 °C and dart impact per micron has risen three- to five-fold, which allowed sealant gauge to fall by roughly half.

Values are representative ranges compiled from typical resin data and converter practice, not a single data set. Other properties trended alongside: gel counts fell with better filtration and cleaner resins, gauge variation fell with automatic die-bolt or air-ring profile control, and treat-side dyne retention improved as slip moved to non-migratory systems (Section 6).

6. Additives and property enhancements

In a lamination web every additive has two faces: what it does for the film, and what it does to the bond. The rule of thumb is to put functional additives only in the layer that needs them, and keep the treat side clean.

6.1 Metallocene PE (mLLDPE) and plastomers

Single-site catalysts give PE with a narrow molecular-weight distribution (Mw/Mn ≈ 2–3) and comonomer spread evenly across all chains. This removes the low-molecular-weight, comonomer-rich fraction that makes Ziegler LLDPE sticky and hazy, and the high-density fraction that raises its sealing temperature.

  • Grades: mLLDPE (0.912–0.920 g/cm³) for toughness; plastomers / POP (0.875–0.905 g/cm³) for very low seal initiation and hot tack.
  • Typical use: 20–60 % mLLDPE in core and sealant layers; 10–40 % plastomer in a 15–25 % sealant skin.
  • Gains: dart impact two to five times Ziegler LLDPE at the same gauge; puncture and flex-crack resistance; seal initiation down by 10–30 °C; wider hot-tack window; seal-through-contamination; 20–40 % downgauging.
  • Costs: low melt strength and high shear viscosity, giving higher motor load, melt fracture and a weaker bubble. The remedy is 10–30 % LDPE for melt strength plus a polymer processing aid (PPA). Plastomer-rich skins can raise COF and blocking, needing more slip and antiblock.

6.2 LDPE as a blend partner

LDPE is now often the additive rather than the base. 5–30 % LDPE in LLDPE or mLLDPE raises extensional viscosity and bubble stability, cuts haze through smoother surfaces, and lets the line run higher BUR and output. Above about 30–40 % LDPE, dart and tear begin to fall sharply.

6.3 Fillers and pigments

Filler Typical loading Benefit Penalty / lamination caution
Calcium carbonate (coated, 1–3 µm) masterbatch 5–20 % of the layer Cost, stiffness, faster cooling, dead-fold; base for breathable film when stretched Dart and tear drop; moisture in masterbatch causes voids and lacing (dry it); rougher surface
Titanium dioxide (white MB, 50–70 % TiO₂) 3–10 % MB Opacity for white sealant and light-sensitive products Die-lip build-up; abrasive; raises density
Talc 1–5 % Antiblock, stiffness, some nucleation Haze; can absorb some additives
Carbon black (black MB) 2–6 % MB UV and light barrier; black/white co-ex Food-contact grade required
Desiccant (CaO) masterbatch 1–3 % Removes moisture when running recycled or CaCO₃-rich feed Fixes moisture only, not contamination

Fillers belong in the core layer of a co-extrusion, with clean, unfilled skins on both sides so that the treat and seal surfaces stay smooth.

6.4 Impact resistance and toughness enhancers

  • Comonomer choice: C6 (hexene) and C8 (octene) LLDPE are markedly tougher than C4 (butene) at the same density.
  • mLLDPE and plastomers: the most effective dart and puncture enhancers (Section 6.1).
  • EVA (4–18 % VA): low-temperature toughness, flexibility and lower seal initiation; adds odour and blocking above about 9 % VA.
  • Ionomers: best seal-through-contamination and hot tack; used as thin sealant skins in meat, cheese and liquid packs.
  • Layer design: a stiff HDPE or MDPE core between tough mLLDPE layers gives both stiffness and impact; asymmetry must be balanced to avoid curl.

6.5 Surface and process additives

Slip is the most frequent cause of bond problems: it migrates into the adhesive and to the treat side, lowering bond strength and dyne, and COF rises after cure as slip moves into the PET side.

Additive Typical loading Function Lamination caution
Slip: erucamide (slow bloom), oleamide (fast bloom) 300–1500 ppm in sealant skin Lowers COF for FFS Use the minimum, keep it out of the treat layer, or use non-migratory slip (siloxane MB, high-MW amides)
Antiblock: natural/synthetic silica, talc, CaCO₃ 1000–4000 ppm Prevents roll blocking; supports slip Haze and roller abrasion; agglomerates read as gels
PPA: fluoropolymer, now PFAS-free alternatives 300–1000 ppm active Removes melt fracture (sharkskin) with mLLDPE; cuts die build-up and gels Needs conditioning; interacts with antiblock and TiO₂; for PFAS-free grades prefer silicone-free chemistries on laminated film and confirm dosing on trial
Antioxidants (hindered phenol + phosphite) 500–2000 ppm Protect melt at 200–230 °C; fewer carbonised gels Over-oxidised film can yellow and give odour
Antistatic (GMS, ethoxylated amines) 0.1–0.5 % Less dust pick-up and static at the winder Migrates like slip; generally avoided in lamination webs
Antifog (glycerol / sorbitan esters) 0.5–2 % Clear view on chilled produce Product side only; not in bonded layers
UV stabiliser (HALS) 0.1–1 % Outdoor and agricultural film life Rarely needed in indoor laminates
Nucleating agents / clarifiers 500–2000 ppm Stiffness, lower haze, faster crystallisation Shifts frost line; check seal window
Compatibilisers / odour absorbers 1–5 % Allow PCR in core layers Verify gels, odour and food-contact status

6.6 Other property enhancements by structure

  • Barrier: an EVOH or PA core with maleic-anhydride tie layers gives oxygen barrier inside a PE structure; EVOH below about 5 % of the structure keeps it recyclable in PE streams [3].
  • Stiffness and print face: HDPE skins or MDO-PE (draw ratio 4–7) give PET-like stiffness and dimensional stability for all-PE laminates.
  • Heat resistance: an HDPE or MDPE outer layer widens the sealing window and resists burn-through on fast jaws.
  • Downgauging: mLLDPE toughness with an HDPE core lets 30–40 µm webs replace 60–80 µm LDPE/LLDPE films.

7. Current trends

The PE sealant is becoming the whole laminate, built for recycling, with recycled content and without fluorinated processing aids.

  • All-PE laminates. MDO-PE or BOPE replaces PET or BOPP as the printed outer web, laminated to a PE sealant. A published ExxonMobil and W&H pouch uses a 25 µm MDO-PE // 120 µm PE laminate in place of PET12 // PE120 [5]. For the blown film line this means tighter gauge, flatness and MDO-ready resin in the outer web, while the LDPE-rich sealant stays largely unchanged.
  • Design-for-recycling limits. CEFLEX D4ACE guidance (September 2025) focuses on mono-PE, mono-PP and mixed polyolefin flexibles and sets limits such as adhesives and coatings at ≤ 5 % by weight [3]. APR asks for testing when a PE film carries EVOH or PA [4]. Barrier therefore moves to thin EVOH cores, AlOx/SiOx coatings or metallised MDO-PE.
  • PFAS-free processing aids. Tightening PFAS rules are pushing converters off fluoropolymer PPAs. Clariant launched silicone- and siloxane-free PFAS-free PPA grades in 2025, stating no effect on dyne, sealing or COF [6]; Ampacet offers a PFAS-free PPA masterbatch at 1–3 % that it states does not interfere with printing or lamination [7].
  • Recycled content. PCR and PIR PE go into core layers, with virgin, clean skins on the treat and seal sides; this needs better melt filtration, desiccant masterbatch and odour control. In India, EPR under the Plastic Waste Management Rules drives the same move.
  • More layers, thinner film. 7–11 layer dies let converters place mLLDPE, HDPE, LDPE and barrier exactly where needed and run 30–50 µm sealants.
  • Line automation. Gravimetric blending, automatic die or air-ring profile control, IBC and inline dyne/gel inspection are now standard on new lamination film lines.
  • Lamination process shift. Solventless polyurethane adhesives dominate; they are more sensitive to slip migration and dyne loss than solvent-based systems, which is pushing non-migratory slip and lower-slip sealant designs.

8. Troubleshooting lamination defects traced to the PE film

Defect in laminate Likely film-side cause Remedy at the blown film line
Low or falling bond strength Dyne below 40 at lamination; slip or antistat migrating to the treat side Raise treatment; slip only in seal skin; non-migratory slip; laminate within 2–4 weeks
COF rises after curing Erucamide migrating into adhesive and PET side Higher initial slip in seal skin, antiblock synergy, or non-migratory slip
Tunnelling or curl Unbalanced orientation (low BUR), gauge bands, asymmetric co-ex BUR 2.2–3.0, oscillating haul-off, auto-profile, balanced layer ratios
Gel bumps through PET or foil Unmelt, oxidised gels, filler agglomerates Finer screens, correct melt temperature, antioxidant, dry masterbatch
Wrinkles and baggy edges Gauge variation, hot nip, unstable bubble Steady frost line, chilled air, more LDPE for melt strength
Blocking on the roll Hot winding, low antiblock, plastomer-rich skin Cool film below 35–40 °C at the winder; raise antiblock
Melt fracture / sharkskin streaks mLLDPE at high shear, die gap too narrow PPA conditioning, wider die gap, higher die-lip temperature

9. Conclusion

LDPE remains essential in lamination film, now mainly as the 10–30 % melt-strength component that keeps a high-BUR bubble stable. A BUR of 2.0–3.0, a frost line at 2–4 die diameters and a 190–215 °C LDPE melt give balanced, flat, low-gel film suited to lamination. Metallocene PE and plastomers have cut seal initiation by 20–30 °C and allowed sealant gauge to roughly halve since the 1980s. Additives must be placed by layer, with slip, antistat and fillers kept away from the treated bonding surface. The next decade is about all-PE recyclable laminates, recycled content in core layers and PFAS-free processing, all of which put a premium on precise layer design and process control.

Process windows, temperature profiles and property ranges given here are typical values for guidance and should be confirmed against resin supplier data sheets and line trials.
