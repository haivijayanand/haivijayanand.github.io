Level 1 · For school students

Every country has its own money. The exchange rate is how we swap one for another.

1What is an exchange rate?

An exchange rate is the price of one country's money in another country's money. For example, 1 US dollar = ₹93.70 means you need ₹93.70 to buy one dollar.

India uses the rupee (₹), the USA the dollar ($), Europe the euro (€), Japan the yen (¥) and the UK the pound (£). When an Indian company buys oil from abroad, or a student goes to study in the USA, rupees must first be changed into dollars. The exchange rate decides how many rupees that takes.

Rupee gets weaker (depreciates)₹90 per $ becomes ₹95 per $. You need more rupees for each dollar.
Rupee gets stronger (appreciates)₹95 per $ becomes ₹90 per $. You need fewer rupees for each dollar.
Careful with the words!A bigger number (₹95 per dollar) means a weaker rupee.

3Why does the rate move?

Like the price of tomatoes, the price of a dollar depends on how many people want dollars (demand) and how many dollars are coming in (supply).

⬆ Indians need dollars to…buy oil, gold, electronics and machines from abroad; pay college fees overseas; travel abroad. Foreign investors also take dollars out when they sell Indian shares.
⬇ Dollars come into India when…we sell software, medicines and goods abroad; Indians working abroad send money home; foreign companies build factories here.
🌍 2026 exampleA war in West Asia pushed oil prices up. India buys most of its oil from abroad, so it needed many more dollars, and the rupee fell to a record low of ₹96.97 per dollar in May.

4A weaker rupee: who gains, who loses?

😟 LosersFamilies paying more for petrol, phones and gold; students studying abroad; companies that import raw materials; tourists going abroad.
😊 WinnersExporters (software firms, textile and mango exporters) who earn more rupees per dollar; families whose relatives send money from abroad; foreign tourists visiting India.
⚖️ Neither good nor badA weaker rupee is not a "score" for the country. Too fast a fall causes inflation, but an overly strong rupee hurts exporters and jobs.

5How the RBI steadies the rupee

The RBI keeps a big savings pot of dollars, gold and other currencies called foreign exchange reserves, worth more than $700 billion. Think of it as a water tank: when there's a shortage of dollars, the RBI sells some to stop the rupee from falling too fast; when dollars flood in, it buys and refills the tank.

The RBI does not fix the rupee at one number. It tries to stop wild jumps, so that families and businesses can plan.

7Check yourself

Level 2 · For working people

How the rupee is quoted and what moves it, the 2026 story, what it means for your expenses, savings and business, and how it ripples through the country and the world. This is education, not financial advice.

1Reading exchange rates

  • Quotes. USD/INR 93.70 means ₹93.70 per dollar. Banks quote a buy (bid) and a sell (ask) price. The gap, plus any charges, is their margin; at airport counters and on some cards it can be 2–5%.
  • Spot vs forward. Spot is for delivery in two days. A forward locks in a rate for a future date; the forward premium mainly reflects the interest-rate gap between India and the US.
  • The percentage trap. If the rupee goes from 90 to 95 per dollar, the dollar has gained 5.6% (5/90), but the rupee has lost 5.3% (5/95) in dollar terms. Check which way a headline measures it.
  • The regime. India has a managed float: the market sets the rate, and the RBI intervenes to smooth volatility, not to defend a fixed level.

3The rupee's 2026 story

  1. Early 2026: the rupee was already weak, near ₹90, after a strong dollar and foreign outflows in 2025.
  2. The West Asia conflict sent crude prices and shipping risk soaring. India's oil import bill jumped and foreign investors sold Indian shares. The rupee hit a record ₹96.97 in May and was near that level again in early October.
  3. Defences: the RBI sold dollars (reserves fell about $50 bn from their peak), the government raised import duty on gold and silver to 15% to cut demand for dollars, steps were taken to attract dollar deposits, and the repo rate was raised in October (Topic 02).
  4. November: softer crude and renewed foreign buying of Indian equities lifted the rupee to about ₹93.7. It is still down about 4% for the year.

4What it means for you

Fuel, gadgets and goldPetrol, LPG, phones, laptops and gold all have dollar prices. A 5% weaker rupee raises their rupee cost unless taxes or margins absorb it.
Foreign education and travelFees are fixed in dollars, euros or pounds, so a falling rupee raises the rupee cost every year. Plan with a depreciation assumption; see the calculator below.
NRI familiesThe same remittance buys more at home when the rupee is weak, which is one reason remittances hit records in 2026.
Your salaryIT, pharma and export-sector earnings tend to benefit from a weaker rupee; import-dependent sectors such as airlines and oil marketing companies get squeezed.
Investing abroadOverseas investments gain in rupee terms when the rupee falls. Residents can remit up to $250,000 a year under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, subject to tax collected at source and other rules.

5For businesses: currency risk and hedging

  • Importers lose when the rupee falls between order and payment. Exporters lose when it rises before they convert their receipts.
  • Forward contracts lock in a rate today for a future payment. Options protect against a bad move while keeping the upside, for a premium.
  • Natural hedges, such as matching dollar costs with dollar revenues, reduce the need for financial hedges.
  • Foreign-currency loans (ECBs) look cheap because of lower interest rates abroad, but an unhedged loan can become far more expensive if the rupee falls. The interest saving is roughly the forward premium you would otherwise pay.

6What the exchange rate does to the country

InflationA weaker rupee raises import prices, especially fuel, which feeds into CPI (Topic 01). This "imported inflation" is a key reason the RBI cares about the rupee.
CompetitivenessThe real effective exchange rate (REER) adjusts for inflation differences across trading partners. If Indian inflation is higher, a stable nominal rupee actually means a stronger real rupee, which hurts exports.
Debt and companiesFirms and the government pay more rupees to service foreign-currency debt when the rupee falls. India's external debt is modest relative to GDP, but some firms are exposed.
Policy trade-offsDefending the rupee costs reserves and can require higher interest rates, which slows growth. Letting it fall raises inflation. Every central bank balances these.

7The global picture and history

  • 1991, India's crisis: reserves fell to roughly three weeks of imports. India pledged gold abroad, devalued the rupee and began the liberalisation that shaped today's economy. Today's roughly 11 months of import cover is the lesson learned.
  • 1997, Asian crisis: fixed exchange rates, short-term dollar debt and sudden capital flight broke the currencies of Thailand, Indonesia and Korea.
  • 2013, the "taper tantrum": a hint of reduced Fed bond-buying sent the rupee to a then-record low near ₹68.8 per dollar. India was one of the "fragile five".
  • The dollar's dominance: most trade invoicing, debt and reserves are in dollars, so US rates and the dollar's strength (Topic 02) ripple everywhere. India is promoting rupee invoicing and settlement with selected partners.

8Sensible habits

  • For goals in foreign currency (education, travel, overseas property), plan with a depreciation assumption and start converting gradually rather than all at once.
  • Compare the total cost (rate plus fees) of forex cards, bank transfers and airport counters.
  • Don't speculate on currencies through unregulated apps; forex trading in India is allowed only on recognised exchanges in permitted pairs.
  • If you run a business with foreign-currency exposure, have a written hedging policy.

9Check yourself

Level 3 · Review for experts

Regimes, parity conditions, balance-of-payments accounting, exchange-rate models, intervention and reserve adequacy, pass-through and the international monetary system.

1Regimes and the trilemma

  • A spectrum from hard pegs (currency board, dollarisation) through conventional pegs and crawls to managed and free floats. IMF de facto classifications often differ from de jure ones. India's is officially market-determined, but the IMF has at times classified its de facto arrangement as "stabilized" because of heavy smoothing.
  • The trilemma: a country can have at most two of a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility and independent monetary policy. India runs a partially open capital account, inflation targeting and a managed float, so intervention is the pressure valve (see Rey's "dilemma" in Topic 02).

2Parity conditions

Absolute PPP: S = P / P* Relative PPP: %ΔS ≈ π − π* Covered IP: F = S · (1 + i·τ) / (1 + i*·τ) Uncovered IP: E[%ΔS] ≈ i − i* (+ risk premium) Real exchange rate: q = S·P* / P (a rise means a real depreciation of the home currency)
  • PPP holds poorly in the short run. Half-lives of real-rate deviations of 3–5 years make up the "PPP puzzle". The Balassa–Samuelson effect (fast productivity growth in tradables) pushes fast-growing economies' price levels and real exchange rates up over time.
  • CIP held almost exactly before 2008. Persistent deviations since then (cross-currency basis) reflect balance-sheet costs and dollar-funding scarcity.
  • UIP fails empirically: the forward-premium puzzle, where high-yield currencies tend to appreciate rather than depreciate, underpins carry trades and their crash risk.

3Balance of payments: the accounting

CA + KA + FA(excl. reserves) − ΔReserves + errors = 0 (BPM6 sign conventions vary by presentation) CA = (S − I) = (S_p − I_p) + (T − G) → "twin deficits" when fiscal and external gaps co-move
  • India's structural pattern is a large goods deficit (energy, gold, electronics), a large services surplus (IT, global capability centres), large remittances, and a moderate current account deficit (about 1–2% of GDP over the cycle; 0.5% in Q1 FY27).
  • Quality of financing: FDI and long-term debt are stable. FPI and short-term debt are flighty. Q1 FY27 shows the swing: FDI +$6.1 bn against FPI −$9.6 bn.
  • Watch the valuation effects. Reported reserve changes mix BoP flows with revaluation of gold and non-dollar assets. The BoP-basis change (−$8.1 bn in Q1) isolates the flows.

4Models of the exchange rate

ModelKey mechanism
Mundell–FlemingIS-LM-BP. Under floating rates and capital mobility, monetary policy is powerful and fiscal policy is crowded out through appreciation.
Monetary (flexible-price)S is determined by relative money supplies and money demands; consistent with PPP
Dornbusch overshooting (1976)Sticky goods prices with UIP: a monetary expansion makes S jump past its long-run level, then appreciate back
Portfolio balanceImperfect substitutability; supply of domestic vs foreign bonds and risk premia. Provides the theoretical channel for sterilised intervention.
Empirical realityMeese–Rogoff (1983): structural models fail to beat a random walk out of sample at short horizons. Order flow and dollar-factor models do better.

5Intervention and reserve adequacy

  • Sterilised intervention: selling dollars drains rupee liquidity, which the RBI offsets with operations such as open market purchases. It works through the signalling and portfolio-balance channels. Unsterilised intervention also tightens domestic liquidity, which in 2026 reinforced the rate hike.
  • Tools: spot and forward intervention, non-deliverable forward (NDF) market operations, buy-sell swaps, and measures to attract inflows (incentives for NRI deposits, relaxed limits on foreign investment in debt).
  • Adequacy metrics: import cover (about 11 months), the Greenspan–Guidotti rule (reserves of at least short-term external debt by residual maturity) and the IMF's composite ARA metric. India sits comfortably above the thresholds, but 2026 showed how fast $50 bn can go.
  • The cost of carrying reserves: the gap between domestic and reserve-currency yields, which India pays as insurance against sudden stops.

6Pass-through, competitiveness and invoicing

  • Exchange-rate pass-through to CPI in India is estimated at roughly 5–15% over a year: higher for fuel and gold, lower for food and services. It is asymmetric and larger when the depreciation is big and persistent.
  • REER (the RBI's 40-currency trade-weighted index) deflates the nominal rate by relative CPI. Persistent inflation differentials can cause real appreciation even during nominal depreciation.
  • The dominant-currency paradigm (Gopinath et al.): because trade is invoiced in dollars, export prices are sticky in dollars. A rupee depreciation lowers imports quickly but raises exports only slowly, and the dollar's strength matters more than bilateral rates.

7The international monetary system

  • Bretton Woods (1944–1971): dollar–gold convertibility. The Triffin dilemma (the world's need for dollars versus confidence in their gold backing) ended it when Nixon closed the gold window in 1971.
  • The Plaza (1985) and Louvre (1987) accords were coordinated interventions on the dollar, a model rarely repeated since.
  • Dollar dominance persists in invoicing, debt, reserves (about 57–58%) and FX turnover (the dollar is on one side of close to 90% of trades, per the BIS triennial survey). "De-dollarisation" is gradual at most.
  • Rupee internationalisation: special rupee vostro accounts, local-currency settlement agreements and UPI cross-border links. Full capital-account convertibility remains a long-term, sequenced goal.

8Review checklist: open questions

  1. How much smoothing is optimal for an inflation-targeting emerging market? Does heavy intervention blunt the shock-absorber role of the exchange rate?
  2. Should the RBI target or monitor the REER explicitly to protect export competitiveness?
  3. Is $700 bn+ of reserves too much insurance given its carrying cost, or too little given 2026's drawdown speed?
  4. What does the dominant-currency paradigm imply for the gains from a weaker rupee?
  5. How fast should India move towards capital-account convertibility and rupee internationalisation?
  6. How should the RBI respond when a currency shock and a supply-side inflation shock come together, as in 2026?
