Summary

Kuruthipunal is a police thriller about what happens to good men when the enemy can reach their families. Two senior officers, Adhi Narayanan (Kamal Haasan) and his friend Abbas (Arjun), run a secret operation — codenamed Dhanush — to plant undercover policemen inside a Naxalite group led by the calm, ruthless Badri (Nassar).

When Badri is captured, he does not break. Instead, he breaks Adhi — by showing that he can reach Adhi’s home and his son whenever he wants. What follows is a story of a decent officer turned informer for the people he is meant to destroy, and of how he finds his way back at the highest possible price.

In 1995, a big-star Tamil film with no songs and a bleak ending was a huge risk. It opened on the same Diwali as Rajinikanth’s Muthu and still succeeded.

Where Nayagan and Thalapathi made heroes out of gangsters, Kuruthipunal turns the camera around: the policeman is the one who falls, and the system is the real villain.

Plot

Spoilers ahead. Acts 4 and 5 are blurred —

Operation Dhanush

Adhi and Abbas send two undercover officers, Anand and Shiva, into Badri’s group. A year later Anand is caught and takes his own life before he can be questioned. Using what he had passed on, the police learn the group is moving someone important towards Tiruchirappalli; a shootout follows and a driver is arrested.

Badri in the cell

The group kills a visiting Union Minister with a rocket launcher. The captured shooter reveals the arrested “driver” is Badri himself — and Badri has him killed inside custody. Under interrogation, Badri gives up a mole in the police: Srinivasan, Adhi and Abbas’s own mentor, who kills himself before arrest.

The family as hostage

Badri’s aide Narasimhan has Adhi’s dog killed and his son hurt, to prove they can reach his home at will. Adhi gives in: he lets Badri escape and becomes the group’s informer inside the police.

A friend lost

Abbas grows suspicious, follows Adhi, and is captured, tortured and killed by Badri. His widow Zeenath and daughter move in with Adhi’s family. Crushed by guilt, Adhi turns: he kills Narasimhan, but is overpowered when he goes after Badri. In the violence at home, the militant Mala dies protecting Abbas’s daughter and Adhi’s wife Sumitra kills Surendar.

“Shoot me”

Badri lets slip that the police spy codenamed Dhanush has gone silent — and Adhi realises Shiva is Dhanush. He breaks free, kills Badri, and orders Shiva to shoot him, so Shiva can rise inside the group and the mission survives. Shiva obeys. Adhi and Abbas are honoured after their deaths, but the closing scene shows the sons of both sides already swearing enmity, as Adhi’s voice warns that the cycle will go on.

Director & cameraman in one

Sreeram — who shot Nayagan — both directed and lit this film, which is why its look is so controlled.

Music & sound

Kuruthipunal has no songs — rare for a big Tamil release then, and rarer still for a Kamal Haasan film opening on Diwali. Its music is a background score by Mahesh, who had worked with Kamal on Nammavar (1994).

Mahesh wrote the score to make Badri feel like a threat even when he is not on screen.

Why the sound mattered

  • First Indian film released in Dolby Stereo SR — a new analogue noise-reduction format that gave cleaner, louder and wider sound in theatres.
  • Gunfire, the RPG strike and the interrogation room were designed for surround speakers, not just the screen.
  • With no songs to break the tension, the soundtrack carries the suspense from start to end.

Technology & craft

Dolby Stereo SR

Kuruthipunal was the first Indian film to use Dolby Stereo SR (Spectral Recording). Together with Sreeram’s images, it gave the film what The Hindu called a foreign-film sheen.

Weapons research

Kamal Haasan consulted army personnel to learn how a rocket-propelled grenade launcher is actually handled, so the assassination scene would be correct in detail.

Police authenticity

Uniforms and haircuts followed National Police Academy specifications, so the officers look like real IPS men rather than film policemen.

The interrogation

Sreeram chose to stylise the famous interrogation of Badri rather than shoot it realistically — the lighting and framing make it feel like a battle of minds, not just a beating.

Speed

Despite the tight budget, the film was shot in about 30 days, with a Telugu version, Drohi, filmed in parallel.

Effects

The New Straits Times singled out its realistic special effects — all practical, on-set work, years before digital effects became common in Tamil cinema.

Emotional arc across the film

Intensity (0–10) at the film’s key turning points, as scored for this review. Unlike the first two reviews, the curve barely dips — there are no songs to let you breathe.

Where the emotions come from

The calm enemy. Nassar’s Badri hardly raises his voice. His quiet certainty — that every man can be broken through the people he loves — is more frightening than any shouting villain.
The dog and the boy. The attack on Adhi’s home is the turning point. It is not big action; it is the cold realisation that a policeman’s family has no protection at all.
A good man’s fall. Kamal Haasan plays Adhi’s surrender as shame, not cowardice. You understand every choice he makes, and that is what makes it painful.
Abbas. Arjun’s Abbas is loyal to the end. His death at Badri’s hands — caused, in a way, by his friend’s silence — is the film’s deepest wound.
The final order. “Shoot me … it’s a bloody order!” Adhi chooses to die so that the mission, and Shiva’s cover, can survive. It is redemption with no reward.
The next generation. The last scene, where the children of both sides already hate each other, refuses any comfort. The river of blood keeps flowing.

What works

  • Nassar’s Badri — one of Tamil cinema’s most chilling villains.
  • Kamal Haasan’s courage as producer: no songs, no easy ending.
  • Sound and effects years ahead of their time in India.
  • Tight, purposeful pacing with almost no wasted scenes.

What doesn’t

  • The New Straits Times felt Adhi’s fear for his family did not fully come through, and that Arjun’s usual intensity was missing.
  • Ananda Vikatan called it distinctive but not for ordinary audiences, rating it 45/100.
  • Its cold, clinical tone keeps you at arm’s length compared with Nayagan or Thalapathi.

Watch it for: the Badri interrogation, the sound design and the last ten minutes. Govind Nihalani himself said the remake was better than his original.

Awards & legacy

Awards

  • Filmfare Award, Best Actor (Tamil) — Kamal Haasan
  • Cinema Express Award, Best Film (Tamil)
  • India’s official entry for Best Foreign Language Film at the 68th Academy Awards (not nominated)

Influence

  • Gained cult status; seen as setting the standard for action films of its era
  • Gautham Vasudev Menon and A. R. Murugadoss cite it as an influence
  • Screened in the Kamal Haasan “Director in Focus” retrospective at Rotterdam

Pop culture

  • Lines like “Delay, disable and disintegrate” are still quoted
  • Actor Dhanush took his screen name from the film’s covert operation
