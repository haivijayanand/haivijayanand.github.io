@sub From Yagn's 1890 spring patent and GE's Hardiman to FDA-cleared walking suits, factory shoulder vests and consumer hip-assist devices
@outline What an exoskeleton is | Four eras of history | Enabling technologies | Path to market: regulation, payment, standards | Major players in 2026 | Market, challenges and outlook | Relevance to India | Conclusions
@tnotes Exoskeletons have gone from a nineteenth-century patent and a famous 1960s failure to a real industry: medical devices walking paralysed patients, tens of thousands of shoulder vests in factories, and hip-assist devices sold to consumers. This talk traces that journey, explains what made the products possible, maps who makes them today and looks at where the field is heading.

T: Classifying exoskeletons | tab1 | cols=0,1
N: Exoskeletons are classified along four axes: the body region they assist, how they are actuated, passive, powered or hybrid, whether the structure is rigid or a soft textile exosuit, and the application, from clinical rehabilitation to consumer sport. Most commercial success so far has come from narrow, well-defined combinations of these.

F: The first failures: GE Hardiman (1965–1971) | fig1
- Full-body hydraulic "man amplifier" for the US Army and Navy
- Meant to let one operator lift about 680 kg; weighed about 680 kg itself
- Never walked under control: the limit was control, not force
- The lesson still holds today
N: The Hardiman was the first serious powered full-body suit, a hydraulic man amplifier. It could lift heavy loads with one arm, but the whole suit never moved safely under control. The lesson of Hardiman still governs the field: the limiting factors are power, control and the human interface, not raw force.

F: Belgrade, 1972: powered gait for paraplegia | fig2
- Vukobratović's group at the Mihajlo Pupin Institute
- Pneumatic actuators, pre-programmed gait patterns
- Zero-moment point (ZMP) theory for balance: later the basis of humanoid robots
N: At the same time, in Belgrade, Miomir Vukobratović's team built active exoskeletons for paraplegic patients. They introduced the zero-moment-point concept for dynamic balance, which later became the foundation of humanoid robot walking. This line of work, rehabilitation rather than strength, is the one that eventually reached the market first.

F: Defence-funded renaissance (1990–2010) | fig3
- Cyberdyne HAL (Tsukuba, 1992 on): reads surface EMG to detect intent
- DARPA EHPA (2000): US$50 M programme; BLEEX, Sarcos XOS
- HULC licensed to Lockheed Martin (2009)
N: The second wave came from defence funding and university labs. Japan's HAL read the wearer's muscle signals to detect intent. DARPA's Exoskeletons for Human Performance Augmentation programme funded Berkeley's BLEEX and the Sarcos XOS, and BLEEX led to the HULC load carrier licensed to Lockheed Martin.

T: Milestones of the commercial era | tab2
N: The timeline shows 124 years from Yagn's 1890 patent to the first home-use clearance, ReWalk in 2014. Industrial passive devices reached volume in car plants from 2015, the TALOS powered combat suit was cancelled in 2019, and since then soft exosuits and consumer devices have widened adoption.

T: Enabling technologies | tab3 | rows=0-4 | cols=0,2
N: Five subsystems had to mature. Actuators trade torque density against back-drivability. Sensing must detect intent robustly without skin preparation. Control must be transparent when assistance is not needed yet stable and safe. Batteries trade run time against mass, still the main barrier for full-body military suits. And structures trade stiffness for comfort.

T: Crossing the regulatory barrier | tab4 | cols=0,1,2
N: Medical devices crossed the FDA barrier one by one: ReWalk Personal by de novo in 2014, then Ekso, Indego, HAL and Keeogo by the 510(k) route. But clearance alone did not create a market; payment did. Medicare placed personal exoskeletons in the brace benefit in 2024, with a lump-sum fee of about 82 000 to 109 000 dollars.

F: Medical rehabilitation and personal mobility | fig6
- Ekso, Lifeward (ReWalk), Cyberdyne, Fourier, Wandercraft …
- Clinic gait training for stroke, spinal cord injury, MS
- Home-use devices need payer coverage to scale
N: In medicine, devices such as the EksoNR are used in clinics with a therapist for stroke and spinal-cord-injury rehabilitation. Personal mobility devices for home use, including self-balancing designs, are scaling only where insurers pay. Chinese and Korean makers are entering with lower-cost devices.

F: Industrial and occupational | fig8
- Passive shoulder and back vests: Comau MATE, Ottobock Paexo, Levitate, Hilti
- Tens of thousands in auto, aerospace, logistics, construction
- Powered soft back suits for warehouses
N: The volume market is industrial. Passive shoulder vests that support the arms during overhead work, and lumbar devices for lifting, are now common in car and aircraft plants and in logistics. They need no batteries, cost a few thousand dollars and solve one well-defined task, which is exactly why they succeeded.

F: What the technology means to a user | fig7
- US Marine Capt. Derek Herrera, paralysed in combat in 2012
- Standing at his retirement ceremony in 2014 in a personal exoskeleton
- Personal devices: home and community walking, not just clinic therapy
N: This photograph shows why personal mobility devices matter. Captain Derek Herrera, paralysed in combat, stood at his own retirement ceremony in 2014 using a personal exoskeleton. Beyond the clinical benefits, standing and walking at eye level has a social and psychological value that is hard to put in a cost-benefit table, but it is real.

B: Military and consumer
- **Military:** converging on light load-carriage and logistics aids, not powered armour (TALOS cancelled 2019)
- **Consumer:** Chinese hip-assist devices sell in large numbers for hiking, walking and the elderly
- Consumer exos may become like e-bikes: mass-market, regulated mobility products
N: Militaries have moved away from the Iron Man vision towards light knee and hip load-carriage aids and logistics lifting suits. The surprise of the last few years is consumer hip-assist devices from China, sold for hiking and walking in large numbers. They may follow e-bikes into the mass market.

T: Market size in 2026 | tab9 | cols=0,1,2
N: Published estimates agree that the global market is around 560 to 600 million dollars in 2025, led in revenue by medical rehabilitation and in units by consumer hip-assist devices. Growth forecasts vary widely between sources, so treat them with caution. Many companies are small and some have consolidated.

B: Challenges and limitations
- Power density: hours for a hip device, not for a heavy-work full-body suit
- Intent and human–machine interaction: misreading intent causes falls
- Fit and joint alignment: human joints are not simple hinges
- Evidence of benefit outside the laboratory
- Standards (ASTM F48, ISO 13482), cost (US$80 000–110 000) and acceptance
N: The open problems are familiar. Batteries limit powered suits. The device must know what the user intends, and misreading intent is dangerous. Human joints move in complex ways, so misalignment causes chafing and wasted effort. Laboratory metabolic savings do not always carry over to real work. And industrial devices fall between medical and protective-equipment rules.

T: Future outlook by application | tab10 | cols=0,1,2
N: Over the next decade, expect exoskeleton gait training to become routine in clinics, lightweight soft suits for ageing populations, passive industrial devices treated like protective equipment and connected to ergonomics programmes, hybrid military devices passive by default and powered on demand, and consumer devices sold like e-bikes.

F: Relevance to India | fig11
- Large rehabilitation need; imported devices at US$80 000+ are out of reach
- Low-cost passive exos for soldiers and porters at high altitude
- Affordable rehab exos for district hospitals on Indian supply chains
- Reliability and endurance testing of actuator drive trains, bearings, gearboxes
N: India has a large rehabilitation need and a defence requirement for load carriage in high-altitude terrain, but imported medical devices are unaffordable for most. The opportunities are low-cost passive devices for soldiers and porters, affordable rehabilitation devices for district hospitals, industrial devices, and the reliability and endurance testing of the drive trains, bearings and gearboxes that power them.

C: Conclusions
- 124 years from Yagn's patent to the first home-use clearance
- Success came from narrowing the problem: defined patients with a payer; passive devices for specific tasks
- 2026 market ≈ US$0.6 billion; medical leads revenue, Chinese consumer devices lead units
- Military converging on light, task-specific aids
FW: Next decade
- Soft, hybrid, AI-controlled devices
- Credible standards and long-term evidence
- Prices that reach beyond rich-country clinics
N: Exoskeletons succeeded when they stopped trying to do everything. The next decade will favour soft, hybrid, AI-controlled devices with credible standards and evidence, and prices that make them available beyond a few wealthy clinics.
