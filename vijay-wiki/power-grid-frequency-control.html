Power Grid Frequency Control: Inertia, RoCoF, Droop, Reserves and Blackouts

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
Power systemsFrequencyGridRenewables

On an AC grid, generation and demand must balance every instant. Any imbalance shows up as a change in frequency: losing a large generator makes frequency fall at a rate set by the rotating inertia of all synchronous machines, until governors raise output. Understanding this chain explains reserve requirements, load-shedding schemes and why grids with many inverter-based renewables need new kinds of support.

Key takeaways

  • Frequency is common to the whole synchronous grid: 50 Hz in India, with the operating band 49.90–50.05 Hz under the Indian Electricity Grid Code.
  • Initial rate of change of frequency RoCoF = ΔP·f₀/(2·H·S); more inertia (H) means slower fall.
  • Governor droop (typically 4–5 %) shares load changes between generators and sets the steady-state frequency deviation.
  • Primary response acts in seconds, secondary (AGC) over minutes restores 50 Hz, tertiary reserves replace them.
  • Under-frequency load shedding is the last automatic defence before collapse; large blackouts usually start with overloads and cascading trips.

1. Inertia and RoCoF

Swing equation (aggregate): (2·H·S / f0) · d(Δf)/dt = ΔP_mech − ΔP_load − D·Δf Initial RoCoF = ΔP · f0 / (2 · H · S) H = stored kinetic energy / rating (s): 2–9 s for synchronous machines; ≈ 0 for inverter-based plant

Example: losing 1,000 MW in a 200 GW system with H = 4 s gives RoCoF ≈ 0.031 Hz/s.

2. Governor droop

Droop R: Δf/f0 = −R · ΔP/P_rated (R = 0.04–0.05) System stiffness β ≈ Σ P_rated/(R·f0) + load damping (MW/Hz) Steady-state Δf = ΔP / β

With 50 GW of generation on 5 % droop plus 1 % load damping, β ≈ 24,000 MW/Hz, so the 1,000 MW loss settles about 0.042 Hz low until secondary control restores 50 Hz.

3. Control layers

LayerTime frameMechanism
Inertial response0–few secondsKinetic energy of rotating machines (automatic, physical)
Primary (governor) responseSeconds to ~30 sDroop control on generators, fast batteries
Secondary (AGC / LFC)30 s – 15 minCentral control restores frequency and tie-line flows
Tertiary15 min – hoursRe-dispatch to restore reserves
Under-frequency load sheddingEmergency stages (e.g. 49.4, 49.2, 49.0 Hz)Disconnect blocks of load to arrest decline

4. Lessons from major blackouts

EventWhat happened (summary)Lesson
Northern & Eastern India, 30–31 July 2012Heavy inter-regional flows and overdrawal; line trips led to grid separation and collapse affecting several hundred million peopleDiscipline on schedules, protection coordination, adequate transmission margins
North-east USA and Canada, 14 Aug 2003Lines sagged into trees; alarm software failure left operators unaware; cascading tripsSituational awareness, vegetation management, reliability standards
Italy, 28 Sep 2003Loss of import lines from Switzerland; frequency collapseCoordination between neighbouring operators, defence plans
Great Britain, 9 Aug 2019Near-simultaneous loss of offshore wind and gas plant after a lightning strike; low-frequency demand disconnectionInertia and RoCoF settings of distributed generation; fast reserves

As synchronous plant is replaced by solar and wind (inverter-based), system inertia falls and RoCoF rises. Grids respond with synchronous condensers, fast frequency response from batteries, grid-forming inverters and minimum-inertia constraints in dispatch.
