Level 1 · For school students

Taxes are how a country pools money to pay for things we all share.

1What is a tax?

A tax is money that people and businesses must pay to the government, by law. The government uses it to pay for things that everyone shares.

Think of a class collecting ₹20 from each student to buy a cricket kit for everyone. No single student could afford the whole kit, but together you can. Taxes work the same way, for a whole country: roads, schools, hospitals, the army, police, satellites and much more.

It is compulsoryUnlike a donation, a tax must be paid. Not paying it is against the law.
No direct returnYou do not get a specific thing back for your tax payment, but you benefit from everything it pays for.
Decided by ParliamentIn India, no tax can be collected unless a law passed by Parliament or a State Assembly allows it (Article 265 of the Constitution).

3Two families of taxes

Direct taxesPaid directly by the person who earns: income tax on salaries and business profits, and corporate tax on company profits. The more you earn, the more you pay.
Indirect taxesIncluded in the price of what you buy: GST on most goods and services, and customs duty on imports. The shop collects it and passes it on to the government. Everyone pays it, even children buying a pencil!

5Is it fair? More income, higher rate

India's income tax is progressive: people with higher incomes pay a higher percentage, not just a higher amount. Income is cut into slabs, and each slab has its own rate.

Example (new regime, simplified): the first ₹4 lakh of a year's income is tax-free, the next ₹4 lakh is taxed at 5%, the next ₹4 lakh at 10%, and so on up to 30%. A special rebate means that anyone earning up to ₹12 lakh pays no income tax at all.

GST is different: a rich and a poor person pay the same GST on a pencil. That is why essentials such as milk and rice are usually exempt or taxed lightly, while luxury cars carry the highest rate.

Level 2 · For working people

Your income tax under the new and old regimes, GST, capital gains, compliance, and how taxes shape the country and the world. This is educational only, not tax advice: rules have conditions and exceptions, so verify with the law or a chartered accountant.

1India's tax map, and the new law

  • Direct taxes (Centre): personal income tax, corporate tax, securities transaction tax (STT). Capital gains are part of income tax.
  • Indirect taxes: GST (shared between Centre and states), customs duty, central excise on petrol, diesel and tobacco, state VAT on fuel and alcohol, and stamp duty.
  • The Income-tax Act, 2025 took effect on 1 April 2026, replacing the 1961 Act. It has simpler language and renumbered sections, and the single term "Tax Year" replaces "previous year / assessment year". Rates and slabs were not changed by the switch; returns for income earned up to March 2026 still follow the 1961 Act.

4GST: how it works, and the 2025 reform

  • One tax, credited along the chain. Each business pays GST on its sales minus the GST it already paid on inputs (input tax credit). The final consumer bears the full tax, as the school-level animation shows.
  • CGST + SGST on sales within a state (split equally); IGST on sales between states and on imports, later apportioned to the consuming state. GST is a destination-based tax.
  • GST 2.0 (from 22 September 2025): most items moved into two main rates, 5% and 18%, with 40% for luxury and sin goods. Many daily-use items, medicines and small vehicles became cheaper. Some essentials are exempt (0%), and special low rates apply to gold and precious stones.
  • Outside GST: petrol, diesel, ATF, natural gas and alcohol for drinking still carry central excise and state VAT. That is why fuel prices differ across states.

6Staying compliant

  • TDS/TCS: tax deducted or collected at source on salary, interest, rent and some purchases. Check that it appears in your Form 26AS / AIS.
  • Advance tax is due in instalments if your tax not covered by TDS exceeds ₹10,000 in a year (for example, rental, freelance or capital-gains income). Late payment attracts interest.
  • File your return even if your tax is zero: it builds a record for loans and visas and lets you claim refunds. The department matches your return with its data from banks, employers and registrars.
  • Tax planning vs evasion: using legal deductions and choosing a regime is planning. Hiding income, fake rent receipts or cash deals is evasion, with penalties and prosecution.

7Taxes and the country

How much?Combined Centre and state tax revenue is roughly 17–18% of GDP, low compared with advanced economies. That limits spending on health and education without borrowing.
2026 so farNet direct taxes were up 11.8% to ₹14.42 lakh crore by 10 November, and gross GST rose 22.9% in October. Strong collections help keep the fiscal deficit on track (Topic 03, Topic 08).
Sharing with statesThe Finance Commission decides the states' share of the Centre's divisible tax pool (41% under the 15th Finance Commission) and how it is split among them. The GST Council, with the Union and state finance ministers, sets GST rates.
Taxes shape behaviourHigh taxes on tobacco and sugary drinks discourage their use; tax breaks encourage saving, housing and pension investment. Every exemption has a cost, called tax expenditure.

8The global picture

  • Global minimum tax: under the OECD/G20 Pillar Two rules, large multinationals face a minimum effective rate of 15% in each country where they operate, to curb profit shifting to tax havens.
  • Digital economy: countries are debating how to tax tech giants that earn from users without a physical presence. India abolished its 6% equalisation levy on online advertising from April 2025.
  • Tariffs are taxes too: import duties raise prices for domestic buyers. The wave of tariffs since 2025 has reshaped trade flows and costs worldwide.
  • Corporate tax competition: India cut its base corporate rate to 22% (15% for new manufacturing firms at the time) in 2019 to stay competitive with Asian peers.

Level 3 · Review for experts

Principles, incidence and deadweight loss, optimal taxation, India's tax architecture and fiscal federalism, and international tax.

1Principles

  • Adam Smith's canons: equity, certainty, convenience and economy (low collection cost). Modern additions: efficiency (minimal distortion), simplicity and administrability.
  • Benefit vs ability to pay. Horizontal equity means equal treatment of equals; vertical equity means appropriate differences for unequals.
  • Average vs marginal rates. Progressivity is measured by how the average rate rises with income (or by the Kakwani index). Incentives depend on the effective marginal rate, including benefit withdrawal and phase-outs such as India's rebate cliff and its marginal relief.

2Incidence and deadweight loss

Consumer share of a per-unit tax ≈ ε_s / (ε_s + |ε_d|) Producer share ≈ |ε_d| / (ε_s + |ε_d|) Deadweight loss (Harberger triangle) ≈ ½ · t² · [ε_s·|ε_d| / (ε_s + |ε_d|)] · P·Q (t as a share of price)
  • Statutory incidence (who writes the cheque) is irrelevant to economic incidence in competitive markets. The more inelastic side bears more of the tax.
  • DWL rises with the square of the rate: many low rates on broad bases beat a few high rates on narrow ones. This is the efficiency case for GST rate rationalisation and for a broad income-tax base with few exemptions.
  • Corporate tax incidence is shared between capital and labour. In open economies, a substantial share may fall on workers through lower investment (an estimate that remains contested).

3Optimal taxation

  • Ramsey (1927): commodity tax rates should be inversely proportional to the demand elasticity. This is efficient but regressive, since necessities are inelastic, hence the equity–efficiency trade-off in GST design.
  • Mirrlees (1971): optimal non-linear income taxation under hidden ability; marginal rates should balance incentives against redistribution.
  • Atkinson–Stiglitz (1976): with an optimal non-linear income tax and separable preferences, differentiated commodity taxes add nothing. This is a theoretical case for uniform GST, though weak administration in developing countries gives reasons to depart from it.
  • Diamond–Saez top rate: τ* = 1 / (1 + a·e), where a is the Pareto parameter of the top income tail and e the elasticity of taxable income.

Laffer logic is embedded here: above τ*, a higher rate lowers revenue from top earners. The empirical elasticity (which depends on avoidance opportunities and the breadth of the base) is the crux, which is why broadening the base raises the optimal rate.

4India's tax architecture

  • Direct-tax shift. The corporate rate cut (2019), the new personal regime (2020), which became the default (2023), and the rebate extended to ₹12 lakh (2025) have narrowed the base for personal income tax. Revenue growth now relies on compliance, data analytics (AIS, TDS/TCS coverage) and formalisation.
  • The Income-tax Act 2025 is a consolidation and simplification (Tax Year concept, fewer sections) rather than a policy change. The test is whether it reduces litigation: a large stock of disputed direct-tax demand remains under appeal.
  • GST design issues: petroleum and electricity remain outside GST, which breaks the credit chain and causes tax cascading. GST 2.0 reduced inverted duty structures, the end of the compensation cess raises questions about state revenues, and rate changes may not be fully passed on to consumers.
  • Buoyancy: tax buoyancy (the % change in tax revenue / % change in nominal GDP) above 1 shows gains in compliance or structure. With low GDP-deflator inflation (Topic 03), nominal growth and therefore collections can lag real growth.

5Fiscal federalism

  • Vertical imbalance: states carry most spending responsibilities, while the Centre raises the more buoyant taxes. Finance Commission devolution (41% of the divisible pool under the 15th FC) and its horizontal formula (population, area, income distance, demographic performance, forest cover, tax effort) correct for this.
  • Cesses and surcharges are outside the divisible pool, and their growing share has been a point of Centre–state contention.
  • GST Council: pooled sovereignty. The Centre has one-third of the votes and states two-thirds, and decisions need a three-quarters majority, so neither can act alone.

6International tax

  • BEPS (base erosion and profit shifting): transfer pricing, intangibles in low-tax jurisdictions, treaty shopping. The responses include the OECD BEPS actions, the multilateral instrument and country-by-country reporting.
  • Pillar Two (GloBE rules, 15% minimum, with an income inclusion rule, an undertaxed profits rule and qualified domestic top-up taxes) is being implemented by many jurisdictions, though US participation remains contested. Pillar One, which reallocates taxing rights over residual profits, has stalled.
  • Tariff incidence: recent evidence suggests that tariffs are passed on largely to domestic importers and consumers, a reminder that border taxes are domestic taxes on residents.

7Review checklist: open questions

  1. With most earners now below the zero-tax threshold, is India's personal income-tax base too narrow? What is the role of TDS/TCS data in widening it?
  2. Should petroleum and electricity be brought into GST, and how should states be compensated?
  3. Is the three-rate GST (5/18/40) close to the efficient frontier, or should India move towards a single standard rate?
  4. How much of the 2019 corporate tax cut passed through to investment and wages?
  5. Should the old regime be phased out entirely, and what replaces its incentives for saving and housing?
  6. How should India position itself on Pillar Two and on taxing the digital economy after abolishing the equalisation levy?
