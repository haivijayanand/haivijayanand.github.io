What Does God Expect from Human Beings?

28 Jun 20264 min readPhilosophy

If God Is Complete, What Satisfaction Does He Derive from Our Actions?

One of the deepest questions that has accompanied humanity across civilizations is this:

If God is supreme, all-powerful, and complete in every sense, why did He create human beings at all? What does He gain from our prayers, our struggles, our successes, or our failures?

At first glance, the question appears simple. Yet, beneath it lies a profound inquiry into the nature of existence, purpose, freedom, and the relationship between the Creator and creation.

Different faiths offer different explanations, but a remarkable common thread emerges: God does not need anything from us. Rather, our actions are intended to transform and elevate us.

A Perfect Being Needs Nothing

Human beings act because they seek something—wealth, recognition, happiness, companionship, or security. Our actions are often driven by needs and desires.

But if God is truly perfect, complete, and infinite, He cannot be lacking anything. A being that requires praise or worship to feel satisfied would not be fully complete.

Most theistic traditions therefore maintain that God gains nothing from human actions.

Our prayers do not increase His greatness.

Our failures do not diminish His power.

Our worship does not make Him more divine.

Instead, these practices are viewed as means through which human beings align themselves with truth, discipline their minds, and cultivate noble qualities.

The Classroom Analogy

A simple analogy helps illustrate this idea.

A teacher does not become more knowledgeable because a student learns mathematics. The teacher's wisdom remains unchanged. Yet, a good teacher derives joy from seeing the growth and success of the student.

Similarly, many believers understand God as a guide who desires human flourishing rather than personal gain.

The journey benefits the traveler, not the destination.

The Concept of Divine Play

Within Hindu philosophy, particularly in Vedantic traditions, another fascinating idea emerges—the concept of Lila, or divine play.

According to this view, creation is not born out of necessity but out of abundance.

An artist paints not because something is missing in his life, but because creativity naturally expresses itself.

A musician sings not because silence is insufficient, but because music flows from within.

Likewise, the universe is seen as an expression of divine fullness rather than divine need.

Human existence becomes part of a grand cosmic drama in which souls evolve, learn, experience, and ultimately rediscover their true nature.

Why Does God Test Human Beings?

Many religions describe life as a test.

This naturally raises another question:

Why should an all-knowing God test people when He already knows the outcome?

The answer often given is that tests are not for God's information but for human realization.

An examination does not reveal to a teacher whether education exists; it reveals to the student what has been understood and what still requires effort.

Similarly, life's challenges uncover courage, compassion, patience, honesty, and resilience that might otherwise remain hidden within us.

The purpose of struggle, according to this perspective, is transformation rather than divine entertainment.

The Skeptic's Question

Philosophers and skeptics have long challenged these ideas.

If God needs nothing, why create a world filled with suffering?

If everything is predetermined, what meaning does free will possess?

If God is perfectly loving, why are hardships distributed unequally?

These questions have no universally accepted answers. They continue to inspire debates among theologians, philosophers, scientists, and ordinary people alike.

The willingness to ask such questions honestly is itself part of humanity's intellectual and spiritual journey.

The Mystical Perspective

Mystics across various traditions often provide a different answer altogether.

They suggest that God desires nothing from human beings except that they recognize their own deeper nature.

Prayer becomes a process of inner transformation rather than a means of informing God.

Rituals become instruments for discipline and mindfulness rather than transactions seeking divine favors.

Good deeds refine the soul, cultivate compassion, and bring human beings closer to truth.

The ultimate objective is not merely obedience but realization—understanding who we truly are and how we are connected to the greater whole.

What Does God Expect from Ordinary People?

While doctrines differ, certain expectations appear repeatedly across traditions:

  • To live truthfully.
  • To practice compassion and kindness.
  • To uphold justice and fairness.
  • To help those in need.
  • To cultivate gratitude and humility.
  • To seek wisdom and self-improvement.
  • To respect life and creation.

These values do not enrich God; they enrich human civilization.

They transform individuals into better parents, better citizens, better leaders, and better companions.

Perhaps the expectation is not blind obedience but conscious participation in the moral and spiritual evolution of humanity.

The Eternal Question

The deepest mystery remains:

Why should a perfect and self-sufficient God create anything at all rather than nothing?

No single answer satisfies everyone.

Some call it divine love.

Some call it cosmic play.

Some call it the evolution of consciousness.

Others remain unconvinced and continue to seek alternative explanations.

Yet, the very act of questioning may itself be part of what makes human life meaningful.

For whether one believes in God or not, the search for purpose, truth, and goodness remains a shared human endeavor—one that has inspired civilizations, philosophies, and spiritual traditions across thousands of years.

Perhaps the greatest expectation, divine or otherwise, is that we strive to become better versions of ourselves while helping future generations do the same.
