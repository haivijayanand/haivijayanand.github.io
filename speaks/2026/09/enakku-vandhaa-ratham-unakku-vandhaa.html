“Enakku Vandhaa Ratham… Unakku Vandhaa Thakkali Chutney” — The Blame Game of Middle Management

4 Sep 20269 min read

There is a popular Tamil expression:

“Enakku vandhaa ratham; unakku vandhaa thakkali chutney.”

When it happens to me, it is blood.
When it happens to you, it is only tomato chutney.

Humorous as it sounds, this line captures a very common human tendency: we judge the same situation differently depending on who is affected.

This tendency becomes particularly dangerous in organizations when it appears in middle management.

A middle-level manager occupies one of the most difficult positions in any organization. He is expected to execute the expectations of senior management while simultaneously leading, protecting, guiding and developing the people working under him.

He stands between authority above and dependency below.

Handled well, this position creates enormous organizational value.

Handled poorly, it creates a culture of blame, distrust and helplessness.

The Convenient Blame Game

When something goes wrong, some managers instinctively begin searching for somebody to blame.

If senior management questions a delay, the answer may become:

“My team did not cooperate.”

“The junior engineer failed to follow up.”

“The staff did not complete the work in time.”

But when the same manager speaks to his juniors, the narrative suddenly changes:

“Management is not supporting us.”

“The senior officers don't understand the ground reality.”

“The system itself is the problem.”

Thus, the manager presents himself differently in two directions.

To seniors, the juniors become the problem.

To juniors, the seniors become the problem.

And conveniently, the manager himself remains somewhere in the middle — apparently innocent.

This may provide temporary protection, but it slowly destroys the very foundation of leadership.

Your Junior Depends on You

One fact every manager must understand is simple:

A junior reporting to you depends on you.

He depends on you for:

  • clarity of task,
  • priorities,
  • resources,
  • guidance,
  • technical direction,
  • coordination,
  • protection from unnecessary pressure,
  • access to decision-makers,
  • conflict resolution,
  • and sometimes even confidence.

Therefore, when a junior repeatedly fails, the manager must ask an uncomfortable question:

“What could I have done differently to enable this person to succeed?”

This does not mean juniors should never be held accountable.

Accountability is essential.

But accountability and abandonment are entirely different things.

A good manager asks:

Was the task clearly explained?

Were expectations measurable?

Did the person have the required resources?

Was progress reviewed early enough?

Did I intervene before the problem became a crisis?

Did I coach the employee when difficulties appeared?

Only after answering these questions honestly should the manager evaluate the individual's responsibility.

Leadership means accepting that the performance of your team is partly a reflection of your own leadership.

Never Sacrifice Your Team to Protect Yourself

One of the fastest ways for a manager to lose credibility is to publicly blame his subordinates before senior management.

Imagine a junior hearing his manager say:

“Sir, I had instructed him correctly. He alone did not complete it.”

Technically, the statement may even be true.

But psychologically, something important changes.

The junior realizes:

“When pressure comes, my manager will save himself first.”

From that moment onward, the relationship changes.

The employee becomes defensive.

Initiative reduces.

People begin keeping documentary evidence.

Emails become longer.

Copies to multiple people increase.

Every conversation becomes cautious.

People stop experimenting because they fear blame.

Eventually, the organization develops what may be called a defensive culture.

Everyone becomes interested in proving:

“It was not my mistake.”

Instead of asking:

“How do we solve the problem?”

But Blaming Senior Management Is Equally Dangerous

There is another side to the problem.

Some middle managers try to win popularity among their juniors by constantly criticizing senior management.

Statements such as:

“What can we do? Management won't approve anything.”

“They don't understand our problems.”

“Everything is political at the top.”

“This organization will never change.”

may temporarily make the manager appear sympathetic.

But the long-term effect is extremely harmful.

The junior begins wondering:

“If my own manager has no faith in the system, why should I?”

Slowly, confidence in the organization erodes.

Employees stop believing in procedures.

Instructions are treated cynically.

Rumours replace facts.

And organizational decisions begin to look like conspiracies rather than management choices.

A middle manager must understand that his words carry institutional weight.

When he repeatedly criticizes the system before his team, he is not merely sharing frustration.

He is weakening organizational trust.

The Middle Manager Is Not a Messenger — He Is a Translator

The role of middle management is often misunderstood.

A manager is not simply a courier carrying complaints upward and instructions downward.

A strong manager translates realities in both directions.

Upward, he translates operational reality.

Instead of saying:

“My team failed.”

he says:

“We underestimated the workload. We are restructuring the task and expect recovery by Friday.”

Notice the difference.

The manager accepts ownership while still describing reality.

Similarly, instead of hiding genuine team limitations, he communicates them professionally:

“The team currently lacks the required test support. With one additional resource, we can meet the target.”

That is not blaming.

That is management.

Downward, he translates organizational intent.

Instead of telling juniors:

“Management has given another impossible deadline.”

he can say:

“The organization has a critical commitment. The timeline is aggressive, so we need to reorganize priorities. Let us identify what can be deferred and what support we need.”

The message remains truthful.

But it creates purpose instead of resentment.

That is the difference between a manager and a commentator.

The Manager Must Absorb Noise and Transmit Clarity

A useful way to think about middle management is this:

Pressure comes from above. Problems come from below.

If the manager simply passes both pressures through unchanged, he adds little value.

The manager's job is to process them.

When pressure comes from above, he should convert it into:

  • priorities,
  • plans,
  • resources,
  • responsibilities,
  • and achievable milestones.

When difficulties come from below, he should convert them into:

  • facts,
  • risks,
  • alternatives,
  • recommendations,
  • and decisions required from higher management.

In other words:

A good middle manager absorbs noise and transmits clarity.

Replace Blame With Ownership

A mature manager gradually changes his vocabulary.

Instead of:

“They didn't do it.”

say:

“We could not complete it. Here is the recovery plan.”

Instead of:

“Management is not supporting us.”

say:

“Approval is pending. I am following it up and meanwhile we will proceed with the activities that are within our control.”

Instead of:

“This junior is useless.”

ask:

“Is this a skill problem, motivation problem, clarity problem or resource problem?”

Instead of:

“The system is bad.”

ask:

“What part of the system can I influence?”

Ownership does not mean blindly accepting responsibility for everything.

It means focusing first on what you can influence before blaming what you cannot control.

Protect Downward, Represent Upward

Perhaps one of the simplest principles of middle management is:

Protect downward. Represent upward.

Protecting downward does not mean hiding mistakes.

It means giving juniors the confidence that mistakes will be corrected professionally rather than converted into public humiliation.

Representing upward means communicating both achievements and difficulties honestly to senior management.

A strong manager can say:

“There was an error in our team. I take responsibility for correcting the process. We have identified the root cause and implemented these actions.”

That sentence demonstrates far more leadership than:

“The junior made a mistake despite my instructions.”

Interestingly, senior leaders often trust managers more when they demonstrate ownership.

Because ownership signals something important:

This person can be trusted with larger responsibilities.

Correct in Private, Credit in Public

Another powerful rule for managers is:

Correct in private. Credit in public.

When an employee makes a mistake, discuss it directly and clearly.

Explain the consequence.

Identify the cause.

Agree on corrective action.

But when the same employee does good work, give him visibility.

Mention his contribution in meetings.

Allow senior management to know who solved the problem.

A manager who steals credit and distributes blame may survive temporarily.

A manager who distributes credit and accepts responsibility builds something much more powerful:

loyalty.

And loyal teams perform far beyond what formal authority alone can achieve.

Do Not Become a Complaint Distribution Centre

Some managers unknowingly become a communication channel for negativity.

Senior management complains about employees.

Employees complain about senior management.

Other departments complain about everyone.

And the manager simply distributes these complaints in every direction.

That is not leadership.

Leadership requires filtering.

Before passing any negative information, ask:

Does the recipient need this information to take action?

If not, perhaps it should not be passed at all.

A manager must distinguish between:

information required for action

and

emotion seeking an audience.

The first should be communicated.

The second should often be absorbed, processed and transformed.

The Bridge Must Not Become the Battlefield

Middle managers are often described as the bridge between management and employees.

But there is an important extension to that metaphor.

A bridge connects two sides. It does not encourage the two sides to fight each other.

When a middle manager tells seniors everything wrong with juniors and tells juniors everything wrong with seniors, the bridge itself becomes the battlefield.

Eventually both sides lose confidence in him.

Senior management begins thinking:

“He cannot control his team.”

Juniors begin thinking:

“He will not stand with us when pressure comes.”

Once that happens, positional authority may remain, but leadership disappears.

The Right Approach

A balanced middle manager follows a few principles.

1. Take ownership before assigning blame.

Ask what you could have influenced.

2. Support juniors without protecting incompetence.

Coach, guide and enable them — but maintain accountability.

3. Represent senior management without becoming its blind spokesperson.

Understand the intent behind decisions and communicate it constructively.

4. Escalate facts, not emotions.

State the problem, impact, options and recommended solution.

5. Never undermine the organization before your own team.

Disagreement with senior management can be expressed upward professionally.

6. Never sacrifice your team merely to protect your image.

Your team will remember how you behave when things go wrong.

7. Build solutions, not narratives.

The question should not be:

“Whose fault is this?”

but rather:

“What caused this, what do we learn, and what do we do next?”

Leadership Begins Where Blame Ends

Anyone can manage when everything is going well.

The real character of a manager becomes visible when deadlines slip, mistakes occur, resources are inadequate and senior management begins asking difficult questions.

At that moment, there are two choices.

One manager begins searching for somebody to blame.

Another begins searching for a solution.

One says:

“Because of them, this happened.”

The other says:

“This happened. Here is what we are doing about it.”

The difference may appear small.

But organizations eventually recognize the second person as leadership material.

Because management positions may be given by designation.

Leadership is earned through ownership.

And perhaps this is where the phrase with which we began deserves to be reversed.

Instead of:

“Enakku vandhaa ratham; unakku vandhaa thakkali chutney.”

a mature manager should learn to think:

“If my team is bleeding, it is my concern too.”

Because the true measure of a middle manager is not how effectively he escapes blame.

It is how effectively he converts pressure into direction, mistakes into learning, conflict into alignment and ordinary people into a dependable team.

That is when a middle manager stops being merely the person between two levels of hierarchy and starts becoming the person capable of moving to the next level of leadership.
