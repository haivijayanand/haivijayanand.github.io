Multipliers

Liz Wiseman · first published 2010 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

The best leaders do not rely on their own brilliance; they amplify the intelligence and capability of the people around them.

Summary

Liz Wiseman spent years developing leaders at Oracle and noticed that some managers left their teams sharper and more energised, while others, often equally talented, left people drained and holding back. Her research, drawn from interviews and studies of well over a hundred leaders, sorts them into two types. Diminishers treat intelligence as scarce and assume people cannot work things out without them. Multipliers believe people are smart and will grow when stretched, and as a result they get far more out of the same team, roughly twice as much by her estimate.

She describes five disciplines that separate the two. Multipliers act as talent magnets, attracting capable people, using them at their full ability and helping them move on to bigger roles, whereas empire builders hoard staff and underuse them. Multipliers are liberators who create a safe but demanding space where people think and speak freely; tyrants create anxiety that shuts thinking down. Multipliers are challengers who set ambitious questions and let others find the answers, instead of know-it-alls who issue directions. They are debate makers who involve people in rigorous discussion before major decisions, rather than deciding alone. Finally, they are investors who hand over real ownership and hold people accountable, instead of micromanagers who take control back at the first sign of trouble.

Wiseman stresses that diminishing is often accidental. Well-meaning leaders who rescue people, jump in with ideas or talk with constant enthusiasm can quietly signal that others need not think.

The good news, she argues, is that multiplying is a learnable mindset rather than a fixed trait. Leaders can start by building on one existing strength, experimenting with a single new practice, asking questions more than giving answers, and noticing which of their own habits cause others to hold back.

295 words

One-line takeaway: Measure your leadership by how much capability you draw out of others, not by how smart you appear yourself.

    Answer C. Her research suggested Multipliers draw roughly double the capability from people compared with Diminishers.

    Answer A. Talent Magnets attract and fully use capable people, while Empire Builders hoard resources and underuse them.

    Answer D. Liberators remove fear while still asking for people's best thinking and work.

    Answer B. Challengers define opportunities and hard questions, whereas Know-It-Alls tell people what to do.

    Answer D. Good intentions such as rescuing people or offering constant ideas can still discourage others from thinking.

    Answer C. Wiseman presents multiplying as learnable, starting with small deliberate experiments.
