Parent Effectiveness Training

Thomas Gordon · first published 1970 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Parents and children get along best when parents give up power-based control and instead use real listening, honest statements about their own feelings, and joint problem solving in which nobody loses.

Summary

Thomas Gordon, a clinical psychologist, noticed that parents are blamed for their children's troubles yet almost never trained for the job. His book offers a set of communication skills that any parent can learn, built on the belief that children deserve the respect we give friends.

A key first step is working out who owns the problem. If a child is upset about something that does not directly affect the parent, such as a quarrel with a friend, the child owns it, and the parent's job is to help the child think it through. If the child's behaviour interferes with the parent's own needs, the parent owns the problem and must speak up.

When the child owns the problem, Gordon recommends active listening. Instead of advising, warning, judging or reassuring, which he lists among common roadblocks that shut conversation down, the parent reflects back what the child seems to be feeling. This shows acceptance, helps the child calm down, and often lets them find their own solution.

When the parent owns the problem, Gordon suggests I-messages rather than you-messages. Rather than blaming the child, the parent describes the behaviour, its concrete effect, and how they feel about it. Children are far more willing to change when they hear an honest statement than when they feel attacked.

For real conflicts of needs, Gordon rejects both the parent winning by force and the child winning by wearing the parent down. Each leaves someone resentful. He proposes a no-lose method: define the problem together, brainstorm solutions, weigh them, agree on one everyone accepts, carry it out, and check later how it is working.

He also argues that punishment and power lose their grip as children grow, and teach them to resist rather than to cooperate. Conflicts over values, such as clothes or friends, call for modelling and gentle influence rather than control.

309 words

One-line takeaway: This week, when something your child does bothers you, try one I-message: calmly name the behaviour, its effect on you, and how you feel, without any blame, and notice how they respond.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What observation about parents prompted Gordon's approach?

    Answer A. Gordon saw that parents are held responsible yet given almost no training in the skills they need.

  2. What is the purpose of deciding who owns the problem?

    Answer B. Problem ownership tells the parent whether to listen actively or to express their own needs.

  3. If a child is upset after a quarrel with a friend that does not affect the parent, who owns the problem?

    Answer C. When the issue troubles the child but does not interfere with the parent's needs, the child owns it.

  4. What does active listening involve?

    Answer D. Active listening mirrors the child's feelings so they feel understood and can work things out.

  5. Which of these does Gordon treat as a roadblock to communication?

    Answer A. Responses like warning, judging and advising tend to shut a child's conversation down.

  6. What does an I-message typically contain?

    Answer C. I-messages honestly describe the behaviour, its concrete effect and the parent's feeling without blame.

  7. Why does Gordon prefer I-messages to you-messages?

    Answer B. You-messages feel like attacks, while honest I-messages invite cooperation.

  8. What is wrong with both the parent-wins and child-wins ways of settling conflicts?

    Answer D. In both methods one side loses, which breeds resentment and resistance.

  9. Which step is part of the no-lose method?

    Answer C. The no-lose method has parent and child generate, weigh and agree on solutions together.

  10. How does Gordon suggest handling conflicts over values, such as clothes or friends?

    Answer A. Gordon argues values are best influenced by example and respectful talk, not by force.
