A differential equation is a rule that tells you how fast something changes, given where it is now. Learn to read equations that way, to build them from "rate in minus rate out", and to see them as a field of slopes that every solution must follow.
  • Read y′ = f(t, y) as a rule for change, not a puzzle to be solved
  • Build an equation from a balance: accumulation = in − out
  • Use the words order, linear, autonomous, initial value problem and solution correctly
  • Draw and read a slope field, and sketch solutions through it
  • Know when a solution exists, when it is unique, and when it can blow up

    A rule for change

    Most laws of nature do not tell you what a quantity is. They tell you how fast it changes. Newton did not say where a planet will be; he said its acceleration is set by the force on it. Fourier did not say how hot a rod is; he said heat flows from hot to cold at a rate set by the temperature gradient. A differential equation is exactly such a statement: it links a quantity to its own rate of change.

    y′ = dy/dt = f(t, y)
    In words: "the rate at which y changes right now depends on the time and on the value of y right now."

    Once you know the rule and where you start, the whole future is fixed — step forward a little at the rate the rule gives, look at the rule again, step again. That is the whole idea, and every method in this course is a more clever way of doing that walk.

    Building an equation: rate in minus rate out

    Almost every model you will ever write starts from one sentence, the balance law:

    Rate of accumulation = rate in − rate out + rate generated − rate consumed. It holds for mass, energy, money, people, heat, charge — anything you can count.

    Example — a tank with an outlet. Water flows in at Qin. It drains through a hole at the bottom; by Torricelli, the outflow is Qout = k·√h, faster when the water is deeper. The volume is A·h, so

    A · dh/dt = Qin − k·√h

    Read it before you solve it. When h is small the outflow is small, dh/dt > 0 and the tank fills. When h is large the outflow wins and the tank drains. Somewhere in between, in and out balance — that level h* = (Qin/k)² is an equilibrium. You know all of this without solving anything.

    Example — a mixing tank. A V-litre tank of brine is flushed with fresh water at Q litres per minute, perfectly mixed. The salt mass m leaves at the outflow concentration m/V: m′ = −(Q/V)·m. Same shape as decay, with time constant V/Q.

    The picture: a field of slopes

    The equation y′ = f(t, y) gives a slope at every point of the (t, y) plane. Draw a tiny line segment with that slope at a grid of points and you get a slope field (or direction field). A solution is a curve that is tangent to the field everywhere — like a leaf carried on a stream, it has no choice where to go.

    • Isoclines are curves where the slope is the same. The isocline f = 0 is special: there the solutions are momentarily flat, so maxima and minima of solutions sit on it.
    • Where the field is the same along every horizontal line, the equation is autonomous: shifting a solution in time gives another solution.
    • Different starting points give different curves, but in a well-behaved field curves never cross. If two did, the point of crossing would have two different futures.
    Existence, uniqueness and blow-up

    Picard–Lindelöf theorem. If f(t, y) and ∂f/∂y are continuous near (t₀, y₀), exactly one solution passes through that point, at least for a short time. The proof builds the solution as the limit of the iteration yk+1(t) = y₀ + ∫ f(s, yk(s)) ds, which is a contraction when ∂f/∂y is bounded.

    When uniqueness fails. y′ = 3·|y|2/3 with y(0) = 0 has the solution y = 0 and y = t³, and infinitely many that sit at zero for a while and then take off. Here ∂f/∂y = 2·|y|−1/3 is infinite at y = 0, so the theorem does not apply. Pick the last preset in the lab to see it.

    Blow-up in finite time. y′ = y², y(0) = 1 has the solution y = 1/(1 − t), which is infinite at t = 1. A smooth, innocent-looking f can still send solutions to infinity — the theorem only promises a solution for "a short time". Linear equations never do this.

    • Check units first. Every term in an equation must have the same units. In the tank, L/min had to become m³/min before dividing by A.

    Separable equations

    If the right-hand side splits into a function of t times a function of y, put all the y on one side and all the t on the other, then integrate both.

    dy/dt = g(t)·h(y) → ∫ dy / h(y) = ∫ g(t) dt + C

    Growth and decay. y′ = k·y → ∫dy/y = ∫k dt → ln|y| = k·t + C → y = y₀·ekt. For decay (k < 0) the half-life is t½ = ln 2/|k| ≈ 0.693/|k|.

    Logistic growth. y′ = r·y·(1 − y/K). Separating with partial fractions gives

    y(t) = K / (1 + (K/y₀ − 1)·e−rt)

    An S-curve: exponential at first, fastest at y = K/2, then flattening onto the capacity K. Sales of a new product, a spreading rumour, a culture of bacteria and the adoption of a new technology all trace S-curves of this kind.

    Linear equations and the integrating factor

    Every first-order linear equation can be written

    y′ + p(t)·y = q(t)

    Multiply by μ(t) = e∫p dt. The left side becomes the derivative of a product, (μ·y)′ = μ·q, because μ′ = p·μ. Integrate once and divide by μ:

    y(t) = (1/μ)·( ∫ μ·q dt + C ), μ = e∫p dt
    Example: y′ + 2y = e−t, y(0) = 0μ = e^(2t) → (y·e^(2t))′ = e^(2t)·e^(−t) = e^t y·e^(2t) = e^t + C, y(0) = 0 → C = −1 y = e^(−t) − e^(−2t) (rises, peaks at t = ln 2 = 0.693, then decays)
    Why linear equations are special: superposition

    For a linear equation L[y] = q, the difference of any two solutions solves L[y] = 0. So every solution = one particular solution + the general solution of the homogeneous equation. The homogeneous part carries the initial condition and dies away (if the system is stable) — it is the transient. The particular part follows the input — it is the steady state. And if inputs q₁ and q₂ give responses y₁ and y₂, input q₁ + q₂ gives y₁ + y₂. This one property is why Laplace transforms (Day 7), Fourier series (Day 12) and modal analysis (Day 8) work — and why none of them work for nonlinear systems.

    Equilibria and the phase line

    For an autonomous equation y′ = f(y) the rate depends only on where you are. Plot f(y) against y:

    • Where f(y) > 0, y increases — draw an arrow to the right on the y-axis.
    • Where f(y) < 0, y decreases — arrow to the left.
    • Where f(y*) = 0, nothing changes: y* is an equilibrium (fixed point, steady state).

    The y-axis with its arrows is the phase line. Solutions slide along it towards the next equilibrium in the arrow's direction and can never pass one. If the arrows on both sides point inwards the equilibrium is stable (an attractor); outwards, unstable (a repeller); same direction on both sides, half-stable.

    Test: f′(y*) < 0 → stable f′(y*) > 0 → unstable f′(y*) = 0 → look closer

    Linearisation: the local time constant

    Near an equilibrium write y = y* + η with η small. Taylor: f(y* + η) ≈ f(y*) + f′(y*)·η = f′(y*)·η. So

    η′ ≈ f′(y*)·η → η(t) ≈ η₀·ef′(y*)·t local time constant τ = −1/f′(y*)

    The slope of f at the crossing tells you how fast small disturbances die (or grow). A shallow crossing means a sluggish system that takes long to recover — an early warning that a tipping point is near ("critical slowing down").

    Logistic example. f(y) = r·y(1 − y/K): equilibria y* = 0 and y* = K. f′(0) = r > 0 → unstable (a tiny population grows). f′(K) = −r < 0 → stable, with recovery time 1/r.

    Bifurcations: when a parameter changes the picture

    Let f depend on a parameter p (a harvest rate, a load, a heating rate). As p changes, the graph of f moves up and down. Usually the equilibria just shift — but at special values they appear, disappear or swap stability. That qualitative change is a bifurcation. Plotting equilibria against p gives the bifurcation diagram.

    TypeNormal formWhat happens
    Saddle-node (fold)y′ = p + y²A stable and an unstable equilibrium approach, merge and vanish. The system falls off a cliff.
    Transcriticaly′ = p·y − y²Two equilibria pass through each other and exchange stability. A threshold: below it the disease dies out, above it the epidemic takes off.
    Pitchforky′ = p·y − y³One stable state splits into two symmetric ones. A straight column buckling left or right; a magnet choosing north or south.
    Why these three are the only common ones

    At a one-dimensional bifurcation f(y*) = 0 and f′(y*) = 0 at the same time. Expand f in a Taylor series about that point and keep the lowest-order terms that do not vanish: generically you get p + y² (the fold). Extra structure removes terms: if y = 0 is always an equilibrium (f(0, p) = 0 for all p) you get p·y − y² (transcritical); if the system is symmetric under y → −y, even powers vanish and you get p·y − y³ (pitchfork). Every bifurcation of y′ = f(y, p) "looks like" one of these near the critical point — that is the meaning of a normal form.

    Worked example

    Fishery with constant harvest: y′ = y·(1 − y/4) − p (fish stock y in thousands of tonnes, harvest p per year)Equilibria: y − y²/4 − p = 0 → y² − 4y + 4p = 0 → y* = 2 ± 2·√(1 − p) p = 0.5: y* = 0.586 (unstable) and 3.414 (stable) ← the fishery settles at 3.414 f′(y) = 1 − y/2: f′(3.414) = −0.707 → stable, recovery time ≈ 1/0.707 = 1.4 years f′(0.586) = +0.707 → unstable: below 0.586 the stock collapses p = 1: both merge at y* = 2 (saddle-node). p > 1: no equilibrium at all → extinction Maximum sustainable yield = 1 = r·K/4, but operating right at it leaves no margin: recovery time → ∞ as p → 1.

Also: lake eutrophication, ecosystem collapse, voltage collapse in power grids, bank runs, and the "click" of a snap-through switch.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • One-dimensional autonomous systems can never oscillate — solutions are monotonic. Oscillation needs at least two dimensions (Day 8 onward) or a time delay.

    Euler's method: follow the tangent

    Stand at (tn, yn). The equation gives the slope there. Walk a step h along that straight line:

    yn+1 = yn + h · f(tn, yn)
    tn+1 = tn + h

    Each step makes a small error because the true curve bends away from its tangent. Taylor's theorem says that local error is about (h²/2)·y″. Over a fixed interval you take T/h steps, so the global error is proportional to h: Euler is a first-order method. Halve h and the error halves — but the work doubles.

    Better slopes: Heun and Runge–Kutta

    The fix is to use a better average slope over the step.

    Heun (RK2, improved Euler):
    k₁ = f(tn, yn) k₂ = f(tn + h, yn + h·k₁)
    yn+1 = yn + (h/2)·(k₁ + k₂) global error ∝ h²

    Classic RK4:
    k₁ = f(tn, yn)
    k₂ = f(tn + h/2, yn + h·k₁/2)
    k₃ = f(tn + h/2, yn + h·k₂/2)
    k₄ = f(tn + h, yn + h·k₃)
    yn+1 = yn + (h/6)·(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄) global error ∝ h⁴

    RK4 is Simpson's rule in disguise: slopes at the start, twice in the middle, at the end, weighted 1 : 4 : 1. Four evaluations per step buy you a fourth-order method — every lab in this course runs on it.

    Systems and higher order

    Every method above works unchanged when y is a vector. And any higher-order equation becomes a first-order system by naming the derivatives:

    m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = F(t) → x′ = v, v′ = (F(t) − c·v − k·x)/m

    This is how every simulation package — Simulink, Modelica, a rotor-dynamics code, a flight simulator — sees a model: a state vector and a function that returns its rate of change.

    Stiffness: when small steps are forced on you

    Apply Euler to the test equation y′ = λ·y with λ < 0. Each step multiplies y by (1 + h·λ). The true solution decays, but if |1 + h·λ| > 1 the numbers grow and oscillate. Euler is stable only for

    h < 2 / |λ|

    A problem is stiff when it contains a very fast decaying component (large |λ|) alongside the slow behaviour you care about. The fast part is dead after a moment, yet it still forces tiny steps for ever. Chemical kinetics, circuits with small parasitic capacitances, and a thermocouple model coupled to a slow oven are typical.

    The cure is an implicit method. Backward Euler, yn+1 = yn + h·f(tn+1, yn+1), multiplies by 1/(1 − h·λ), which is below 1 for any h when λ < 0 — stable at any step. The price: an equation to solve each step. Stiff solvers (ode15s, BDF, Radau, LSODA) do exactly this.

    Local error, global error and why they differ by one order

    The local truncation error of a method of order p is C·hp+1 per step. Errors made early are carried forward and amplified (or damped) by the equation itself, roughly by eL·(T−t) where L bounds ∂f/∂y. Summing N = T/h such contributions gives a global error ≤ (C/L)·(eLT − 1)·hp. One power of h is "spent" on the number of steps. Note the eLT factor: for unstable or chaotic problems the global error grows exponentially with time no matter how good the method — the theme of Day 11.

    Worked example

    y′ = y, y(0) = 1. Estimate y(1) (exact e = 2.71828)Euler, h = 0.5: y₁ = 1 + 0.5·1 = 1.5, y₂ = 1.5 + 0.5·1.5 = 2.25 error 0.468 Euler, h = 0.25: 1.25, 1.5625, 1.9531, 2.4414 error 0.277 (≈ halved) RK4, h = 1 (one step): k₁ = 1, k₂ = 1 + 0.5 = 1.5, k₃ = 1 + 0.75 = 1.75, k₄ = 1 + 1.75 = 2.75 y₁ = 1 + (1 + 3 + 3.5 + 2.75)/6 = 2.7083 error 0.0099 One RK4 step (4 evaluations) beats four Euler steps by a factor of 28.
    Where you meet thisEvery simulation you run: rig controller models, rotor run-up simulations, battery state-of-charge estimators, orbit propagation, game physics engines (which often use simple "semi-implicit Euler" for speed), and the training of neural ODEs.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • At very small h the RK4 curve flattens out at ~10⁻¹⁴: that is round-off, not truncation. Smaller steps stop helping.
    • Always check a simulation by halving the step (or tightening the tolerance). If the answer changes in a digit you care about, it was not converged.
    • Energy-conserving problems (orbits, undamped oscillators) need symplectic methods (leapfrog, Verlet) for long runs; plain RK4 slowly loses energy.
    • If a simulation suddenly needs tiny steps somewhere, suspect stiffness or a discontinuity (a switch, a stop, friction stick-slip) — tell the solver about events.

    From Newton to the equation

    A mass m on a spring of stiffness k with a viscous damper c. Displace it by x. The spring pulls back with −k·x, the damper resists motion with −c·x′. Newton:

    m·x″ = −c·x′ − k·x ⇔ m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = 0
    Divide by m: x″ + 2ζωn·x′ + ωn²·x = 0, ωn = √(k/m), ζ = c / (2√(k·m))

    Two numbers describe everything: ωn, the natural frequency (how fast it wants to swing), and ζ, the damping ratio (what fraction of "critical" damping it has).

    The characteristic equation

    Guess x = est. Then x′ = s·x and x″ = s²·x, and the equation becomes algebra:

    m·s² + c·s + k = 0 → s = −ζωn ± ωn·√(ζ² − 1)
    CaseRootsMotion
    Under-damped ζ < 1Complex pair −ζωn ± j·ωd, with ωd = ωn·√(1 − ζ²)x = e−ζωn·t·(A·cos ωd·t + B·sin ωd·t): oscillates inside a decaying envelope
    Critically damped ζ = 1Repeated real −ωnx = (A + B·t)·e−ωn·t: fastest return with no overshoot
    Over-damped ζ > 1Two negative realsx = A·es₁t + B·es₂t: creeps back slowly
    Undamped ζ = 0±j·ωnPure sine for ever; energy ½kx² + ½mv² is constant

    The real part of the roots is the decay rate, the imaginary part the oscillation frequency. Plotting the roots in the complex plane is a complete summary of the motion — the idea behind Day 7's s-plane.

    Why est, and where the sines come from

    Exponentials are the only functions whose derivatives are multiples of themselves, so a linear constant-coefficient equation turns into a polynomial in s. Complex roots come in conjugate pairs s = σ ± jω, and Euler's formula ejωt = cos ωt + j·sin ωt turns the pair into real sines and cosines. The repeated-root case needs the extra t·est because the two exponentials coincide; you can get it as the limit of (es₁t − es₂t)/(s₁ − s₂) as s₂ → s₁.

    Phase plane: motion as a spiral

    Plot velocity against displacement. An undamped oscillator traces an ellipse — constant energy. Damping drains energy every cycle, so the ellipse shrinks into a spiral that ends at the origin. The phase plane will be our main tool from Day 9 onward; here is its first appearance.

    Logarithmic decrement. For an under-damped system, successive peaks shrink by the same factor:

    δ = ln(xn / xn+1) = 2πζ / √(1 − ζ²) → ζ = δ / √(4π² + δ²) ≈ δ / 2π for light damping
    Over N cycles: δ = (1/N)·ln(x0/xN)

    Worked example

    m = 1 kg, k = 40 N/m, c = 1.5 N·s/m (the lab's default)ωn = √(40/1) = 6.325 rad/s → fn = 1.007 Hz ζ = 1.5 / (2·√40) = 1.5/12.649 = 0.119 → under-damped ωd = 6.325·√(1 − 0.119²) = 6.280 rad/s Roots s = −0.75 ± 6.28j (−ζωn = −c/2m = −0.75) δ = 2π·0.119/0.993 = 0.750 → each peak is e^(−0.75) = 0.47 of the one before
    Tap test on a bracket: peak amplitude falls from 2.0 g to 0.5 g over 4 cyclesδ = ln(2.0/0.5)/4 = 1.386/4 = 0.347 ζ = 0.347 / √(4π² + 0.347²) = 0.055 (5.5 % of critical — typical of a bolted steel structure)
    Where you meet thisVehicle suspensions (ζ ≈ 0.2–0.4), instrument needles and accelerometers (ζ ≈ 0.7 for the flattest response), machine foundations, door closers (critically damped), building sway, torsional vibration of a gearbox drive line, and every RLC tuned circuit in a radio.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Use consistent units: k in N/m with m in kg gives ωn in rad/s. Divide by 2π for Hz.
    • Light damping hardly changes the frequency: at ζ = 0.1, ωd is 99.5 % of ωn. Damping shows up in the decay, not in the period.
    • Critical damping is not "best" for everything: it is fastest without overshoot, but a little overshoot (ζ ≈ 0.6–0.7) settles faster within a tolerance band.
    • Real structures are rarely viscous; friction and material damping are approximated by an "equivalent ζ" measured exactly as in the tap test.
