Maintenance Strategy, TPM, MTBF and MTTR

Keeping machines running: breakdown, preventive, predictive and proactive maintenance; MTBF, MTTR and availability; spares; the CMMS; and Total Productive Maintenance with autonomous maintenance by operators.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: Availability and downtime cost❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Choose between run-to-failure, time-based, condition-based and design-out maintenance
  • Calculate MTBF, MTTR and inherent availability
  • Explain the bathtub curve and why time-based replacement does not suit random failures
  • Describe the eight pillars of TPM and the steps of autonomous maintenance
  • Plan critical spares and a maintenance budget

2Maintenance strategies

StrategyWhen it fitsExample
Run to failure (breakdown)Cheap, non-critical, failure has no consequenceLight fittings, a spare fan
Preventive (time / usage based)Wear-out failures with a predictable lifeLubrication, filter change, doctor-blade change, belt replacement
Predictive (condition based)Failures with a warning period you can measure (Day 21)Bearings by vibration, gear oil by analysis, panels by thermography
Proactive / design-outRepeat failures; root cause in designBetter seals, guards against ink splash, relocated sensors
MTBF = operating time ÷ number of failures MTTR = total repair time ÷ number of repairs Inherent availability A = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR)
The bathtub and beyond. The classic bathtub has early (infant) failures, a flat random period and wear-out. Studies of complex equipment (Nowlan and Heap) found most failures are random or infant — so replacing parts on a fixed schedule often does not help and can even add infant failures. Condition monitoring and good installation practices work better for those.

3Total Productive Maintenance

TPM makes equipment care everyone's job, aiming at zero breakdowns, zero defects and zero accidents. The eight pillars: autonomous maintenance (operators clean, inspect, lubricate, tighten), planned maintenance, focused improvement (attack the six big losses, Day 3), quality maintenance, early equipment management, training, safety, health and environment, and TPM in offices.

  1. Initial clean and inspection — tag every abnormality.
  2. Eliminate sources of contamination and hard-to-reach places.
  3. Set cleaning, inspection and lubrication standards (CIL sheets).
  4. General inspection training for operators.
  5. Autonomous inspection to the standard.
  6. Standardise and organise the workplace.
  7. Full autonomous management and continuous improvement.

4Maintenance in a converting plant

What fails, and what to watchExtrusion: screw and barrel wear (output per rpm falls), heater bands and thermocouples, gearbox bearings, air-ring blower, die lips. Printing: doctor-blade holders and oscillation, impression roller rubber hardness and covers, drying-hood fans and burners, drives and encoders, register sensors. Lamination: coating and nip rollers, adhesive metering pumps. Slitting: knives and anvils, shafts, tension control. Pouch making: seal jaws and heaters, cutting knives, servo drives, cams. Utilities: compressors, chillers, cooling towers, boilers / thermic-fluid heaters, solvent recovery (Day 24). Keep critical spares: heater bands, thermocouples, encoders, drives and cards for the bottleneck.

5Worked example

Availability of a laminatorLast 6 months: 4 200 operating hours, 14 breakdowns, total repair 63 h (including waiting for spares 28 h) MTBF = 4 200 / 14 = 300 h MTTR = 63 / 14 = 4.5 h A = 300 / 304.5 = 98.5 % Stocking the two spares that caused the waits would cut MTTR to 2.5 h → A = 99.2 % At contribution ₹9 000 per laminator hour, 28 h of waiting cost ₹2.5 lakh — more than the spares.

6Setting up planned maintenance

  1. Make an asset register; rank each asset by criticality (safety, quality, bottleneck, cost of downtime).
  2. For critical assets list the failure modes and choose a strategy for each (Day 21's P–F thinking).
  3. Write PM and CIL sheets; load them into a CMMS (computerised maintenance management system) or a simple schedule.
  4. Record every breakdown: asset, failure mode, cause, repair time, waiting time, parts.
  5. Review MTBF, MTTR and the top-10 breakdowns monthly; design out repeat failures.
  6. Hold critical spares based on lead time and cost of downtime, not on habit.

7Lab: availability and downtime cost

Enter operating hours, number of failures, and repair and waiting times. See MTBF, MTTR, availability and the money lost; then test the effect of fewer failures or faster repairs.

8Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Separate repair time from waiting time — for spares, permits, the technician. Waiting is usually bigger.
  • Over-maintenance is real: every intervention can introduce a fault. Do what the failure mode needs.
  • Operators are the best sensors. Teach them what normal sounds, smells and looks like.
  • Managers: never cut PM to meet a month's output. You borrow from next month at high interest.
