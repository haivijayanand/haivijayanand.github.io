The Effective Executive

Peter F. Drucker · first published 1967 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Effectiveness is not talent or intelligence; it is a set of habits that any knowledge worker can learn.

Summary

Peter Drucker wrote this book for a new kind of worker: the person paid to think, decide and contribute rather than to follow instructions. He calls anyone in that position an executive, whether or not they manage people. His starting point is blunt. Intelligent, hard-working people are often ineffective, because nobody teaches them how to get the right things done. Effectiveness, he argues, is a practice, and it rests on five habits.

The first is to know where your time goes. Time is the scarcest resource and cannot be stored or replaced, yet most people guess wrongly about how they spend it. Drucker asks executives to record their time, cut activities that add nothing, and gather what remains into large uninterrupted blocks, since serious work cannot be done in fifteen-minute fragments.

The second is to focus on contribution. Instead of asking how busy they are, effective people ask what results the organisation needs from them. This outward view improves teamwork, communication and their own development.

The third is to build on strengths: your own, your colleagues' and your superiors'. Organisations should staff for what people do well and make their weaknesses irrelevant, not hunt for well-rounded people who excel at nothing.

The fourth is concentration. Doing first things first, and one thing at a time, means deliberately dropping yesterday's tasks that no longer earn their place. Choosing what not to do needs courage more than analysis.

The fifth is making effective decisions. Good executives make few decisions, but they treat each one as a matter of principle. They define the problem, set the conditions a solution must meet, listen to dissent, and build the action needed to carry it out.

The book's lasting lesson is that effectiveness can be learned and must be practised daily.

295 words

One-line takeaway: Record your time, ask what you contribute, use strengths, do one important thing at a time, and decide on principle.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. Whom does Drucker call an 'executive' in this book?

    Answer B. Drucker widens the term to every knowledge worker whose decisions and contribution shape the organisation's results, with or without subordinates.

  2. According to Drucker, what is the scarcest resource an executive has?

    Answer C. Time cannot be bought, stored or replaced, so managing it is the foundation of effectiveness.

  3. What does Drucker advise as the first step in managing time?

    Answer A. People misjudge where their time goes, so he starts with an honest record before cutting and consolidating.

  4. Why does Drucker want discretionary time gathered into large blocks?

    Answer D. Serious thinking and creative work cannot be completed in small, scattered fragments of time.

  5. The question 'What can I contribute?' mainly shifts an executive's attention towards:

    Answer B. Focusing on contribution turns attention outward to the results the organisation and its customers need.

  6. How should an organisation staff its positions, in Drucker's view?

    Answer C. Excellence comes from strengths; seeking people without weaknesses produces mediocrity.

  7. What does 'first things first' require besides choosing priorities?

    Answer D. Concentration needs planned abandonment of yesterday's activities, which frees capacity for what matters now.

  8. According to Drucker, effective executives make decisions that are:

    Answer A. They avoid deciding the same problem repeatedly by finding the underlying principle behind each decision.

  9. What role does disagreement play in effective decision-making?

    Answer C. Drucker holds that good decisions come from clashing opinions, not from early consensus.

  10. What is the book's central claim about effectiveness?

    Answer B. Drucker repeatedly stresses that effectiveness is a discipline built through habits anyone can practise.
