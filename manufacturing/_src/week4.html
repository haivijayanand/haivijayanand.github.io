    Lean principles

    1 · ValueDefined by the customer: what they would pay for.
    2 · Value streamMap every step (Day 2); remove what adds no value.
    3 · FlowMake the remaining steps flow without stops and queues.
    4 · PullMake only what the next step needs, when it needs it (Day 12).
    5 · PerfectionRepeat — there is always more waste to remove.

    The Toyota house: just-in-time and jidoka (stop and fix at the first defect) as the pillars, standardised work, levelled production (heijunka) and stability as the foundation, and people and teamwork at the centre.

    The eight wastes — TIMWOODS

    WasteShop-floor example
    TransportRolls moved to a far store and back between printing and lamination
    InventoryDays of WIP, obsolete printed stock
    MotionOperator walking to fetch tools, tape, knives, documents
    WaitingPress waiting for ink approval, cylinders, QC release
    OverproductionRunning extra metres "just in case"; making before the order
    Over-processingTighter tolerance or more colours than the customer needs; double inspection
    DefectsScrap, rework, sorting, complaints
    Skills (unused talent)Operators' ideas never asked for

    Overproduction is the worst: it creates all the others — inventory, transport, waiting space, hidden defects.

    SJapaneseDo

    5S around a printing pressSort: old cylinders, dried ink tins, unused doctor blades go.     Standard work and kaizen

    Standard work is the current best way to do a job: sequence of steps, time for each, and the standard WIP. Without a standard there is nothing to improve from — every change is just a different way. Kaizen is small, frequent improvement by the people who do the work; a kaizen event is a focused 3–5 day team effort on one area.

    Worked example

    Motion waste at a slitterSpaghetti chart over one hour: operator walked 840 m — to the core store (4 trips), knife cabinet, label printer, scale. Kaizen: core rack beside the unwind, knives on a shadow board at the machine, label printer and scale at the rewind. After: 210 m per hour. Time saved ≈ 9 min/h → 1.2 h per 8-h shift → ~15 % more slitting time.

    Starting lean in an area

    1. Pick one area with a visible problem and a willing team.
    2. Do 5S first — it builds discipline and shows quick results.
    3. Walk the area for waste with TIMWOODS; take photos.
    4. Write the standard work for the main job.
    5. Run a kaizen event on the biggest waste; measure before and after.
    6. Audit 5S weekly; review suggestions every week and implement fast.

    Lab: 5S audit radar

    Score an area from 0 to 5 on each S. The radar shows the shape; the weakest S is the next focus. Compare this month with last month.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • 5S is not housekeeping day. It is a standard that is kept every shift.
    • Lean tools copied without respect for people fail. Ask operators first.
    • Don't remove inventory before fixing the problems it hides (Day 10).
    • Managers: go to the floor (gemba) daily. Ask what is in the way; remove it.

    Practice

    1. Walk one area for 30 minutes and list examples of each of the eight wastes.
    2. Run a red-tag Sort in one cabinet or rack.
    3. Score your area on the 5S audit; pick the weakest S and act on it this week.

    Maintenance strategies

    StrategyWhen it fitsExample
    Run to failure (breakdown)Cheap, non-critical, failure has no consequenceLight fittings, a spare fan
    Preventive (time / usage based)Wear-out failures with a predictable lifeLubrication, filter change, doctor-blade change, belt replacement
    Predictive (condition based)Failures with a warning period you can measure (Day 21)Bearings by vibration, gear oil by analysis, panels by thermography
    Proactive / design-outRepeat failures; root cause in designBetter seals, guards against ink splash, relocated sensors
    MTBF = operating time ÷ number of failures MTTR = total repair time ÷ number of repairs Inherent availability A = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR)
    The bathtub and beyond. The classic bathtub has early (infant) failures, a flat random period and wear-out. Studies of complex equipment (Nowlan and Heap) found most failures are random or infant — so replacing parts on a fixed schedule often does not help and can even add infant failures. Condition monitoring and good installation practices work better for those.

    Total Productive Maintenance

    TPM makes equipment care everyone's job, aiming at zero breakdowns, zero defects and zero accidents. The eight pillars: autonomous maintenance (operators clean, inspect, lubricate, tighten), planned maintenance, focused improvement (attack the six big losses, Day 3), quality maintenance, early equipment management, training, safety, health and environment, and TPM in offices.

    1. Initial clean and inspection — tag every abnormality.
    2. Eliminate sources of contamination and hard-to-reach places.
    3. Set cleaning, inspection and lubrication standards (CIL sheets).
    4. General inspection training for operators.
    5. Autonomous inspection to the standard.
    6. Standardise and organise the workplace.
    7. Full autonomous management and continuous improvement.

    Maintenance in a converting plant

    What fails, and what to watchExtrusion: screw and barrel wear (output per rpm falls), heater bands and thermocouples, gearbox bearings, air-ring blower, die lips. Printing: doctor-blade holders and oscillation, impression roller rubber hardness and covers, drying-hood fans and burners, drives and encoders, register sensors. Lamination: coating and nip rollers, adhesive metering pumps. Slitting: knives and anvils, shafts, tension control. Pouch making: seal jaws and heaters, cutting knives, servo drives, cams. Utilities: compressors, chillers, cooling towers, boilers / thermic-fluid heaters, solvent recovery (Day 24). Keep critical spares: heater bands, thermocouples, encoders, drives and cards for the bottleneck.

    Worked example

    Availability of a laminatorLast 6 months: 4 200 operating hours, 14 breakdowns, total repair 63 h (including waiting for spares 28 h) MTBF = 4 200 / 14 = 300 h MTTR = 63 / 14 = 4.5 h A = 300 / 304.5 = 98.5 % Stocking the two spares that caused the waits would cut MTTR to 2.5 h → A = 99.2 % At contribution ₹9 000 per laminator hour, 28 h of waiting cost ₹2.5 lakh — more than the spares.

    Setting up planned maintenance

    1. Make an asset register; rank each asset by criticality (safety, quality, bottleneck, cost of downtime).
    2. For critical assets list the failure modes and choose a strategy for each (Day 21's P–F thinking).
    3. Write PM and CIL sheets; load them into a CMMS (computerised maintenance management system) or a simple schedule.
    4. Record every breakdown: asset, failure mode, cause, repair time, waiting time, parts.
    5. Review MTBF, MTTR and the top-10 breakdowns monthly; design out repeat failures.
    6. Hold critical spares based on lead time and cost of downtime, not on habit.

    Lab: availability and downtime cost

    Enter operating hours, number of failures, and repair and waiting times. See MTBF, MTTR, availability and the money lost; then test the effect of fewer failures or faster repairs.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Separate repair time from waiting time — for spares, permits, the technician. Waiting is usually bigger.
    • Over-maintenance is real: every intervention can introduce a fault. Do what the failure mode needs.
    • Operators are the best sensors. Teach them what normal sounds, smells and looks like.
    • Managers: never cut PM to meet a month's output. You borrow from next month at high interest.

    Practice

    2. Write a CIL sheet for one machine with its operators.
    3. List the five spares whose absence would stop the plant longest.

    The P–F curve

    Most failures give warning. P is the point where a developing failure can first be detected; F is functional failure. The time between them is the P–F interval. To catch the failure, you must inspect more often than the P–F interval — usually at half of it or less.

    Inspection interval ≤ (P–F interval − time needed to plan and act) ÷ n (n ≈ 2 for one good chance, more for confidence)
    TechniqueDetectsTypical warning
    Vibration analysisUnbalance, misalignment, looseness, bearing and gear defectsWeeks to months
    Oil / wear-debris analysisWear metals, contamination, water, oil degradationWeeks to months
    UltrasoundEarly bearing lubrication problems, leaks, electrical dischargeWeeks
    Infrared thermographyLoose electrical joints, overloaded circuits, bearing heat, insulationDays to weeks
    Motor current signatureBroken rotor bars, eccentricity, load problemsWeeks
    Human senses (noise, heat, smell)Late-stage problemsHours to days

    Vibration basics

    Read the frequency, not just the level. 1× running speed dominant → unbalance; 2× and high axial → misalignment; many harmonics → looseness; non-integer high frequencies → rolling-bearing defects (BPFO, BPFI, BSF, FTF from bearing geometry); gear-mesh frequency (teeth × speed) with sidebands → gear wear or eccentricity. Trend the overall velocity (mm/s RMS, 10–1000 Hz) and compare with ISO 20816 zones: for medium machines on rigid foundations roughly A < 1.4, B < 2.8, C < 4.5, D above (alarm and trip levels depend on machine class).

    Condition monitoring in converting

    Rotating machines everywhereExtruder gearboxes (vibration and oil analysis — the costliest single failure), extruder and air-ring blower motors, drying-hood fans on gravure and laminator (unbalance from ink build-up), chill rolls and idlers (bearing defects), cooling-tower fans and pumps, air compressors and chillers. Thermography of panels and drives every quarter; heater bands and thermocouple connections on extruders. Route-based monthly vibration readings with a portable analyser are a cheap start; permanent sensors on the extruder gearbox and the press main drive come next, feeding the plant data system (Day 27).

    Worked example

    Inspection interval for a fan bearingExperience: vibration first rises about 8 weeks before the bearing fails (P–F ≈ 56 days). Planning a bearing change takes 7 days (part, shutdown slot). Interval ≤ (56 − 7) / 2 ≈ 24 days → a monthly route gives only one look inside the window; set 3-weekly readings for two. Bearing 6312 at 1480 rpm: BPFO ≈ 3.08 × 24.7 Hz ≈ 76 Hz — watch for peaks there and its harmonics.

    Starting a programme

    1. List critical rotating machines (Day 20's criticality ranking).
    2. For each, mark measurement points (bearing housings, three directions) with studs or labels.
    3. Take baseline readings when the machine is healthy; record speed and load.
    4. Set alarm levels from ISO 20816 and from your own baseline (e.g. 2× baseline).
    5. Run routes at intervals shorter than half the P–F interval; trend every reading.
    6. When a trend rises, diagnose (spectrum), plan the repair, and check the removed part — it teaches you the P–F interval.

    Lab: P–F interval

    The curve shows a machine's condition falling from P to F. Set the P–F interval and your inspection interval, then press P: each tick is an inspection. See how often the defect is caught in time over many random failure starts.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Trend beats absolute. A machine at 3 mm/s that has been 3 mm/s for five years is healthier than one that went from 1 to 2.5 in a month.
    • Always record speed and load with a reading — vibration changes with both.
    • Collecting data nobody analyses is the most common failure of CM programmes.
    • Managers: value CM by the failures it prevented, with the cost of each. Keep that list.

    Practice

    1. List five rotating machines whose failure would stop your bottleneck.
    2. Estimate the P–F interval for their main failure mode and set an inspection interval.
    3. Take a baseline vibration reading on one of them.

    Hazard, risk and the hierarchy of controls

    Risk = likelihood × severity (each rated 1–5 → risk 1–25)
    1. Eliminate the hazard (no solvent: water-based or solvent-less adhesive).
    2. Substitute something less hazardous (lower-toxicity solvent; lower voltage).
    3. Engineering controls — guards, interlocks, local exhaust, enclosures, static eliminators.
    4. Administrative controls — procedures, permits, training, signage, rotation.
    5. PPE — gloves, glasses, shoes, respirators, hearing protection. Last line, not first.
    Heinrich's pyramid (and its lesson). For every serious injury there are many minor injuries and hundreds of near misses and unsafe acts. The ratios are debated, but the lesson holds: report and act on near misses, because they share causes with the serious accidents.

    Key controls

    ControlWhat it is
    Lockout–tagout (LOTO)Isolate every energy source (electrical, pneumatic, hydraulic, stored, gravity), lock with personal locks, tag, verify zero energy before work
    Machine guardingFixed guards on nips, gears, belts; interlocked guards that stop the machine when opened; light curtains; two-hand controls
    Permit to workWritten authorisation for hot work, confined spaces, work at height, electrical work
    Emergency preparednessFire detection and suppression, drills, exits, first aid, spill kits
    Incident investigationEvery injury and near miss analysed for root cause (Day 17)

    In India the Factories Act 1948 and state rules (being replaced by the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code 2020 as it is notified) set duties for guarding, hazardous processes, safety officers and committees; the Petroleum and explosives rules and fire regulations govern solvent storage. Check the current law and your state's rules with a qualified safety professional.

    Converting-plant hazards

    In-running nips, solvents and staticNip points between rollers are the classic converting injury — hands drawn in while threading or cleaning a running web. Controls: nip guards, inch-only threading speed, crawl speed with hold-to-run, never clean running rolls. Flammable solvents (ethyl acetate, toluene-free inks, IPA, MEK) in gravure and solvent-based lamination: keep vapour below 25 % of the lower explosive limit (LEL) in dryers with LEL monitors and ventilation interlocks; earth and bond drums, pumps and presses; flameproof electrics in classified zones; closed bins for solvent rags; limited quantities at the press. Static electricity on fast-moving films ignites solvent vapour: static eliminators, humidity, earthing. Also: roll handling and falling rolls, knife cuts in slitting and pouch making, hot surfaces of sealing jaws and dies, noise from extruders and granulators, and confined spaces in solvent tanks.

    Worked example

    Risk assessment: cleaning an impression rollerHazard: in-running nip of impression roller and cylinder; solvent-soaked cloth Current: done at crawl speed by habit, no guard Likelihood 4, Severity 5 → risk 20 (high) Controls: (1) engineering — interlocked nip guard; cleaning only with drive locked out or with a dedicated cleaning mode at ≤ 5 m/min, hold-to-run; (2) admin — SOP, training; (3) PPE — solvent-resistant gloves Residual: Likelihood 1, Severity 5 → 5 (low).

    Running a HIRA

    1. List activities, routine and non-routine (cleaning, threading, maintenance, changeovers, emergencies).
    2. For each, identify hazards with the people who do it.
    3. Rate current risk (likelihood × severity) with existing controls.
    4. Add controls in hierarchy order until the risk is acceptable; assign owners and dates.
    5. Re-rate residual risk; update SOPs and training.
    6. Review after every incident, change and at least yearly.

    Lab: risk matrix

    A list of hazards with likelihood and severity before and after controls. Edit the ratings; the 5 × 5 matrix places each hazard and shows how far the controls move it. Shading is by risk band (low, medium, high).

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Never bypass an interlock to "save time" — make the safe way the easy way instead.
    • Count near misses as a good sign of reporting, not a bad mark against an area.
    • Changeovers and maintenance are where most injuries happen, not normal running.
    • Managers: walk the floor and ask "what could hurt you here?" Act on the answer within a week.

    Work study

    Method study finds the best way to do a job (record — examine — develop — install — maintain). Work measurement finds how long the best way should take for a qualified worker at a normal pace. Always improve the method before you time it.

    Normal time = observed time × rating ÷ 100 Standard time = normal time × (1 + allowances) Allowances: personal needs, fatigue, contingency — typically 10–20 %
    TechniqueUse
    Stopwatch time studyRepetitive manual work; time several cycles, rate the pace
    Predetermined motion time systems (MTM, MOST)Set times from standard motion data — before the job exists
    Work samplingRandom observations to estimate % of time on each activity — good for long, varied jobs
    Standard data / synthesisBuild times from elements already measured

    Manpower and productivity

    Operators needed = Σ (standard time × quantity) ÷ (available time × expected efficiency) Labour productivity = output ÷ labour hours (e.g. kg per man-hour, pouches per man-hour)

    In machine-paced work the machine sets the cycle; the question is how many machines one person can tend (machine interference). In manual work the standard time sets the staffing directly.

    People in a converting plant

    Crew sizes, skills and shiftsMachine-paced jobs: a blown-film line needs 1–2 operators and a helper; a gravure press an operator, an assistant and an ink-kitchen share; a laminator one or two; a slitter one; pouch machines one operator per 1–2 machines. Manual jobs with real standard times: packing pouches into cartons, quality sorting, core cutting, roll wrapping and labelling — here time study pays. The skill matrix (operators × machines, rated 0–4: not trained, under training, can run with help, independent, can train others) is the key planning tool: it shows who can cover absence and where a single skilled operator is a risk. A gravure printer takes 2–3 years to become fully competent; plan successors.

    Worked example

    Packing pouches into cartonsObserved mean per carton of 1 000 pouches (count, bundle, pack, label): 2.40 min, rating 110 % Normal time = 2.40 × 1.10 = 2.64 min; allowances 15 % → standard time = 3.04 min/carton Demand: 9 lakh pouches/day = 900 cartons → 2 736 min of work Available: 450 min/shift × 85 % expected efficiency = 382 min → 7.2 operator-shifts/day → 8 packers over 3 shifts.

    Doing a time study

    1. Agree the method, explain the purpose to the worker and supervisor — openly.
    2. Break the job into elements with clear start and end points.
    3. Time at least 10–20 cycles with a qualified, experienced worker; rate the pace each cycle.
    4. Calculate normal time per element; add allowances; sum to standard time.
    5. Check against output records; discuss with the team.
    6. Re-study when the method, equipment or product changes.

    Lab: standard time calculator

    Enter the observed time, rating and allowances to get standard time; then demand and shift data to get the number of operators.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Time study is not a weapon. Used to squeeze people, it gets gamed; used to plan and improve, it helps everyone.
    • Rating is a skill; train raters together on films of known pace.
    • Don't standardise a bad method — fix the method first (Day 19).
    • Managers: a skill matrix with one "4" per machine is a single point of failure. Fund cross-training.

    Practice

    1. Time a manual job for 10 cycles and calculate its standard time.
    2. Build the skill matrix of one department.
    3. Calculate the labour productivity of your plant in kg (or pieces) per man-hour for the last 12 months.

    Where the energy goes

    Specific energy consumption (SEC) = energy used ÷ good output (kWh/kg, kWh/1 000 pieces, MJ/t)

    Track SEC by machine and by month. It rises when OEE falls (machines idle hot), when scrap rises (energy wasted on bad product) and when utilities leak. A plant that never measures SEC usually wastes 10–20 % of its energy.

    UtilityTypical lossesFixes
    Compressed air20–30 % lost in leaks; pressure set too high; open blowingLeak surveys, lower pressure, zero-loss drains, VSD compressors
    Motors, fans, pumpsThrottled valves and dampers; oversized motorsVariable-speed drives (VSD), IE3/IE4 motors, correct sizing
    Heating (dryers, ovens, heaters)Hot air exhausted; poor insulation; idle heatingRecirculation, heat recovery, insulation, standby modes
    Cooling (chillers, towers)Chiller at low set-point; fouled condensersRaise set-point, free cooling, clean, VSD pumps
    Electricity tariffLow power factor; peak demand penaltiesCapacitor banks / APFC, load scheduling
    Affinity laws (fans, pumps): flow ∝ speed, pressure ∝ speed², power ∝ speed³ → 20 % less speed ≈ 49 % less power

    Energy in converting

    Extruder heat, dryer air and solventBlown-film extrusion: typically 0.35–0.6 kWh/kg (motor, heaters, blower, chiller) — track it by line. Insulate barrel and die, use VSD on blowers, IBC cooling with chilled water. Gravure printing and solvent-based lamination: drying hoods move huge volumes of hot air; much of the energy goes out of the exhaust. Recirculate dryer air up to the LEL limit (Day 22) with LEL control, recover heat from exhaust, and treat the exhaust with solvent recovery (activated carbon adsorption and distillation — reclaimed ethyl acetate goes back into the inks) or a regenerative thermal oxidiser (RTO) whose heat is reused for the dryers. Compressed air for pneumatic chucks, splicers and pouch machines; chillers for chill rolls and IBC; thermic-fluid heaters for lamination.

    Worked example

    Compressed-air leaksLeak survey with an ultrasonic detector: 40 leaks, average equivalent hole 1 mm at 7 bar g Flow per leak ≈ 2.7 Nm³/h (≈ 45 L/min) → 108 Nm³/h total Compressor SEC ≈ 0.11 kWh/Nm³ → 11.8 kW → × 8 000 h ≈ 94 700 kWh/yr × ₹8 ≈ ₹7.6 lakh/yr Cost to fix: ultrasonic detector + a technician-week + fittings ≈ ₹1.5 lakh. Payback < 3 months.

    An energy audit

    1. Collect 12 months of bills (power, fuel, water) and production; plot energy against output.
    2. Meter the big users: extruders, dryers, compressors, chillers, heaters, lighting.
    3. Calculate SEC by machine and a baseline for the plant.
    4. Walk the plant at a weekend or shutdown: what is still running? Listen for air leaks.
    5. List measures with savings, cost and payback (Day 29); do the quick ones first.
    6. Put SEC on the monthly dashboard and the energy KPI into the plant review.

    Lab: compressed-air leak cost

    Set the number of leaks, their size, the pressure, running hours and tariff. See the air lost, the compressor power it takes and the money per year. Also try lowering the system pressure.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Compressed air is the most expensive utility — about 8–10 kW of electricity for 1 kW of air power at the tool.
    • Every 1 bar less system pressure saves about 6–7 % of compressor power.
    • A machine idling hot between jobs uses energy for nothing — add standby modes to the changeover standard.
    • Managers: meter by department. What is not measured is not managed — and nobody owns it.

    Practice

    1. Calculate your plant's SEC for each of the last 12 months.
    2. Do a leak walk with a soap solution or an ultrasonic detector on one Sunday.
    3. Find one fan or pump with a throttled valve or damper and estimate the VSD saving.
