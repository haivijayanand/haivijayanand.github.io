When Objectives Are Unclear, the Other Side Always Looks Greener

28 Nov 20265 min readSelf-GrowthPhilosophy

Clarity of purpose is perhaps one of the greatest contributors to contentment in life.

Whenever our objectives are not clear, we tend to look at others. We observe their progress, achievements, relationships, possessions and choices. Gradually, we begin questioning our own decisions and wondering whether life would have been better had we taken a different path.

The other side of the river always appears more attractive, particularly when we do not know what we want to accomplish on our own side.

This phenomenon is not restricted to any particular age group, profession or stage of life. It is a fundamental human tendency that manifests itself in different forms throughout our lives.

Different Ages, Same Dilemma

A school student compares marks, talents and popularity with classmates. A college student questions whether choosing a different institution or course would have provided better opportunities.

A young professional compares salary, designation and career growth with peers. Someone in their forties may begin questioning their professional achievements, financial position, marriage or personal relationships.

Even after retirement, people may compare their accumulated wealth, children's achievements, social recognition and quality of life with others.

The subjects of comparison keep changing with age, but the underlying human tendency remains remarkably similar.

When we do not have our own yardstick for success, we unknowingly borrow someone else's.

In the Professional Space

In an organisation, two individuals may begin their careers at the same time but progress along entirely different paths.

One may pursue technical excellence, another administrative authority, another financial growth, and yet another a comfortable work-life balance.

Each path has its own rewards, sacrifices and challenges.

However, when our professional objectives are unclear, we may desire the authority of one colleague, the recognition of another, the freedom of a third and the financial benefits of someone else.

We want the advantages of multiple paths without necessarily being prepared to make the sacrifices associated with each of them.

Consequently, even a reasonably successful career may appear disappointing.

The problem may not always be the career itself, but the absence of clarity about what we expect from it.

In Our Personal Space

The same tendency extends into our personal lives.

We compare our homes, lifestyles, friendships, marriages, children and family relationships with those of others.

We see someone enjoying greater freedom, someone receiving more affection, someone travelling frequently and someone appearing financially prosperous.

What we see, however, is only a visible portion of their lives. We rarely understand their struggles, compromises, responsibilities and emotional burdens.

Sometimes, we begin undervaluing what we already possess simply because someone else possesses something different.

We may even disturb an otherwise peaceful life in pursuit of an imagined better one.

The Problem of Constantly Changing Objectives

Many of our decisions were made based on the circumstances, priorities, knowledge and resources available to us at that particular point in time.

Years later, when our circumstances change, we evaluate those decisions using an entirely different set of parameters.

Someone who once prioritised job security may later regret not pursuing entrepreneurship. Someone who prioritised financial growth may later regret not spending enough time with family. Someone who chose freedom may subsequently long for stability.

Changing priorities is a natural part of growing older. But repeatedly judging past decisions through present-day desires can create unnecessary dissatisfaction.

We must distinguish between a genuinely wrong decision and a decision whose relevance has changed with time.

Comparison Without Context

There is nothing inherently wrong with observing others. Comparison can inspire us, expose us to new possibilities and help us identify areas for improvement.

The difficulty begins when comparison replaces purpose.

Instead of asking ourselves:

  • What do I genuinely want?
  • What are my priorities at this stage of life?
  • What am I willing to sacrifice?
  • What is sufficient for me?
  • What gives me a sense of fulfilment?

We begin asking why others have something that we do not.

These are fundamentally different approaches to life. One promotes self-awareness and growth; the other, when left unchecked, promotes insecurity and dissatisfaction.

Clarity Does Not Mean Lack of Ambition

Being content with our decisions does not mean that we should stop growing, exploring opportunities or correcting mistakes.

Neither does clarity of purpose mean that we must remain committed to a decision that no longer serves us.

We can change careers, reconsider relationships, redefine priorities and discover new ambitions. But such changes should ideally arise from an understanding of our own needs rather than merely from observing the apparent happiness of others.

There is a significant difference between moving towards something we genuinely desire and moving away from something simply because another person's life appears more attractive.

Define Your Own Parameters of Success

As we grow through different stages of life, it is worthwhile to periodically examine what we truly value.

For some, success may mean wealth. For others, it may mean professional contribution, intellectual satisfaction, family bonding, independence, good health, social service or peace of mind.

There is no universally correct combination.

Once our objectives become reasonably clear, decisions become easier to evaluate. We become better equipped to accept trade-offs, appreciate our achievements and acknowledge that we cannot have everything simultaneously.

We also become capable of appreciating another person's success without interpreting it as our own failure.

The Other Side of the River

Perhaps the other side of the river is indeed greener. There is no necessity to convince ourselves otherwise.

But is that where we genuinely want to be? Are we prepared for the journey, the sacrifices and the uncertainties associated with crossing the river? Or are we attracted merely because it is different from where we currently stand?

These are questions worth asking at every stage of life.

Happiness may not necessarily come from being on the greener side of the river. It may come from knowing why we have chosen to stand where we are, what we intend to accomplish, and when it is genuinely necessary to move.

When the destination is unclear, every passing boat appears to be heading somewhere better. When the destination is clear, we can appreciate other journeys without regretting our own.
