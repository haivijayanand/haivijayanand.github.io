Prepress: from film to PDF

Between a finished design and a running press lies prepress: separating colours, making halftone dots, checking files, imposing pages and making plates. For a century it was a craft of cameras, film and darkrooms. Software turned it into PostScript output to film, then direct-to-plate imaging, and finally a PDF-based, colour-managed, automated workflow.

01The requirement: turning artwork into plates

A printing press cannot print a photograph or a smooth tint directly. Offset, flexo and gravure lay down ink or no ink, so every grey and every colour must be broken into dots, and every colour into separate plates. Before computers this meant:

Process camera with glass halftone screen→Colour filters for C, M, Y, K separations→Hand retouching on film→Stripping film onto flats for imposition→Contact plates under UV lamps

Electronic drum scanners (Hell, Crosfield) from the 1960s–70s, and the high-end colour electronic prepress systems of the 1980s (Scitex, Crosfield, Hell), automated much of this — but they were closed, proprietary and cost millions. Only large trade shops could own them.

04PDF and PDF/X: one file that prints the same everywhere

PostScript files were programs: the result depended on fonts, settings and the RIP that ran them, and errors appeared only at output. Adobe's PDF (1993) was a page description that had already been "run" — fixed, self-contained and viewable. Printers wanted a strict subset that guaranteed printable files, which became PDF/X (ISO 15930).

StandardYearMain ruleTypical use

An output intent embedded in the file states which printing condition (for example FOGRA51 or GRACoL) the colours were prepared for.

06Colour management and proofing

  • ICC (1993): founded by Adobe, Agfa, Apple, Kodak, Microsoft, Silicon Graphics, Sun and Taligent to standardise how devices describe colour. An ICC profile maps a device's colours to a device-independent space (CIELAB), so files can be converted between monitor, proofer and press.
  • Print standards: ISO 12647 sets target values for each printing process; characterisation data such as FOGRA39/FOGRA51 (Europe) and GRACoL/G7 (USA) describe a "standard" press so printers can match it.
  • Spot colours: the Pantone Matching System (1963) gives designers a shared book of mixed ink colours; today spectral data (PantoneLIVE and similar) replaces printed swatch books in packaging workflows.
  • Proofs: analogue proofs made from the films (DuPont Cromalin, 3M Matchprint) gave way to inkjet contract proofs driven by colour-managed proofing RIPs (GMG, EFI and others), and to calibrated-monitor soft proofs.
  • Measurement: spectrophotometers and press-side scanning systems check colour bars against tolerance (ΔE).
For the extended colour gamut approach — printing with fixed CMYK+OGV inks instead of mixed spot colours, especially in gravure — see the separate paper Extended Color Gamut in gravure printing in Technical Papers.

09What retired

  • Process cameras, stripping tables and film — first replaced by imagesetter film, then removed by CTP for most offset printing.
  • Proprietary colour prepress systems — replaced by Macs, PCs and PostScript in the early 1990s.
  • Analogue film proofs — replaced by inkjet contract proofs.
  • PostScript as an exchange format, and OPI image swapping — replaced by self-contained PDF/X files.
  • PDF/X-1a — still accepted, but PDF/X-4 is now recommended because flattening transparency caused errors.

In India, commercial print centres — Sivakasi, Chennai, Mumbai, Delhi and others — moved from film to CTP over the last two decades, and CTP has become the norm for offset; film and screen-based methods linger in screen printing and some small shops.

11Lessons for engineers

  1. Check before you commit expensive resources. Preflight is to printing what design review is to manufacturing.
  2. Standardise the interface between organisations. PDF/X let any designer send to any printer.
  3. Remove intermediate steps. Every film stage removed by CTP removed a source of dust, scratches and register error.
  4. Measure, don't guess. Colour management replaced "it looks right to me" with numbers and tolerances.
