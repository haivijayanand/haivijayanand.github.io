The Story

Nan-in, a Japanese Zen master, received a visit from a university professor who had come to learn about Zen. The professor talked at length — about what he had read, what he believed, and what he expected to hear.

Nan-in served tea. He poured his visitor’s cup full, and then kept on pouring. Tea ran over the rim, across the saucer and onto the floor.

The professor watched until he could restrain himself no longer. “It is overfull! No more will go in!”

Nan-in set down the pot.

“Like this cup, you are full of your own opinions. How can I show you Zen unless you first empty your cup?”

Reflection

In Story 01 the cup spilled what was already inside it; this one cannot take in anything new. Knowledge is a fine thing, but it quietly fills the cup. The more we know, the more we listen only to confirm, and the less we actually hear.

Emptying the cup does not mean throwing away experience. It means setting it aside for a moment — in a meeting, with a junior colleague, with your child — and receiving what is said as if for the first time. You can always pour your own tea back afterwards.

Carry this

Empty the cup before you ask to have it filled.
