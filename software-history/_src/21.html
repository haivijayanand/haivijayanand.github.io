01The requirement: get ideas out of your head and into order

People have drawn branching diagrams for centuries — the "tree of Porphyry" in logic textbooks, family trees, the branching classifications of naturalists. In the 1970s the British author Tony Buzan popularised the mind map: a central topic with curved branches, keywords and colours, promoted for note-taking and memory. In the same decade the educator Joseph Novak developed concept maps, where labelled arrows state the relationships between ideas. In computing, Douglas Engelbart's 1968 "Mother of All Demos" had already shown a hierarchical outline editor.

02Outliners: ideas as an indented tree

Dave Winer's ThinkTank (Living Videotext, 1983–84) brought outlining to personal computers; its successor MORE (1986) could turn an outline into presentation charts. Outlining then became a feature of word processors — Word's Outline view, still there today — and of PowerPoint, whose slide titles are an outline. Winer later created OPML (2000), the XML outline format still used to exchange outlines and podcast subscriptions.

In the 2010s web outliners revived the idea: Workflowy (2010) made the whole of one's life a single infinite outline; Dynalist, Logseq and OneNote use outlines inside notes (Episode 22).

03Try it: one tree, two views

An outline and a mind map are the same data structure — a tree. Type or edit the indented outline (two spaces per level), then switch views. You can copy the Markdown or OPML into other tools.

05Infinite whiteboards

Online whiteboards turned the mind map into an unlimited canvas of sticky notes, shapes, arrows and images, used live in meetings. Miro (founded 2011 as RealtimeBoard, renamed 2019) and Mural grew rapidly during the 2020 shift to remote work; Microsoft Whiteboard, FigJam and Lucidspark followed. Open-source alternatives include Excalidraw (hand-drawn style, 2020) and draw.io (Episode 24).

Engineering uses of structured thinking diagrams are older than software: the fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram for root-cause analysis (1960s), fault trees (Bell Labs, 1961) and FMEA worksheets. Many mind-map tools now offer fishbone layouts for this reason.

09Lessons for engineers

  1. Structure first, prose later. An outline of a test report finds the gaps before you write a paragraph.
  2. The same data, many views. Outline, map, slide list and task list can all be one tree.
  3. Use the right diagram for the question. Mind maps for brainstorming, fishbones for causes, fault trees for safety.
  4. Keep thinking portable. A plain indented text file is the most durable mind map of all.
