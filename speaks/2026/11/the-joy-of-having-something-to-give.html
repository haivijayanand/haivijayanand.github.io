The Joy of Having Something to Give

5 Nov 20264 min readSelf-GrowthFamily

Joy is in caring and sharing.

We spend a considerable part of our lives trying to become capable.

We study to earn a living. We work to support a family. We save to feel secure. We gather knowledge, develop skills, and slowly find our place in the world.

Along this journey, we often ask: What more do I need to be happy?

Then, sometimes through a very ordinary experience, another question appears:

What do I already have that could make someone else’s life a little better?

That question can change the way we experience our own lives.

Perhaps we help a student understand something that has troubled them for weeks. Perhaps we accompany an elderly person to an appointment. Perhaps we share a meal, finish a difficult task for someone who is exhausted, or simply sit beside a person who needs to speak.

Afterwards, we feel a quiet satisfaction.

Someone needed something. We were able to respond. Our presence made a difference.

There is immense joy in that realisation.

We already have something to give

Many of us postpone giving because we imagine that generosity requires abundance.

“When I earn more, I will help.”

“When my responsibilities reduce, I will make time.”

“When I become more knowledgeable, I will guide others.”

But care can begin within our present circumstances.

A person with limited money may have the patience to listen. Someone with a busy schedule may still find fifteen minutes to explain a lesson. A retired person may have experience that helps a younger person avoid a costly mistake.

We have different resources at different stages of life: money, knowledge, strength, contacts, patience, attention, or time.

The first step is recognising their value.

Something that feels ordinary to us may be exactly what another person needs. A task we complete easily may feel overwhelming to someone attempting it for the first time.

Care gives sharing its meaning

Sharing begins to feel personal when we pay attention to the person receiving it.

What would actually help them? What are they comfortable accepting? Do they need advice, practical assistance, or simply someone willing to listen?

Sometimes we are eager to offer what we have, but care asks us to understand what is needed.

A child struggling with studies may need encouragement before another explanation. A colleague facing difficulty may need help completing the work before hearing suggestions for improvement. An elderly parent may value an unhurried conversation more than another item purchased for the house.

Care makes our contribution thoughtful. It helps us give in a way that respects the other person’s dignity and choices.

The special value of our time

Money can support many useful things. Our time carries another kind of meaning.

When we give someone our attention, we give them a portion of our life.

We pause our own activities. We make room in our thoughts. We communicate, through our presence: You matter to me.

Consider the difference between saying, “Let me know if you need help,” and returning later to ask, “How did it go?”

That small act of remembering can make someone feel supported.

Dedication often lives in such follow-through. We explain again when understanding takes time. We keep a promise. We return when the first attempt has failed.

Through these ordinary acts, care becomes something another person can depend upon.

Giving changes how we see ourselves

When we spend all our attention on what is missing from our lives, we can overlook what is already useful and valuable.

Helping someone brings those resources into view.

We discover that our experience has meaning. Our effort can ease a burden. Our patience can create confidence. Our presence can bring comfort.

This does not remove our own difficulties. Yet it can widen our perspective. Alongside our needs and worries, we begin to see our capacity to contribute.

There is a particular happiness in thinking: I was able to help today.

We can welcome that happiness without expecting gratitude, obedience, or recognition in return. The person we help remains free to make their own decisions. Our care need not become a claim upon their life.

A little, given consistently

Caring also needs to be sustainable. We have responsibilities, limits, and a need for rest. We cannot answer every request or carry every burden.

But we can choose a contribution we can maintain.

A regular call. An hour of guidance. A shared meal. A small amount set aside. A promise kept.

Over time, these modest acts become part of the way we live.

Perhaps joy becomes more accessible when we stop waiting to have everything and notice that, even now, we have something to offer.

“Today, someone may need a little of what we can spare. And in caring for them, we may discover how much that simple act adds to our own lives.”
