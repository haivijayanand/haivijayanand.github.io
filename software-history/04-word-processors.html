WordStar, WordPerfect and Word: the word-processor wars

Word processing replaced the typewriter, the typing pool and the correction fluid. Three programs led in turn — WordStar on CP/M, WordPerfect on DOS and Word on Windows — and each one stored "bold" in a different way.

01The requirement: stop retyping the whole page

Every typed business letter, report and legal contract had a hidden cost: a single mistake or late change meant retyping the page. Offices kept typing pools; lawyers' contracts went through many retyped drafts. IBM's MT/ST (1964) stored keystrokes on magnetic tape so a corrected document could be replayed; Wang Laboratories' screen-based word processors (1976 onwards) became expensive dedicated office machines.

Microcomputers offered the same thing for a fraction of the price. Michael Shrayer's Electric Pencil (1976) was the first for personal computers; WordStar (1978) made it professional.

04Version explorer

Blue chips: WordStar and WordPerfect. Amber chips: Microsoft Word and the cloud.

Two own goals: MicroPro released WordStar 2000 (1984), which was incompatible with WordStar's keys and files, and lost its users. WordPerfect Corporation bet on OS/2 and arrived late and buggy on Windows (1991–92). Word was waiting in both cases.

06Decline and retirement

  • WordStar: lost the market after WordStar 2000; the last DOS version was 7.0 (1992). MicroPro became WordStar International and was absorbed by SoftKey in 1994; the product was discontinued in the late 1990s. A few writers still use it — novelist George R. R. Martin famously writes on WordStar 4.0 under DOS.
  • WordPerfect: sold to Novell in 1994 and to Corel in 1996. It was never retired: WordPerfect Office is still sold by Alludo (formerly Corel), mainly to law firms and government offices that value Reveal Codes.
  • Word: part of Microsoft Office/365 since 1990; still dominant in business, alongside Google Docs in education and start-ups.

08Legacy

  • Ctrl key cursor movement from WordStar survives in many programmers' editors.
  • Reveal Codes lives on as "Show formatting marks" (¶) in Word and in HTML/Markdown source views.
  • Mail merge, styles, track changes and spell check were all invented in this era.
  • Unicode (from 1991) finally let one document mix English, Tamil, Hindi and other scripts.

09Lessons for engineers

  1. Never break your users' muscle memory. WordStar 2000 discarded the keys people knew and lost them.
  2. Drivers decide adoption. WordPerfect won offices by supporting almost every printer.
  3. Bet on the platform users actually adopt. WordPerfect's OS/2 bet cost it Windows.
  4. Show the structure when it matters. Reveal Codes is still loved because it hides nothing.
