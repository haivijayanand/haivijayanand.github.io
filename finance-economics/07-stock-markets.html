Level 1 · For school students

A share is a tiny piece of a company. The stock market is where those pieces are bought and sold.

1What is a share?

A share (or stock) is a small piece of ownership in a company. If you own a share, you own a tiny part of that company, including a tiny part of its profits.

Imagine your friend Meena runs a lemonade stand and needs ₹1,000 to buy a bigger cooler. She splits the business into 10 equal pieces and sells each for ₹100. If you buy one piece, you own 1/10th of the stand. When the stand earns profit, you get your share. If the stand becomes famous, others may want to buy your piece for more than ₹100!

Company gets moneyTo grow: new factories, shops, research
You get ownershipA share of profits (called a dividend) and a share of the company's growth
But no promiseIf the company does badly, your piece may be worth less. A share is not a fixed deposit.

3Where shares are traded

  • A stock exchange is a marketplace for shares. Today it runs entirely on computers: buyers and sellers place orders, and the exchange matches them in fractions of a second.
  • India's two big exchanges are the BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange, started in 1875, one of the oldest in Asia) and the NSE (National Stock Exchange, 1992).
  • When a company sells shares to the public for the first time, it is called an IPO (Initial Public Offering). After that, people trade those shares among themselves on the exchange.
  • SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) is the referee. It makes the rules and punishes cheating.

4Sensex and Nifty: the market's thermometer

Thousands of companies are listed, so how do we know if "the market" went up today? We use an index: an average of the prices of big, important companies.

SensexTracks 30 large companies on the BSE
Nifty 50Tracks 50 large companies on the NSE
Reading the news"Sensex up 773 points" means these big companies' prices rose on average that day. On 11 November 2026, the Sensex closed at 81,879.

5Risk: why prices can fall

  • Prices go down as well as up, sometimes a lot. In 2026, Indian markets fell sharply when a war pushed oil prices up, then recovered later in the year.
  • Don't put all your eggs in one basket: owning many companies (diversification) means one company's bad news hurts less.
  • Time helps: over many years, good companies tend to grow, but over a few months anything can happen. Money you need soon should not be in shares.
  • Beware "get rich quick": anyone promising sure profits is either mistaken or cheating.

7Check yourself

Level 2 · For working people

How the market is organised, the ways to invest, what drives prices, the 2026 story, the real risks, and how markets connect to the country and the world. This is educational only, not investment advice.

1How the market is organised

PieceRole
Primary marketCompanies raise new money through IPOs, follow-on offers and rights issues
Secondary marketInvestors trade existing shares on the BSE and NSE. The company gets no new money, but a liquid market makes its shares valuable.
SEBIThe regulator: listing rules, disclosure, insider-trading and manipulation cases, investor protection
Depositories (NSDL, CDSL)Hold your shares electronically in a demat account
BrokersRoute your orders to the exchange. Discount brokers charge low fees; full-service brokers add research.
Clearing corporationsGuarantee settlement. India moved to T+1 settlement in 2023: shares and money change hands the next working day.

2Ways to invest

Direct sharesYou pick companies yourself. This needs time, knowledge and discipline, and concentration risk is high.
Mutual fundsA professional manager pools money from many investors. Diversified, regulated, with costs shown as the expense ratio.
Index funds and ETFsSimply copy an index such as the Nifty 50. Very low cost; you get the market's return, not more and not less.
SIPA Systematic Investment Plan invests a fixed amount every month. It builds discipline and averages your buying price. Indians put about ₹32,000 crore a month into SIPs in 2026.

Calculator: SIP growth under different return assumptions

Returns are assumptions, not forecasts. Equity returns arrive unevenly, with years of losses. Past index returns do not guarantee future ones. Remember inflation (Topic 01) and taxes (Topic 06).

3What moves share prices

  • Earnings: over the long run, prices follow company profits. A share's P/E ratio (price ÷ earnings per share) shows how much investors pay for each rupee of profit.
  • Interest rates: higher rates make bonds and FDs more attractive and future profits less valuable today, so rising rates usually weigh on shares (Topic 02).
  • Money flows: foreign investors (FPIs) versus domestic institutions (mutual funds, insurers, the EPFO). In 2026, steady domestic SIP money absorbed much of the record foreign selling.
  • The rupee and oil: a weak rupee and costly oil hurt importers and foreign investors' dollar returns (Topic 05).
  • Sentiment: fear and greed can push prices far from fair value in the short run.

4The 2026 story

  1. Early 2026: the Nifty traded near 25,000–26,000, but foreign investors were already selling after heavy outflows in 2025.
  2. The West Asia shock: oil prices spiked, the rupee slid towards ₹97 per dollar, and FPIs sold a record amount in March (about ₹1.18 lakh crore). The Sensex fell to the 70,000s.
  3. The domestic cushion: SIP inflows kept rising to records above ₹32,000 crore a month. Domestic institutions bought what foreigners sold, and FPI ownership fell to about 15%, a multi-year low.
  4. Autumn recovery: easing crude prices, strong GDP growth (Topic 03) and some foreign buying lifted the Sensex to about 81,900 by mid-November, its highest since February.

5The real risks, and the traps

F&O tradingFutures and options are leveraged bets. SEBI found that 91% of individual traders lost money in FY25, about ₹1.06 lakh crore in total. Its 2026 study found that experience did not help: only about 1 in 200 stayed profitable in every one of five years.
Tips and "finfluencers"Telegram and WhatsApp stock tips, pump-and-dump schemes and fake trading apps cost investors heavily. Check that any adviser is SEBI-registered.
IPO hypeOversubscription does not mean value. Many popular IPOs trade below their offer price a year later.
BehaviourSelling in panic during falls, chasing last year's best fund, and checking prices daily all hurt returns more than market crashes do.

6Stock markets and the country

  • Funding growth: markets channel savings into companies building factories, telecom networks and new technology, alongside bank credit (Topic 04).
  • Household savings shift: Indian households are moving from gold, property and FDs towards mutual funds and shares. This deepens the market but raises the stakes of investor education.
  • Wealth effect: rising markets make shareholders feel richer and spend more; crashes do the reverse.
  • Market infrastructure: SEBI cleared the long-awaited NSE IPO in 2026, which will let investors own a stake in the exchange itself.

7The global picture and history

  • Famous crashes: 1929 (the Great Depression), 1987 (Black Monday, −22% in a day in the US), 2000 (the dot-com bust), 2008 (the global financial crisis), March 2020 (COVID). Markets recovered from each, sometimes only after many years.
  • India's own episodes: the 1992 Harshad Mehta scam (which led to SEBI getting statutory powers and the NSE's electronic trading), the 2008 fall of about 50% or more, and the 2020 crash and recovery.
  • Global links: US markets, driven in 2025–26 by the AI boom, set the global mood. When US yields rise or tech shares fall, emerging markets such as India often see foreign selling.

8Sensible habits

  • Invest in equities only for goals five or more years away. Keep an emergency fund and near-term money in safer options.
  • Prefer diversified, low-cost funds; check the expense ratio and pick direct plans where suitable.
  • Continue SIPs through downturns; that is when they buy more units.
  • Rebalance once a year to your chosen mix of equity and debt.
  • Avoid leverage, F&O speculation and unregistered advice.

9Check yourself

Level 3 · Review for experts

Valuation, the efficient-market debate, risk and diversification, factor models, microstructure, India's market structure and the macro-finance links.

1Valuation

Gordon growth: P₀ = D₁ / (r − g) → P/E = payout × (1 + g) / (r − g) CAPM cost of equity: r = r_f + β · ERP Earnings yield ≈ r − g (when growth earns its cost of capital)

Valuation is very sensitive to (r − g): a 1-point change can move the fair P/E by 20–40% when the spread is small. Use the calculator to see why the "right" multiple for India is so contested.

2Market efficiency and behavioural finance

  • EMH (Fama, 1970): weak form (past prices are useless for prediction), semi-strong (public information is priced in), strong (even private information is). Grossman–Stiglitz (1980): perfectly efficient markets are impossible, because someone must be paid to gather information.
  • Evidence: most active funds underperform their benchmark after costs over long periods (SPIVA India shows the same pattern for large caps). Yet anomalies persist: momentum, value, low volatility and post-earnings drift.
  • Behavioural finance: prospect theory (Kahneman–Tversky), overconfidence, the disposition effect, herding, limits to arbitrage (Shleifer–Vishny) and excess volatility (Shiller, 1981). The retail F&O losses documented by SEBI are a textbook case of overconfidence plus lottery preference.

3Risk, diversification and factors

Equal-weight portfolio of n stocks: σ_p² = σ²/n + (1 − 1/n)·ρ·σ² → as n→∞, σ_p → σ·√ρ (systematic risk)
  • CAPM (Sharpe, Lintner, 1964–65): only systematic (β) risk is priced. Empirically the security market line is too flat, the "low-beta anomaly".
  • Factor models: Fama–French three and five factors (market, size, value, profitability, investment), plus momentum (Carhart). Factor premia exist in India too but are time-varying and crowded.
  • Tail risk: returns are fat-tailed and correlations rise in crises, just when diversification is needed most.

4Microstructure and regulation

  • Continuous double auction with a limit-order book; price-time priority; pre-open call auction; index-based market-wide circuit breakers (10/15/20%) and stock-level price bands.
  • Settlement: India's T+1 (fully adopted in 2023) and optional T+0 make it one of the fastest-settling major markets. Margin rules and peak-margin reporting curbed intraday leverage.
  • Derivatives: Indian index options became the world's largest by contract volume. SEBI's late-2024 measures (bigger contract sizes, one weekly expiry per exchange, upfront premium collection, intraday position monitoring) and studies in 2025–26 show fewer traders but persistent losses.
  • Algorithmic and high-frequency trading: co-location, order-to-trade ratios and the 2025 Jane Street order (alleged index manipulation around expiry) highlight surveillance challenges.

5India's market structure in 2026

  • The domestic flow regime: SIP inflows of more than ₹32,000 crore a month plus EPFO and insurance money create a steady domestic bid. Domestic institutions now rival or exceed foreign investors in ownership; FPI ownership is near 15%, a multi-year low after record selling in 2026.
  • Valuation premium: India trades at a premium P/E to emerging-market peers, justified by its higher expected earnings growth and return on equity. Its persistence depends on the cost of capital (rates, the rupee) and earnings delivery.
  • Primary-market depth: a strong IPO pipeline including large new-economy listings and the NSE's own IPO, with SME-IPO excesses prompting tighter SEBI rules.
  • Household balance sheets: the shift of financial savings towards equity improves capital allocation but concentrates market risk on households, many of them first-time investors.

6Macro-finance links

  • Discount-rate channel: long-duration equity is sensitive to the real rate and the term premium. A US 10-year yield near 5% (Topic 02) compresses global multiples, particularly for growth stocks.
  • Tobin's q and investment: high market valuations relative to replacement cost encourage capital spending and equity issuance.
  • Wealth effect and financial conditions: equity prices feed into financial-conditions indices that central banks monitor.
  • Bubbles and crashes: Minsky's financial-instability hypothesis, rational bubbles, and Shiller's CAPE as a long-horizon (not timing) signal.

7Review checklist: open questions

  1. Is India's structural domestic SIP bid making the market less sensitive to foreign flows, and does it compress the equity risk premium?
  2. What is a defensible long-run (r − g) for Indian equities, given the rupee's depreciation trend?
  3. Have SEBI's F&O measures reduced household losses enough, or are suitability tests (as in some markets) needed?
  4. How much active management can India's market support before it becomes efficient, as the US large-cap market has?
  5. Is the concentration of index weight in a few stocks, and the rise of passive funds, changing price discovery?
  6. How should household equity risk be weighed in financial-stability assessments?
