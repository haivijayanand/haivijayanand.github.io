The Collatz Conjecture (3n + 1)
Home · Interesting Math Problems · Problem 20 · Growth & numbers

1. The problem

Pick any whole number. If it is even, halve it. If it is odd, multiply by 3 and add 1. Repeat.

6 → 3 → 10 → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1

Does every starting number eventually reach 1?

2. The answer

Nobody knows
Every number checked so far reaches 1 — by computer, all numbers up to 271 ≈ 2.36 × 1021 (Barina, 2025). There is no proof.
27
is the famous small surprise: 111 steps, climbing as high as 9,232 before falling to 1.

Paul Erdős said: "Mathematics may not be ready for such problems." It is perhaps the simplest unsolved problem in mathematics to state.

3. Why it is believable — and why it is hard

A rough argument that it should fall: after an odd step (3n + 1) the result is always even, so at least one halving follows. On average an odd number is followed by two halvings, so a typical "round" multiplies by about 3/4. The numbers drift downwards, like a random walk with a downhill slope.

Why that is not a proof: "on average" says nothing about every single number. There could be one starting value that climbs forever, or a loop other than 4 → 2 → 1. Terence Tao proved in 2019 that almost all numbers reach values almost as small as you like — the strongest result so far, and still not the full conjecture.

5. Fine print

  • Big starting numbers are handled exactly here using JavaScript BigInt — try 9780657630 (1,132 steps) or 63728127 (949 steps).
  • Lothar Collatz posed it in 1937. It has many names: the 3n + 1 problem, the hailstone problem (numbers rise and fall like hailstones in a cloud), Ulam's problem.
  • Change the rule to 3n − 1 and other loops appear (5 → 14 → 7 → 20 → 10 → 5). The +1 is what makes the known behaviour so tidy.
