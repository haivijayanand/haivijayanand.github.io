Fourier Series and the Fourier Transform: Building Signals from Sine Waves

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
FourierSignal processingHarmonicsMaths

Joseph Fourier showed that any reasonable periodic signal can be written as a sum of sine and cosine waves at multiples of its fundamental frequency. That idea underlies every spectrum analyser: a vibration spectrum is the list of those sine-wave amplitudes. Seeing how square or triangle waves are built from harmonics explains why impacts and clipping produce long rows of harmonics.

Key takeaways

  • Periodic x(t) = a₀ + Σ [aₙcos(nω₀t) + bₙsin(nω₀t)], with coefficients from integrals over one period.
  • Square wave: only odd harmonics, amplitude 4/(πn); triangle wave: odd harmonics falling as 1/n²; sawtooth: all harmonics as 1/n.
  • Sharp edges need many harmonics; near a jump the partial sum overshoots by about 9 % (Gibbs phenomenon).
  • Parseval: mean square of the signal = sum of squared harmonic RMS values, the basis of overall RMS from a spectrum.
  • The Fourier transform extends this to non-periodic signals; the DFT/FFT computes it for sampled data.

1. Fourier series

x(t) = a0 + Σ [an·cos(nω0t) + bn·sin(nω0t)] an = (2/T)·∫ x(t)·cos(nω0t) dt bn = (2/T)·∫ x(t)·sin(nω0t) dt (over one period) Square (±1): x = (4/π)·Σ sin(nω0t)/n, n odd Triangle: x = (8/π²)·Σ (−1)^k·sin((2k+1)ω0t)/(2k+1)² Sawtooth: x = (2/π)·Σ (−1)^(n+1)·sin(nω0t)/n

2. Parseval and RMS

RMS² = a0² + ½·Σ (an² + bn²) = Σ (harmonic RMS)² Overall RMS from a spectrum = √(Σ line RMS²)

3. Transform and DFT

X(f) = ∫ x(t)·e^(−j2πft) dt DFT: X_k = Σ_{n=0}^{N−1} x_n·e^(−j2πkn/N), frequency of bin k = k·Fs/N

Engineering consequences: impacts and clipping (sharp edges) create many harmonics; a pure sine creates one line; amplitude modulation creates sidebands; a periodic train of impulses creates a comb of lines at the repetition rate.
