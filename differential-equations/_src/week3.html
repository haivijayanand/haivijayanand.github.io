    Determinism without predictability

    A system is chaotic when it is deterministic (the equation fixes the future exactly), bounded (it stays in a finite region) and yet sensitive to initial conditions: two starts as close as you like separate exponentially fast. Three ingredients are needed:

    • Nonlinearity — linear systems only add up exponentials and sines.
    • At least three dimensions for an autonomous system (or two plus periodic forcing). In the plane, trajectories cannot cross, so they can only settle to equilibria or cycles (Day 10).
    • Stretching and folding — nearby states are pulled apart in one direction, then the region is folded back so that it stays bounded, like kneading dough.

    The Lorenz system

    In 1963 Lorenz cut a model of a fluid layer heated from below down to three variables: x, the speed of the convection roll; y and z, horizontal and vertical temperature differences.

    x′ = σ(y − x) y′ = x(ρ − z) − y z′ = x·y − β·z
    Classic values: σ = 10, β = 8/3, ρ = 28 (ρ measures the heating)
    ρBehaviour
    < 1Origin stable: no convection, heat conducts.
    1 – 24.06Two steady convection rolls C± = (±√(β(ρ − 1)), ±√(β(ρ − 1)), ρ − 1), turning one way or the other.
    24.06 – 24.74Steady rolls still stable, but a chaotic attractor also exists; the start decides.
    > 24.74The rolls lose stability (subcritical Hopf). The flow wanders on the butterfly, flipping between left and right lobes at irregular times.

    The attractor has zero volume (the flow contracts volume at rate σ + 1 + β = 13.7 per unit time) yet never closes on itself: it is a strange attractor, a fractal of dimension about 2.06.

    Measuring sensitivity

    A tiny separation δ₀ grows on average like δ(t) ≈ δ₀·eλt. The largest Lyapunov exponent λ is about 0.906 for the classic Lorenz system. If you can tolerate an error Δ, your forecast is useful only up to the predictability horizon

    tpred ≈ (1/λ)·ln(Δ/δ₀)

    The logarithm is the cruel part: making the initial measurement 1000 times better only adds ln(1000)/λ ≈ 7.6 time units. This is why weather forecasts stop being useful after roughly two weeks however good the instruments become.

    What can still be predicted

    Individual trajectories are unpredictable, but statistics are not: the fraction of time spent in each lobe, the average of z, the spectrum of x. Climate is the statistics of weather — which is why a climate projection can be meaningful when a 30-day weather forecast is not. A numerical solution of a chaotic system is also "wrong" after a while, yet the shadowing lemma says that (for many systems) it stays close to some true trajectory from a slightly different start — so its statistics are trustworthy.

    Poincaré sections and period doubling

    A trajectory in three dimensions is hard to read. Henri Poincaré's trick: look only at where it pierces a surface — or, for a periodically forced system, take a snapshot once per forcing period (a stroboscope).

    • Period-1 motion → one dot. Period-2 → two dots. Quasi-periodic → a closed curve.
    • Chaos → a cloud with fine, layered, fractal structure.

    The forced Duffing oscillator x″ + δx′ − x + x³ = γ·cos ωt (a beam buckled between two magnets, or a mount with a snap-through) shows a period-doubling cascade: as γ rises the motion repeats every 1, 2, 4, 8 … periods, the doublings come faster and faster (by Feigenbaum's universal ratio 4.669), and then chaos.

    Worked example

    How long until two Lorenz runs started 10⁻⁸ apart disagree completely (Δ ≈ 1)?t ≈ (1/0.906)·ln(1/10⁻⁸) = 18.42/0.906 = 20.3 time units Measure 1000× better (δ₀ = 10⁻¹¹): t ≈ 25.4/0.906 = 28.0 — only 7.6 units more. Lorenz's own experience: restarting a run from a printout rounded to 3 decimals (δ₀ ≈ 10⁻⁴) gives t ≈ 9.2/0.906 ≈ 10 units of agreement — which is what he saw.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • "Random-looking" data is not proof of chaos — noise looks random too. Chaos has structure: a low-dimensional attractor and a positive Lyapunov exponent.
    • Do not trust a single long simulation of a chaotic system point by point; trust its statistics, and check them against a different step size.
    • Many machines are chaotic only in a narrow parameter band. Changing clearance, preload or damping a little can restore periodic motion.

    From energy balance to a PDE

    Take a thin slice of a rod between x and x + dx. Heat flux q (W/m²) flows in at x and out at x + dx. Fourier's law: heat flows down the temperature gradient, q = −k·∂u/∂x. Energy balance on the slice:

    ρ·c·∂u/∂t = −∂q/∂x = k·∂²u/∂x² → ut = α·uxx, α = k/(ρ·c) (thermal diffusivity, m²/s)

    Read it: the temperature at a point rises if it is below the average of its neighbours (uxx > 0, a valley) and falls if it is above (a peak). Diffusion fills valleys and flattens peaks. The same equation governs ink spreading in water, a pollutant in groundwater, and — after a change of variables — the Black–Scholes model of option prices.

    A PDE needs boundary conditions as well as an initial shape: fixed temperature (Dirichlet), insulated ends (Neumann, ux = 0), or convection to the air (Robin).

    Separation of variables

    Rod of length L, both ends held at 0, initial shape f(x). Guess u = X(x)·T(t):

    X·T′ = α·X″·T → T′/(αT) = X″/X = −λ (a constant, because one side depends only on t, the other only on x)
    X″ + λX = 0 with X(0) = X(L) = 0 → Xn = sin(nπx/L), λn = (nπ/L)²
    T′ = −αλnT → Tn = e−α(nπ/L)²t

    u(x, t) = Σ bn·sin(nπx/L)·e−α(nπ/L)²t

    Each sine shape (a mode, just like Day 8) keeps its shape and simply decays. Mode n decays n² times faster than mode 1, so fine detail disappears first and every profile ends up looking like the single hump sin(πx/L).

    Fourier series

    To fit the initial shape, choose the bn so that Σ bn·sin(nπx/L) = f(x). Because the sines are orthogonal (∫ sin(mπx/L)·sin(nπx/L) dx = 0 for m ≠ n), each coefficient can be picked out alone:

    bn = (2/L)·∫₀ᴸ f(x)·sin(nπx/L) dx
    Full series on (−π, π): f(x) = a₀/2 + Σ (an·cos nx + bn·sin nx), an = (1/π)∫ f·cos nx dx, bn = (1/π)∫ f·sin nx dx
    Wave (amplitude 1, period 2π)CoefficientsDecay of coefficients
    Squarebn = 4/(nπ), odd n only1/n — it has jumps
    Sawtoothbn = 2(−1)n+1/(nπ)1/n — one jump per period
    Trianglebn = ±8/(n²π²), odd n1/n² — continuous, kinked

    The smoother the function, the faster its coefficients fall. Near a jump, any partial sum overshoots by about 9 % of the jump — the Gibbs phenomenon — and adding terms only squeezes the overshoot closer to the jump.

    Fourier, eigenfunctions and the FFT you already use

    The sines are the eigenfunctions of d²/dx² with the boundary conditions — exactly as eigenvectors were for a matrix on Day 8. Expanding in eigenfunctions decouples the PDE into independent ODEs, one per mode. The same idea with complex exponentials over a finite record is the discrete Fourier transform; the FFT computes it fast. When your vibration analyser shows a spectrum, it is printing the an, bn of a measured signal.

    Worked example

    A 0.5 m steel bar (k = 50 W/m·K, ρ = 7850 kg/m³, c = 490 J/kg·K), ends held at room temperature. How fast do disturbances decay?α = 50 / (7850 × 490) = 1.30 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s Mode 1 time constant τ₁ = L²/(α·π²) = 0.25 / (1.30e-5 × 9.87) = 1950 s ≈ 32 min Mode 3 time constant τ₃ = τ₁/9 ≈ 3.6 min So a hot spot spreads into a smooth hump within minutes, then the hump fades over half an hour.     The wave equation

    A string under tension T with mass μ per metre. A small element bent into a curve feels a net vertical force T·uxx·dx (the tension pulls it towards the straight line). Newton:

    μ·utt = T·uxx → utt = c²·uxx, c = √(T/μ)
    SystemWave speed cSteel, typical
    String√(T/μ)100 – 400 m/s for instrument strings
    Axial waves in a bar√(E/ρ)≈ 5100 m/s
    Torsional waves in a shaft√(G/ρ)≈ 3200 m/s
    Sound in a pipe√(γp/ρ)≈ 343 m/s in air at 20 °C

    d'Alembert: two travelling copies

    Any function of x − ct is a shape moving right at speed c, and any function of x + ct moves left. Both satisfy the wave equation, and so does their sum. For a string released from rest in shape f(x):

    u(x, t) = ½·[ f(x − ct) + f(x + ct) ]

    The initial bump splits into two half-height copies that run apart. At a fixed end the copy reflects upside down (mathematically: extend f as an odd function beyond the end). After a time 2L/c everything returns to the start — the string is periodic.

    Standing waves and harmonics

    Separation of variables again (as on Day 12), but now T″ = −c²λT gives oscillation instead of decay:

    u = Σ sin(nπx/L)·[ An·cos(nπct/L) + Bn·sin(nπct/L) ] fₙ = n·c/(2L)

    The frequencies are whole-number multiples of the fundamental — the harmonic series that makes a string sound musical. Pluck at a point and every mode with a node there is not excited: pluck at L/2 and all even harmonics vanish (a hollow sound); pluck near the end and the high harmonics are strong (a bright sound).

    Fixed–free shafts, and why drums sound "out of tune"

    A shaft fixed at one end and free at the other has u = 0 at x = 0 and ux = 0 at x = L. The modes become sin((2n − 1)πx/2L) with frequencies (2n − 1)·c/(4L): only odd multiples, like a clarinet. A drum membrane obeys the 2-D wave equation; on a circle its modes are Bessel functions whose frequencies are not whole-number multiples, which is why a drum has a clear pitch only with careful tuning.

    Laplace's equation: when time stops

    Let a heat problem run until nothing changes. Then ut = 0 and in two dimensions

    ∂²u/∂x² + ∂²u/∂y² = 0

    It describes steady temperature in a plate, electrostatic potential, groundwater pressure, potential flow round a wing, and the stress function in torsion of a non-circular shaft. Its solutions (harmonic functions) have two remarkable properties:

    • Mean value: the value at any point equals the average around any circle centred there. On a grid: each point equals the average of its four neighbours.
    • Maximum principle: the hottest and coldest points are on the boundary — never inside, unless there is a source.

    The mean-value property gives a solution method a child could follow: guess any values, then repeatedly replace each interior point by the average of its neighbours. This relaxation (Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel, and the faster over-relaxed SOR) converges to the answer.

    Parabolicut = αuxxDiffusion, smoothingInstantly, but weakly
    Ellipticuxx + uyy = 0Equilibrium, smooth everywhereEvery point depends on the whole boundary

    The modelling cycle

    1. Ask a precise question. "When will the oil temperature be stable enough to take readings?" is a modelling question; "model the gearbox" is not.
    2. List assumptions. Well-mixed oil, constant load, constant room temperature, heat loss proportional to temperature rise.
    3. Write the balance. m·c·T′ = Ploss − h·A·(T − Troom) — a first-order lag.
    4. Non-dimensionalise. Find the natural scales (here τ = m·c/(h·A) and ΔT = P/(h·A)); they show which parameters matter and which terms are negligible.
    5. Fit the unknown parameters to data.
    6. Validate on data you did not fit — a different run, a different load.
    7. Use the model, and say how far it can be trusted.

    Least squares for ODE models

    Given measurements (ti, yi) and a model y(t; p) obtained by solving the ODE with parameters p, choose p to minimise

    S(p) = Σ ( yi − y(ti; p) )²

    For an ODE there is usually no formula for the best p, so a search algorithm adjusts p, re-solves the ODE, and repeats: Nelder–Mead (used in the lab), Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt. Report the fit with its RMS error and R², and always look at the residuals yi − y(ti; p): they should look like noise. A smile, a wave or a trend means the model is wrong, however high R² is.

    Identifiability and over-fitting

    Some parameter combinations cannot be told apart from the data. In SIR fitted only to the early, exponential phase, the data fixes β − γ (the growth rate) but not β and γ separately — many pairs fit equally well and predict very different peaks. That is practical non-identifiability; the cure is better data (the turning point) or prior knowledge (the known infectious period). Adding parameters always reduces S on the fitted data; judge models with fresh data, or penalise complexity (AIC, BIC).

    The rate plot: choose the model before you fit

    For an autonomous first-order model y′ = f(y), estimate the slope of the data by finite differences, (yi+1 − yi−1)/(ti+1 − ti−1), and plot it against y. The points trace out f itself:

    Shape of y′ against yModel
    Straight line falling through zero at y∞First-order lag: y′ = (y∞ − y)/τ, slope −1/τ
    Straight line through the originExponential growth or decay
    Upside-down parabola through 0 and KLogistic
    A curve bending down at large yQuadratic loss, e.g. windage: ω′ = −a − bω²

    It turns a curve-fitting guess into a picture of the differential equation itself — the theme of this course.

    Worked example

    Gearbox oil on an endurance rig: fitted T∞ = 78 °C, T(0) = 32 °C, τ = 22 min. The procedure says "record when T changes by less than 1 °C in 30 min".     Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Finite-difference slopes amplify noise. Sample often enough, average, or smooth before the rate plot — never before the fit itself.
    • Fit in the units the data was measured in; check that parameters come out physically sensible (positive time constants, capacities above the data).
    • A model that fits one run is a hypothesis. A model that predicts the next run is knowledge.
    • Keep the model as simple as the question allows. A first-order lag that is good to ±1 °C is worth more than a twelve-parameter model nobody can check.

    Derivatives on a grid

    Put the rod on a grid xi = i·Δx and step in time tn = n·Δt. Taylor series give

    Forward difference: u′ ≈ (ui+1 − ui)/Δx error O(Δx)
    Central difference: u′ ≈ (ui+1 − ui−1)/(2Δx) error O(Δx²)
    Second derivative: u″ ≈ (ui+1 − 2ui + ui−1)/Δx² error O(Δx²)

    Replacing the space derivatives in a PDE but keeping time continuous turns it into a large system of ODEs — the method of lines. Everything from Day 4 then applies: you can hand the system to an RK or a stiff solver.

    Three schemes for the heat equation

    r = α·Δt / Δx²
    Explicit (FTCS): uin+1 = uin + r·(ui+1n − 2uin + ui−1n) stable only if r ≤ ½
    Implicit (backward Euler): un+1 − r·δ²un+1 = un stable for all r, first order in time
    Crank–Nicolson: average of the two stable for all r, second order in time

    The implicit schemes need a tridiagonal solve each step (cheap: the Thomas algorithm is O(N)). The explicit scheme is simplest but halving Δx forces Δt down four times — sixteen times more work in one dimension.

    Von Neumann stability analysis

    Put a single Fourier mode uin = Gn·ejkiΔx into the scheme. For FTCS: G = 1 + r·(ejkΔx − 2 + e−jkΔx) = 1 − 4r·sin²(kΔx/2). The scheme is stable if |G| ≤ 1 for every k. The worst mode is the grid's zig-zag, kΔx = π, where G = 1 − 4r; |G| ≤ 1 needs r ≤ ½. For the wave equation the same analysis of the leapfrog scheme gives the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition c·Δt/Δx ≤ 1: in one time step, a wave must not travel further than one grid cell — the numerical domain of dependence must contain the physical one. Every explicit CFD and crash code respects a condition of this kind.

    Capstone: a gearbox on a test rig

    Bring the course together on one system. A gearbox runs on an endurance rig: motor → coupling → gearbox → coupling → absorbing dynamometer. Work through the questions on paper and check each in the labs.

    1. Thermal (Days 1–2, 14). The oil gains Ploss = 1.2 kW and loses h·A·(T − 30) with h·A = 26 W/K; the oil-and-casing heat capacity is m·c = 34 kJ/K. Write the ODE, find τ and the final temperature, and the time to "stabilised" (rate < 1 °C per 30 min). Answer: τ = 34000/26 = 1308 s ≈ 21.8 min; T∞ = 30 + 1200/26 = 76.2 °C.
    2. Runaway check (Day 3). If the loss rises with temperature, P = 1.2·(1 + 0.01·(T − 30)) kW, is there still a stable equilibrium? Sketch generation and dissipation against T. Generation slope 12 W/K < dissipation slope 26 W/K, so yes: T∞ = 30 + 1200/(26 − 12) = 115.7 °C — much hotter. The margin is the gap between the two slopes.
    3. Torsional modes (Days 5, 8). Lump the drive as three inertias Jm = 0.25, Jg = 0.05, Jd = 0.4 kg·m² on two shafts k₁ = k₂ = 2×10⁴ N·m/rad. Write M·θ″ + K·θ = 0, note the rigid-body mode at 0, and find the two non-zero natural frequencies. (≈ 40 Hz and ≈ 148 Hz.) Compare with the motor speed and gear-mesh frequency.
    4. Forced response (Days 6–7). The gear mesh excites the drive at z·n/60 Hz. Sweep the motor speed: at what speeds does mesh frequency cross each mode? What damping would keep the dynamic magnification below 10?
    5. Data (Day 14). Paste a real warm-up log into the model fitter and compare its τ with item 1.
    6. Simulation (Days 4, 15). If you simulate the coupled thermal + torsional model with an explicit method, which time scale sets the step size, and why is the system stiff?

    Hint for 3: with M = diag(0.25, 0.05, 0.4) and K = 2×10⁴·[[1, −1, 0], [−1, 2, −1], [0, −1, 1]], det(K − ω²M) = 0 gives ω = 0 (the rigid-body mode) and two roots ω ≈ 254 and ≈ 930 rad/s. In mode 1 the motor and the dynamometer swing against each other across the gearbox; in mode 2 the light gearbox inertia vibrates between the two shafts while the heavy ends hardly move. For 6: the mode periods are milliseconds, the thermal time constant is twenty minutes — a ratio of about 10⁶.
