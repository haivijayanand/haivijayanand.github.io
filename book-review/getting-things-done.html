Getting Things Done

David Allen · first published 2001 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

A clear mind and steady productivity come from moving every commitment out of your head into a trusted system and deciding its next physical action.

Summary

David Allen starts from a simple observation: most stress at work comes not from having too much to do, but from keeping unfinished commitments loosely in your head. Each open loop quietly pulls at your attention, leaving you anxious without real progress. His remedy is a practical method for getting everything out of memory and into a system you can trust, leaving the mind free to focus calmly on the task in front of it.

The method has five stages. First, capture: collect every task, idea and obligation, large or small, into a few inboxes rather than leaving any of it in your head. Second, clarify: take each item and ask whether it requires action. If not, throw it away, file it as reference, or park it on a someday list. If it does, decide the very next visible step.

Allen insists on defining that next action concretely, such as phoning a supplier rather than vaguely sorting out the contract. If the step takes less than two minutes, do it immediately. Otherwise delegate it and track it on a waiting-for list, or defer it to your calendar or a list of next actions.

Third, organise the results into a small set of lists. A project is any outcome that needs more than one step, and every project should always have at least one next action attached. Actions are grouped by context, such as calls, errands or computer work, so you can see what is possible wherever you are.

Fourth, reflect. A weekly review, in which you empty inboxes, update every list and look ahead, keeps the system current and therefore trustworthy. Fifth, engage: choose what to do based on context, time available, energy and priority.

Allen also offers a natural planning approach for projects, moving from purpose and vision through brainstorming to concrete next steps.

305 words

One-line takeaway: Capture everything, decide each item's next concrete action, and review your lists weekly so your mind can rest on the work at hand.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. According to Allen, what is a main source of stress for busy people?

    Answer C. Open loops kept in memory keep demanding attention, creating anxiety without progress.

  2. What is the first stage of the GTD workflow?

    Answer A. The process begins by collecting every task, idea and obligation out of your head into trusted collection points.

  3. When clarifying an item that needs no action, which is NOT one of Allen's options?

    Answer D. Non-actionable items are trashed, filed as reference or incubated; the calendar is for actions tied to a specific day or time.

  4. What does the two-minute rule say?

    Answer B. Tracking a very quick task would take longer than simply doing it, so it is done on the spot.

  5. Which is the best example of a well-defined next action?

    Answer C. A next action is a specific, visible physical step, not a vague area of concern.

  6. How does Allen define a project?

    Answer B. Allen uses a broad definition: anything requiring more than one step to complete counts as a project.

  7. Why are next actions grouped by context such as calls or errands?

    Answer D. Context lists match actions to the tools and places available at the moment.

  8. What is the purpose of the weekly review?

    Answer A. Regularly emptying inboxes and updating lists maintains trust in the system, which is what frees the mind.

  9. Where should a delegated task be tracked?

    Answer B. A waiting-for list lets you follow up on things others owe you without keeping them in your head.

  10. Besides priority, which factors does Allen suggest for choosing what to do in the moment?

    Answer C. He proposes choosing by context, time available, energy and then priority.
