@sub Seventeen companies, eleven industries, two markets: how giants rise, the warning signs, how fast they fall, and what Apple and IBM did differently
@outline Theories of corporate decline | Seventeen cases and the method | A taxonomy of ten decline factors | Global cases: Nokia to Sears | Indian cases: Satyam to Hindustan Motors | Companies that came back | Cross-case analysis | An engineering view: early-warning indicators | Conclusions
@tnotes No company compounds profit forever. This talk looks at fifteen companies that rose to dominance and then collapsed, from Nokia, Kodak and Enron to Satyam, Jet Airways and Yes Bank, and two that came back from near-failure, Apple and IBM. The aim is to find the recurring mechanisms and the measurable warning signs.

F: The corporate life cycle | fig1
- Collins: hubris → undisciplined growth → denial of risk → grasping for salvation → irrelevance
- The time axis is unlabelled: the same shape can play out over months or decades
N: Jim Collins described five stages of decline: hubris born of success, undisciplined pursuit of more, denial of risk and peril, grasping for salvation, and capitulation. The figure deliberately has no time scale, because the same shape can unfold over thirty years, as at Kodak, or eighteen months, as at Lehman.

B: Four theories of rise and decline
- **Life cycle** (Adizes, Collins): organisations age like organisms
- **Disruptive innovation** (Christensen): incumbents rationally ignore cheap new markets
- **Financial fragility** (Minsky): stability breeds leverage, leverage breeds collapse
- **Governance and agency:** managers' interests diverge from owners'
N: Four bodies of theory explain decline. Life-cycle theory says organisations age. Christensen's disruption theory explains why well-run incumbents lose to cheaper technologies. Minsky explains how good times encourage leverage that turns a bad year into a collapse. And agency theory explains fraud and short-termism when managers are not held to account.

N: And the Indian cases plus the two contrast cases. Satyam lost 97 per cent in days, Jet Airways 99, Reliance Communications 99.9, Kingfisher 98.5, DHFL 97 and Yes Bank 98.6. Hindustan Motors faded with the end of the protected market. Apple and IBM, by contrast, recovered from near-failure.

B: A taxonomy of ten decline factors
- **Strategic:** F1 technology disruption · F2 core-business lock-in · F6 over-expansion · F8 price war
- **Financial / governance:** F3 leverage and liquidity · F4 fraud · F5 financialisation of culture
- **External trigger:** F7 regulatory or legal shock · F9 macro shock
- **People:** F10 unchecked key person or promoter
- Typical failure = one strategic + one financial cause + an external trigger
N: Reading across all seventeen histories, ten recurring causes can be separated. They rarely act alone. The typical failure combines one strategic weakness with one financial or governance weakness, and is then triggered by an external shock such as a recession, a regulator's action or a price war.

F: Global cases: share price after the peak | fig2
- Nokia and Kodak: slow disruption over a decade
- Enron and Lehman: collapse within months
- GE, Boeing: financialisation eroded engineering and balance sheets
N: This chart shows each global company's share price as a percentage of its peak, on a log scale, against years after the peak. The difference in slope is striking. Disrupted companies such as Kodak and Nokia slide for a decade. Enron and Lehman fall off a cliff. GE and Boeing show the slow damage of putting financial targets ahead of engineering.

B: Disruption, fraud and leverage: four global stories
- **Nokia:** led phones, but Symbian lost to iPhone and Android; survived as a network vendor
- **Kodak:** invented the digital camera (1975), protected film margins; Chapter 11 in 2012
- **Enron:** mark-to-market accounting and off-balance-sheet entities; bankrupt in weeks
- **Lehman:** 30-to-1 leverage on mortgage assets; bankrupt September 2008
N: Kodak's story is the classic: it invented the digital camera and then protected its film business. Nokia saw the smartphone coming but bet on its own platform too long. Enron booked imagined future profits and hid debt off the balance sheet. Lehman ran thirty dollars of assets for every dollar of equity; a small fall in mortgage values wiped it out.

B: When finance outranks engineering: GE and Boeing
- **GE:** GE Capital grew to about half of profits; 2008 funding crisis; dividend cut; split into three companies
- **Boeing:** after the 1997 McDonnell Douglas merger, cost and buyback targets displaced engineering voice
- 737 MAX crashes (2018, 2019), 20-month grounding, later quality escapes
- Both: buybacks while core engineering investment lagged
N: GE and Boeing illustrate factor F5, the financialisation of culture. GE became in effect a bank with an industrial business attached, and the 2008 crisis exposed it. Boeing after the McDonnell Douglas merger put cost and share buybacks ahead of engineering judgement, and the 737 MAX crashes followed. GE Aerospace today, run as an engineering company again, trades at records.

F: Indian cases on the same basis | fig3
- Satyam: −95 % in three days after the chairman's confession (Jan 2009)
- Jet, Kingfisher: price war from low-cost carriers plus debt
- RCom: debt plus the Jio price war; DHFL and Yes Bank: funding and governance
- Hindustan Motors: comfort of a protected market
N: The Indian cases show the same mechanisms. Satyam, India's Enron, lost 95 per cent in three days after Ramalinga Raju admitted the accounts were falsified. Jet Airways and Kingfisher were full-service carriers loaded with debt in a low-cost market. Reliance Communications was crushed by debt and Jio's free data. DHFL borrowed short and lent long; Yes Bank grew at any cost. Hindustan Motors simply never adapted after protection ended.

F: Companies that came back: Apple and IBM | fig4
- Apple 1997: Jobs returns, product line cut to four, focus on design; ≈3 000× from trough
- IBM 1993: Gerstner keeps the company whole, shifts to services and integration
- Intel, Nokia, Yes Bank: partial recoveries
N: Apple and IBM show that even severe strategic mistakes are survivable. Apple in 1997 was weeks from insolvency; Steve Jobs cut the product line drastically and refocused on design. IBM under Lou Gerstner kept the company together and moved into services. What both had was honest accounts and enough balance sheet to buy time.

F: The decline-factor matrix | fig7
- Rows: seventeen cases; columns: factors F1–F10
- Fraud and leverage cluster in the fastest falls
- Disruption and lock-in cluster in the slow ones
- Recovered companies lacked F3 and F4
N: This matrix is the author's structured assessment of all seventeen cases against the ten factors. Darker cells are stronger factors. Two clusters are visible: fraud and leverage together in the fast collapses, disruption and core-business lock-in in the slow declines. The companies that recovered had strategic problems but not hidden leverage or fraud.

F: The failure mode sets the speed of the fall | fig5
- Fraud: 90 % lost in months or days
- Leverage: within a year or two
- Disruption and price war: a decade or more, like fatigue cracking
N: An engineer would recognise this pattern. Technology disruption and price wars cause slow, fatigue-like declines lasting a decade or more. Fraud and excessive leverage cause brittle fracture, a collapse within months. The years from peak to a 90 per cent fall group clearly by failure mode.

F: The falls were company-specific | fig6
- Benchmark indices usually rose while these companies lost almost everything
- Not market crashes: internal failure modes
- Relative under-performance of −30 to −40 % over 12 months is a strong warning
N: These were not market crashes. Over the same windows the benchmark index usually rose while the company lost almost everything. That makes relative share-price performance a useful early-warning signal: a year of under-performance of thirty to forty per cent against the index was present in almost every case.

T: Company versus index over the same window | tab2
N: The numbers behind the previous chart. In window after window the company lost 90 per cent or more while the benchmark index was flat or rose. The falls were driven by company-specific failure modes, not by the market.

T: An engineering view: twelve warning indicators | tab3 | rows=0-11 | cols=1,2,3
N: Treating a company like a machine under condition monitoring, the paper proposes twelve measurable symptoms. Profit not converting to cash, funding mismatch, leverage outrunning earnings, promoter pledging, related-party dealings, auditor divergence, losing the new segment, buybacks instead of investment, safety escapes, unexplainable complexity, an unchecked key person and persistent under-performance. Each was visible in several cases well before the fall.

C: Conclusions
- Decline follows recognisable mechanisms
- Disruption and price wars: slow, fatigue-like; fraud and leverage: brittle, within months
- Falls are company-specific; indices usually rose
- Strategic mistakes are survivable with a sound balance sheet and honest accounts
FW: Lessons
- Managers: fund the new segment even when it cannibalises the old
- Boards: challenge the dominant individual; watch cash, not profit
- Investors: track the twelve indicators, not only the price
N: Strategic mistakes, even severe ones, are survivable if the balance sheet is sound and the accounts are honest, as Apple, IBM and, so far, Intel and Nokia show. What kills companies quickly is the combination of hidden leverage or fraud with an external shock.
