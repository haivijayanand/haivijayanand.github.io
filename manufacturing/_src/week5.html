    Purchasing is a big lever

    Where bought-in material is 60–80 % of cost (as in converting, food, electronics assembly), a 2 % lower purchase cost can add as much profit as a 15–20 % rise in sales. But the cheapest price is not the lowest cost: a supplier whose film breaks on the press, whose foil arrives late or whose ink shade wanders costs far more in downtime and scrap.

    Total cost of ownership = price + freight and duties + inventory carrying + inspection + scrap and downtime caused + administration + risk

    Make or buy

    Make whenBuy when
    It is core to your competitive advantage or know-howSpecialists do it better or cheaper
    You have spare capacity on the right machinesVolumes are too small to justify the equipment
    Quality or confidentiality must be controlled in-houseTechnology changes fast
    Suppliers are unreliable or fewCapital is better used elsewhere

    Use relevant costs (Day 6): if the capacity would otherwise be idle, the in-house cost is only the variable cost; if it would displace other work, include the lost contribution.

    Choosing and rating suppliers

    1. Define requirements: specification, volumes, delivery, certifications (food-contact compliance, ISO 9001), payment terms.
    2. Shortlist from market search; send an RFQ with the same data to all.
    3. Score offers on weighted criteria: quality, price / TCO, delivery and lead time, technical support, financial health, location and risk.
    4. Audit the top one or two; run trials on your machines; approve with a first-article and samples.
    5. Contract: specification, quality agreement, price basis with raw-material index clause, delivery terms, penalties.
    6. Rate monthly: PPM rejects, OTIF, price, responsiveness; review with the supplier quarterly.

    Supply in flexible packaging

    Few big suppliers, volatile pricesBase films (BOPP, PET, CPP, BOPA), aluminium foil and polymer resins come from a few large producers, often with import content; prices follow crude oil, polymer and aluminium markets and move monthly. Inks, adhesives and solvents come from specialist suppliers who also give technical support — a good ink supplier's colour lab is part of your capability. Cylinders come from engravers; sleeves and plates from repro houses. Risks: single-source films (one supplier's specific grade qualified with a customer), long import lead times, port delays, force majeure at a resin plant. Controls: qualify two sources for every A-class film, keep a safety stock sized to the lead-time variation (Day 10), pass raw-material price changes through with indexed price clauses agreed with customers, and approve supplier changes with the customer when the structure is specified.

    Worked example

    Two BOPP film offers Supplier A Supplier B Price ₹168/kg ₹162/kg Lead time, reliability 2 wk, 96 % OTIF 6 wk (import), 85 % OTIF Extra safety stock — +25 t × ₹162 × 25 %/yr = ₹10.1 lakh/yr Web breaks / rejects 0.4 % 1.2 % → extra 0.8 % of 1 200 t × ₹162 = ₹15.6 lakh/yr Price saving at 1 200 t — ₹72 lakh/yr B is still cheaper by about ₹46 lakh/yr — but only if quality is held; dual-source with A for urgent jobs.

    • Treat key suppliers as partners. Share forecasts (Day 7) — you get better lead times and priority.

    Yield through the process

    Overall yield Y = y₁ × y₂ × … × yₙ Input needed = good output ÷ Y Scrap = input − good output Value of scrap loss = Σ scrap at each step × cost accumulated up to that step − resale value

    Scrap late in the process is the most expensive: a pouch rejected at the end has already been extruded, printed, laminated, cured and slit. A one-point gain in yield at the last step is worth more than at the first.

    Mass balance

    Input = output + scrap + losses (evaporation, trim, dust) ± stock change. Do it monthly for the whole plant and per department. An unexplained gap of a few percent usually means unrecorded scrap, wrong BOM allowances (Day 8), give-away (Day 15) or weighing errors.

    Sustainability

    Waste hierarchyExamples
    PreventShorter make-ready, better capability, right-first-time colour, smaller trims
    ReduceThinner films (down-gauging) with the same performance, fewer layers
    ReuseIn-house regrind of clean edge trim into extrusion; returned ink reused after correction; reclaimed solvent
    RecycleSell segregated scrap (PE, PP, PET, laminate) to recyclers at the best grade
    RecoverEnergy recovery (co-processing in cement kilns) for mixed laminates
    Carbon footprint. Scope 1 (fuel burned on site), scope 2 (purchased electricity), scope 3 (materials bought, transport, end of life). For converters, scope 3 — the polymers and foil — is by far the largest. Material yield and down-gauging cut carbon as well as cost.

    Packaging and the circular economy

    Yield, regrind, EPR and mono-materialsTypical step yields in a converter: extrusion 97–98 % (start-up and edge trim mostly reground), printing 93–97 % (make-ready), lamination 97–99 %, slitting 95–98 % (edge trim), pouch making 92–97 %. On small jobs the losses are much higher. Segregate scrap at source — clean single-polymer scrap is worth several times mixed laminate scrap. In India the Plastic Waste Management Rules place extended producer responsibility (EPR) on brand owners, importers and producers, with collection targets, recycled-content obligations for some packaging categories, and registration on the central portal; converters are "producers" for the packaging they supply and must support customers' EPR returns. Brand owners increasingly ask for recyclable mono-material structures (all-PE or all-PP laminates with barrier coatings instead of PET/Al/PE). Check the latest rules and amendments for your category.

    Worked example

    Input for a pouch orderGood output needed: 10 t of pouches Step yields: print 95 %, lamination 98 %, slitting 96 %, pouch making 94 % Y = 0.95 × 0.98 × 0.96 × 0.94 = 0.840 → input = 10 / 0.840 = 11.9 t of laminate-equivalent material Scrap 1.9 t; at ₹210/kg average accumulated value and ₹15/kg scrap resale → loss ≈ ₹3.7 lakh on one order Pouch yield 94 → 96 %: Y = 0.858, input 11.65 t → saves 0.25 t ≈ ₹50 000 per such order.

    A yield and waste programme

    1. Weigh every scrap bin by step and reason, every shift.
    2. Calculate yield by step and job size; do the monthly mass balance.
    3. Pareto the losses by value (Day 17); attack the top three.
    4. Segregate scrap by polymer and cleanliness; regrind what can be reused.
    5. Track EPR obligations and recycled-content requirements with customers.
    6. Work with customers on down-gauging and mono-material structures.

    Lab: yield cascade

    Set the yield of each step, the order size and the cost added at each step. The cascade shows input needed, scrap at each step and its value.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Weigh, don't estimate. Unweighed scrap is always under-reported.
    • Yield on small jobs is a pricing issue too — quote set-up waste explicitly (Day 28).
    • Regrind only clean, compatible material; contaminated regrind causes gels and film breaks.
    • Managers: report scrap in rupees next to sales every month.

    What to collect

    Machine stateRunning, stopped, changeover, idle (no order), fault — from PLC signals or a simple current sensor.
    Counts and speedsMetres, pieces, kg; actual vs rated speed.
    Stop reasonsOperator picks from a short list on a tablet when a stop exceeds 2 minutes.
    Process valuesTemperatures, tensions, viscosities, coat weights, register error — with timestamps and job number.

    Digital in a converting plant

    Roll IDs, recipes and live OEEEvery roll gets a barcode or QR label at birth (extruded, printed, laminated, slit), linking job, machine, operator, time, input rolls and test results — so a complaint traces back to the exact film lot and adhesive batch in minutes. Recipes for each job (speeds, temperatures, tensions, slitter knife positions, pouch dimensions) are stored and loaded at changeover (Day 11). Online thickness gauges, 100 % print inspection cameras and register controllers feed defect maps that travel with the roll so the slitter or pouch machine can cut defects out. A live OEE screen at each machine and a plant board in the office replace the morning argument about what happened last night. Many converters start with low-cost sensors and an open-source or SaaS dashboard before buying a full MES.

    Worked example

    Payback of automatic stop logging8 machines; manual logs captured only stops > 15 min. Sensors + tablets + dashboard ≈ ₹12 lakh. First month of real data: 2.1 h/day/machine of short stops (2–15 min) never recorded before. Top cause on presses: waiting for ink approval (28 %); on pouch machines: film splice (22 %). Fixing these two recovered 0.6 h/day/machine × 8 × 330 days × ₹4 000 contribution/h ≈ ₹63 lakh/yr.

    A practical digital roadmap

    1. Start with a business problem (OEE, traceability, energy) — not with a technology.
    2. Capture machine states and counts automatically on the bottleneck; add stop reasons.
    3. Show it live at the machine and review daily; act on the top losses.
    4. Add roll / batch IDs and genealogy; link lab results.
    5. Connect to ERP for orders and stock; add recipes and condition monitoring.
    6. Only then add analytics, prediction and AI — on data you trust. Secure every connection (cybersecurity) and back up.

    The cost build-up

    Price = (material + conversion + set-up share + freight and packing) × (1 + overhead %) × (1 + margin %) Material = Σ (quantity per unit × price) ÷ yield Conversion = Σ (run hours + set-up hours) × machine-hour rate ÷ quantity

    This works for any made-to-order product: a machined part (bar stock, machining hours, heat treatment), a fabricated frame (steel, welding hours, painting), a printed carton or a laminated pouch.

    Quoting in converting

    Per kg, per 1 000, and the small-order problemRolls are quoted per kg, pouches per 1 000. Material cost comes from the structure; conversion from the route (print colours, lamination passes, slitting, pouch type, zipper, laser scoring). The cost driver of small jobs is make-ready: a 9-colour gravure set-up takes about an hour and 800–1 500 m of film whatever the job size, and cylinders cost ₹1–3 lakh per set (charged separately or amortised). So quote a price ladder by quantity and a minimum order quantity. Add price-variation clauses linked to film, resin and foil prices (Day 25), a validity date, payment terms, tolerance on quantity (often ±10 %) and the specification reference.

    Worked example

    PET 12 / Al 9 / PE 80 stand-up pouchGSM: PET 16.8 + Al 24.4 + PE 73.6 + ink 3 + adhesive 2 × 2.5 = 122.8 g/m² ₹/m²: PET 16.8×₹150 + Al 24.4×₹520 + PE 73.6×₹125 + ink 3×₹600 + adh 5×₹420 (all ÷ 1000) = ₹28.3/m² Pouch area 0.0727 m² → material per 1 000 = ₹2 057; ÷ yield 0.84 → ₹2 449 Conversion (print + 2 lam + slit + pouch) ≈ ₹420 per 1 000 at 5 lakh pouches; zipper ₹180 Overhead 8 %, margin 12 % → price ≈ (2 449 + 420 + 180) × 1.08 × 1.12 ≈ ₹3 690 per 1 000 pouches.

    How to quote

    1. Confirm the specification: structure, dimensions, print, special features, quantity and delivery schedule.
    2. Compute material cost from the structure with current prices and the realistic yield for this job size.
    3. Route the job; estimate run and set-up hours per machine; apply MHRs.
    4. Add tooling (cylinders, plates, dies) separately or amortised; add packing and freight.
    5. Apply overhead and margin; check contribution per bottleneck hour against other work (Day 6).
    6. Issue the quote with a quantity ladder, MOQ, validity, price-variation clause and tolerances.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Use replacement cost, not book cost, for material in a quote when prices are rising.

    Daily management

    1. Tier 1 (start of shift, at the machine, 10 min): safety, yesterday's performance, today's plan, problems.

    Investment appraisal

    Simple payback = investment ÷ annual net saving NPV = −I + Σ (CFₜ ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ) accept if NPV > 0 IRR = the rate r at which NPV = 0 accept if IRR > cost of capital (hurdle rate)

    Payback is easy and favours quick wins; NPV accounts for the time value of money and the whole life; IRR is easy to compare with a hurdle rate. Use all three, and test the result with ±20 % on savings.

    Investments in a converting plant

    New press or better press?Typical proposals: a new 10-colour gravure press (₹12–20 crore), a solvent-less laminator to replace solvent-based (lower energy, no VOC, faster release), a solvent recovery plant or RTO (compliance plus reclaimed solvent), 100 % print inspection (fewer complaints), automatic gauge control on extrusion (less give-away, Day 15), a VSD compressor (Day 24). Before a new machine, ask what OEE improvement, SMED or a third shift on the existing constraint (Day 4) would give — it is often half the capacity for a tenth of the money.
