Only Mad People Make History

16 Jul 20265 min readSelf-GrowthManagement

"You have to be mad at something."

Not mad in the clinical sense. Mad in the sense of passion. An unreasonable, uncompromising, and relentless passion for something that refuses to leave your mind—even when the world tells you to slow down, take it easy, or find balance.

History is not made by people who merely like what they do. It is made by people who are obsessed with it.

The world often celebrates greatness without acknowledging the madness that produced it. We applaud the achievements but conveniently ignore the sacrifices hidden behind them.

Take S. Janaki, one of India's greatest playback singers. She has sung more than 48,000 songs across multiple languages over her illustrious career. Think about that number for a moment. Forty-eight thousand songs. That is not talent alone. That is madness. It is impossible to reach such a number if singing is treated as a profession that begins at 9 AM and ends at 5 PM.

Or consider Ilaiyaraaja. He has composed more than 8,600 songs and scored music for over a thousand films. His body of work is so vast that musicians often spend their entire careers merely studying it. People see the genius. What they don't see are the countless nights when music occupied his mind while the rest of the world slept.

One may ask, "How is this humanly possible?"

The answer is simple. To such people, work is not work.

It is rest.

It is sleep.

It is meditation.

It is joy.

What exhausts ordinary people rejuvenates them.

When a passionate scientist spends fourteen hours in a laboratory, outsiders call it overwork. The scientist calls it Tuesday.

When a musician composes through the night, people wonder why he has no work-life balance. The musician wonders why everyone else is wasting so much time.

The greatest contributors in science were no different.

Isaac Newton spent years obsessing over mathematics, gravity, optics, and astronomy. He famously remarked that if he had seen further, it was by standing on the shoulders of giants—but history also tells us that he often forgot food and social engagements while immersed in his work.

Nikola Tesla slept only a few hours a day and devoted his entire life to electrical engineering and invention. His obsession was so extreme that he sacrificed wealth, relationships, and personal comfort.

Marie Curie spent countless hours in laboratories processing tonnes of pitchblende to isolate radium, ultimately sacrificing her health in pursuit of scientific discovery. Today, we celebrate her Nobel Prizes. Few appreciate the physical and emotional cost at which they came.

Closer to our times, A.P.J. Abdul Kalam dedicated his life to science and national service. His colleagues often speak of his simplicity and relentless commitment to his mission. He never treated work as an obligation; it was his purpose.

The same story repeats itself across every field.

Sachin Tendulkar was obsessed with batting long before India became obsessed with him.

Viswanathan Anand spent thousands of hours studying positions that most people would find incomprehensible.

Steve Jobs was notoriously uncompromising in his pursuit of design perfection.

Elon Musk sleeps on factory floors when necessary.

Thomas Edison famously tested thousands of materials before inventing the practical incandescent lamp.

These individuals had different talents, different personalities, and different circumstances. They shared only one thing—a beautiful kind of madness.

But greatness always comes at a price.

There is something we seldom discuss when we glorify success.

Passion is expensive.

Every extraordinary achievement is paid for using an invisible currency:

  • Time.
  • Relationships.
  • Comfort.
  • Leisure.
  • Predictability.
  • Social acceptance.
  • Personal sacrifices.

No individual in history has managed to become extraordinary while simultaneously being extraordinary in every other aspect of life.

When someone gives 120% to one thing, something else necessarily receives less.

The scientist may miss family functions.

The musician may sacrifice vacations.

The entrepreneur may sacrifice weekends.

The writer may sacrifice sleep.

The sportsperson may sacrifice celebrations.

The researcher may sacrifice promotions elsewhere for the love of a problem worth solving.

Society often demands greatness while simultaneously demanding perfect work-life balance. Unfortunately, history suggests that the two rarely coexist at the highest levels.

This is not an argument against family, health, or personal commitments. They are immensely important. Rather, it is an acknowledgment of reality: excellence has a price tag attached to it.

Nobody accidentally composes 8,600 songs.

Nobody accidentally sings 48,000 songs.

Nobody accidentally wins Nobel Prizes.

Nobody accidentally changes science, technology, music, sports, or literature.

The world calls it talent because talent sounds pleasant. But talent is merely the admission ticket. Obsession buys the seat.

There is another interesting observation about highly passionate people. They do not wait for motivation. Motivation is temporary. Passion is permanent.

An ordinary person asks, "How many hours do I need to work?"

A passionate person asks, "How much more can I do today?"

An ordinary person waits for weekends.

A passionate person forgets what day of the week it is.

An ordinary person needs a holiday from work.

A passionate person often needs work as a holiday from life's other problems.

The line between work and life slowly disappears.

Ironically, society may initially call such people foolish, unbalanced, or even mad. History later calls them visionaries.

Perhaps madness is simply passion that the majority does not yet understand.

So if you wish to make history in whatever field you choose—music, science, engineering, teaching, sports, writing, business, or public service—you must find something worth being mad about.

Find that one thing that excites you enough to forget time.

Find that one problem that keeps you awake at night.

Find that one pursuit that feels easier than resting.

Because history has a peculiar habit.

It remembers the passionate. It remembers the obsessed. It remembers the mad.

The rest of us merely remember their names.

After all, only mad people make history.
