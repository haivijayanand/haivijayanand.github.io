Laplace Transforms, Transfer Functions and Poles

The Laplace transform turns calculus into algebra: derivatives become multiplications by s and initial conditions drop in automatically. It also gives every linear system a transfer function whose poles, plotted in the s-plane, tell you at a glance how it responds. Drag the poles yourself and watch.

3Solving an equation in three steps

y″ + 3y′ + 2y = 1 (a unit step), y(0) = y′(0) = 01. Transform: s²Y + 3sY + 2Y = 1/s 2. Solve for Y: Y = 1 / (s·(s + 1)·(s + 2)) Partial fractions: Y = (1/2)/s − 1/(s + 1) + (1/2)/(s + 2) (cover-up: at s = 0 → 1/(1·2) = ½; s = −1 → 1/(−1·1) = −1; s = −2 → 1/(−2·−1) = ½) 3. Invert: y(t) = ½ − e^(−t) + ½·e^(−2t) Check: y(0) = ½ − 1 + ½ = 0 ✓ y(∞) = ½ ✓ (final value theorem: lim s·Y as s→0 = ½)

4Transfer functions, poles and zeros

With zero initial conditions, output over input in the s-domain is a ratio of polynomials — the transfer function:

G(s) = Y(s)/U(s) e.g. first order: K/(τs + 1) second order: ωn² / (s² + 2ζωn·s + ωn²)

Roots of the denominator are poles; roots of the numerator are zeros. Each pole p contributes a term ept to the response. So the s-plane is a map of behaviour:

  • Left half-plane (Re p < 0): decays → stable. Further left = faster.
  • Right half-plane: grows → unstable.
  • Imaginary part = oscillation frequency. On the jω axis: oscillates for ever.
  • For a pole pair at σ ± jω: ωn = distance from the origin, ζ = cos of the angle from the negative real axis.
Overshoot to a step: Mp = e−πζ/√(1−ζ²) peak time tp = π/ωd 2 % settling ts ≈ 4/(ζωn) = 4/|σ|

Put s = jω into G and you get the frequency response of Day 6. Time response, frequency response and pole positions are three views of the same object.

Convolution, the impulse response and the final value theorem

The inverse transform of G(s) itself is the impulse response g(t). Because a product in s is a convolution in time, the response to any input is y(t) = ∫₀ᵗ g(τ)·u(t − τ) dτ — the output is the input "smeared" by the system's memory. The final value theorem limt→∞ y(t) = lims→0 s·Y(s) holds only if y actually settles, i.e. all poles of s·Y are in the left half-plane; apply it to an unstable system and it gives a confident, wrong answer.

5Feedback moves poles

Take a slow plant G = K/(τs + 1) with K = 2, τ = 5 s, and close a proportional loop with gain Kp = 4:

Closed loop Y/R = KpK / (τs + 1 + KpK)Open-loop pole: s = −1/τ = −0.2 → time constant 5 s Closed-loop pole: s = −(1 + 8)/5 = −1.8 → time constant 0.56 s (9× faster) Steady-state error to a step = 1/(1 + K_p·K) = 1/9 = 11 % (an integral term removes it)

Control design is, at heart, the art of putting poles where you want them. Push the gain too far on a higher-order plant and a pole pair crosses into the right half-plane: the loop oscillates.

Where you meet thisSpeed and torque control of dynamometers and motors, temperature controllers on test rigs, suspension and engine-mount design, filter design in data acquisition (Butterworth poles sit on a circle), PLL clock circuits, aircraft autopilots.

6Lab: drag the poles

Drag the × poles around the s-plane and watch the step or impulse response and the frequency response change together. Move them left: faster decay. Up: faster ringing, more overshoot. Across the jω axis: growth. Choose "pole pair + zero" and drag the ○ zero into the right half-plane: the step response starts the wrong way — a non-minimum-phase system (like the level in a boiler drum after a sudden steam demand, or the dip in altitude when an aircraft pulls up).

7Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Use the transform for linear, constant-coefficient systems. For nonlinear or time-varying ones it does not apply.
  • A pole close to a zero nearly cancels it: the term exists but with a tiny coefficient.
  • The slowest (right-most) stable poles dominate the response: they are the last to decay.
  • A system with any pole in the right half-plane is unstable even if that pole's term starts tiny — it will win eventually.

8Practice

  1. Solve y′ + 4y = 0, y(0) = 3 by Laplace transform.
  2. Find poles, ωn and ζ of G = 25/(s² + 4s + 25). Predict the overshoot.
  3. Solve y″ + y = sin 2t, y(0) = y′(0) = 0.
  4. In the lab, place the poles to give about 10 % overshoot and settle within 2 s. Where do they sit?
