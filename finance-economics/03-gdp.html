GDP

Gross Domestic Product: the single number used to measure an economy. What it counts and what it leaves out, how India measures it, why "big" is not the same as "rich", and what growth means for you. Pick your level.

Level 1 · For school students

GDP is just a very big sum: how much a whole country makes in a year.

1What is GDP?

GDP (Gross Domestic Product) is the total value of all the final goods and services produced inside a country in a year (or a quarter).

Imagine adding up everything made and done in India in one year: every bag of rice, mobile phone, haircut, bus ride, school class, doctor's visit and software project. Add up their prices and you get India's GDP. It tells us how big the economy is, and whether it is growing or shrinking.

GrossThe total, before anything is subtracted.
DomesticMade inside the country, whether by Indian or foreign companies.
ProductThings made (goods) and things done (services).

3Three ways to count, one answer

🏭 What we makeAdd the value each farm, factory and shop adds.
🛒 What we spendAdd what families, businesses and the government spend on final goods, plus what we sell to other countries, minus what we buy from them.
💰 What we earnAdd everyone's wages, rents, interest and profits.

These should all give the same total, because every rupee one person spends is a rupee someone else earns, for something that someone made.

4A growing economy

When this year's GDP is bigger than last year's, the economy is growing. The growth rate tells us by how much. In April–June 2026, India's economy grew 7.8% compared with the same months a year earlier. That is one of the fastest rates of any large country.

One catch: if prices go up (inflation, Topic 01), GDP measured in rupees also goes up, even if we did not make anything more. So economists remove the effect of rising prices and report real GDP, which counts only the extra things actually produced.

5Big is not the same as rich

Total GDP: how bigIndia has one of the world's largest economies: 6th in 2026, and expected to be 4th in 2027.
GDP per person: how richDivide GDP by the number of people. India has more than 140 crore people, so each person's share is still much smaller than in countries like Japan or Germany, which have far fewer people.
An exampleA class of 40 students with ₹4,000 in total has more money than a class of 10 with ₹2,000. But each student has ₹100 in the first class and ₹200 in the second.

6What GDP does not count

  • Work at home: cooking, cleaning and caring for grandparents are valuable, but nobody pays for them, so they are not in GDP.
  • Nature: cutting down a forest adds timber to GDP, but the lost forest is not subtracted.
  • Happiness and health: a country can grow richer while people feel more stressed.
  • Who gets what: GDP can rise even if most of the gain goes to a few people.

So GDP is a very useful number, but it is not a score for how good life is.

Level 2 · For working people

How to read a GDP release, why rankings move with the rupee, and how growth reaches jobs, salaries, taxes and markets.

1Who measures India's GDP, and when

  • The National Statistics Office (NSO), under MoSPI, publishes quarterly GDP about two months after each quarter ends, usually on the last working day of February, May, August and November.
  • Annual estimates come in stages: first advance estimates (January), second advance (February), provisional (end of May), then first, second and third revised estimates over the following years. Each one uses more complete data.
  • A new base year. On 27 February 2026, MoSPI moved the series from base 2011-12 to 2022-23, with new producer price indices, a rebased index of industrial production and newer administrative data. The new series changed history: FY 2023-24 growth fell from 9.2% to 7.2%, FY 2024-25 rose from 6.5% to 7.1%, and the economy came out 3–4% smaller in nominal terms.

Takeaway: treat the first GDP print as a draft. Watch the revisions and the trend over several quarters, not one headline.

2Reading the release: GVA, GDP and demand

GDP = GVA (value added by sectors) + product taxes − product subsidies GDP = C (private consumption) + G (government consumption) + I (investment) + (X − M) (net exports)

In Q1 FY27, services grew fastest (about 10% for the tertiary sector), investment had its strongest quarter in four years, and lower imports (partly gold) meant net exports added to growth. Services make up roughly two-thirds of India's output.

3Nominal vs real, and the deflator

Nominal growth ≈ Real growth + GDP deflator inflation (1.103 / 1.078 − 1 ≈ 2.3% in Q1 FY27)
  • Real GDP measures volume: how much more was produced.
  • Nominal GDP measures money: it drives tax revenue, company sales and the ratios the government quotes (fiscal deficit as % of GDP, debt as % of GDP).
  • When the deflator is low, as in Q1 (about 2.3% while CPI was rising), nominal growth is weak even when real growth is strong. That squeezes tax collections and makes debt ratios harder to bring down.

4What GDP growth means for you

Jobs and salariesFaster growth usually means more hiring and better increments, but India has seen periods of "jobless growth", when output rose faster than employment.
Company earnings and marketsCorporate profits track nominal GDP over the long run, which is one reason equity markets watch the GDP print.
Government budgetTax revenue grows with nominal GDP. Faster growth funds roads, schools and subsidies without raising tax rates.
Interest ratesVery strong growth plus high inflation can lead the RBI to raise rates (Topic 02). The RBI raised its FY27 growth forecast to 7.1% even as it hiked in October 2026.
Your own "GDP"A household's income growth is its own growth rate. The average for the country can hide very different experiences across sectors, states and income groups.

5Size and rankings: why the rupee matters

Global rankings convert each country's GDP into US dollars at market exchange rates. So India's rank depends on three things: real growth, inflation, and the rupee.

  • India slipped to 6th by nominal GDP in 2026, behind the UK and Japan, because the rupee weakened and the new base year shrank nominal GDP. The IMF projects India to overtake Japan in 2027 at about $4.52 trillion.
  • By purchasing-power parity (PPP), which adjusts for the fact that most things cost less in India, the economy is the world's 3rd largest.

6Per person and fairness

  • GDP per capita divides output by population. India's is still a small fraction of advanced economies' levels, which is why a large total can coexist with modest average incomes.
  • Distribution matters: the average can rise while the median household sees little change. Indicators of wages, employment and consumption spending complete the picture.
  • Regional gaps are large. State GDP (GSDP) per person varies several-fold across Indian states.

7The global picture

  • The IMF expects world growth of 3.1% in 2026, slower than 2025, as an energy-price shock from the war in the Middle East is partly offset by strong AI-related investment.
  • The US is forecast to grow 2.3%; India about 7% (FY 2026-27), the fastest among major economies.
  • Recession is often shorthand for two quarters in a row of falling real GDP. Official bodies, such as the NBER in the US, use a broader judgement based on jobs, incomes and output.
  • Slower growth in India's trading partners hurts exports of goods and IT services; faster growth in India raises demand for imported oil, electronics and machinery.

8How to read GDP news sensibly

  • Check whether a figure is year-on-year or quarter-on-quarter, and real or nominal.
  • Look at GVA, investment and consumption, not only the headline.
  • Remember the base effect: a weak quarter last year makes this year's growth look strong.
  • Expect revisions, and compare with high-frequency indicators (GST collections, PMI, power demand, credit growth).

Level 3 · Review for experts

National-accounts identities, India's measurement debates, growth accounting, the output gap and the "beyond GDP" agenda.

1Framework and identities (SNA 2008)

Production: GDP = Σ GVA_basic + (product taxes − product subsidies) Expenditure: GDP = C + G + I (GFCF + Δinventories + valuables) + X − M (+ discrepancies) Income: GDP = compensation of employees + operating surplus / mixed income + CFC + net production taxes GNI = GDP + net primary income from abroad; NNI = GNI − CFC; NDI = GNI + net current transfers
  • Production boundary. GDP includes market output, own-account goods and imputed owner-occupied rent; it excludes unpaid household services. That choice, rather than measurement error, explains much of the "what GDP misses" critique.
  • Discrepancies. In India, GDP is compiled mainly from the production side, and the expenditure side carries a balancing "discrepancies" line. Large swings in this line make demand-side analysis less reliable.

2India's measurement debates

  • The 2022-23 rebasing (released 27 February 2026) introduced an output Producer Price Index, a Banking Services Price Index, a rebased index of industrial production and a double-deflation framework for manufacturing. The method follows the IMF Quarterly National Accounts Manual (2017).
  • Single vs double deflation. The 2011-12 series deflated manufacturing value added with an output price index alone. When input prices (commodities) fall faster than output prices, single deflation overstates real value added, and the opposite happens when they rise. Double deflation, which deflates outputs and inputs separately, removes this bias but is more volatile.
  • The informal sector. Unincorporated enterprises are estimated using indicators and benchmark surveys (ASUSE, PLFS). A shock that hits informal units harder than formal firms (demonetisation, GST, COVID) can be missed by formal-sector proxies.
  • Revision size. The rebasing cut FY24 growth from 9.2% to 7.2% and raised FY25 from 6.5% to 7.1%, a reminder that confidence bands on Indian growth are wide.

3Real GDP: index-number issues

  • Fixed-base versus chain-linked volumes. Fixed-base volumes overweight sectors whose relative prices fall (such as electronics) the further you move from the base year, which biases growth upwards. Chain-linking with annually updated weights fixes this, at the cost of making components non-additive. India still uses a fixed base, rebasing periodically.
  • The deflator gap. In Q1 FY27 the implicit deflator was about 2.3% (10.3% nominal, 7.8% real) while CPI inflation was rising, because the deflator weights wholesale and producer prices heavily. A low deflator flatters real growth if output prices are measured too low.
  • GDP vs GDI. In principle they are equal. Comparing real GDP with real gross domestic income (GDI) adjusted for terms of trade shows how much of the output gain households can actually spend, which matters for an oil importer.

4Growth theory and growth accounting

Y = A·K^α·L^(1−α) → g_Y = g_A + α·g_K + (1−α)·g_L (Solow residual g_A = TFP growth)
  • Solow (1956): long-run growth per worker comes from technology. Capital deepening raises the level of output, not its long-run growth rate. This predicts conditional convergence: poorer economies grow faster, other things being equal.
  • Endogenous growth (Romer, Lucas, Aghion–Howitt): ideas, human capital and creative destruction make growth depend on policy, institutions and research and development.
  • For India: the sustainability of 7%+ growth depends on investment rates, labour-force participation (especially women's), and productivity gains from moving workers out of agriculture.

The inputs are illustrative. Measured capital shares and labour-quality adjustments for India vary widely by study (KLEMS, Penn World Table).

5The short run: potential output and the cycle

Output gap = (Y − Y*) / Y* Okun: Δu ≈ −β·(g_Y − g*)
  • Potential output Y* is unobservable. It is estimated with filters (HP, Hamilton) or with production-function and semi-structural models, all of which are revised heavily at the end of the sample.
  • A positive output gap (Y above Y*) adds to inflation through the Phillips curve (Topic 01). That is why the RBI tightened in October 2026 while also raising its growth forecast.
  • Okun's law is weak in India because of the large informal sector and self-employment. Unemployment does not capture slack well, so underemployment and participation rates matter more.
  • Nowcasting: dynamic factor models on high-frequency indicators (GST, e-way bills, electricity, PMI, credit, vehicle sales) bridge the two-month publication lag.

6Beyond GDP

  • Stiglitz–Sen–Fitoussi Commission (2009): shift the emphasis from production to household income and consumption, include distribution, and measure sustainability separately.
  • SEEA (System of Environmental-Economic Accounting): accounts for natural capital and its depletion, including "green GDP" adjustments.
  • HDI, multidimensional poverty, time-use surveys (unpaid care work) and wellbeing measures.
  • The digital economy: free services (search, maps, messaging) add consumer surplus but little measured GDP. Mismeasurement is real, but studies find it is too small to explain the productivity slowdown.

7International comparison

  • Market-exchange-rate rankings mix real performance with currency moves. India's slip to 6th in 2026 reflected rupee depreciation and the rebasing, not slower real growth.
  • PPP (from the World Bank's International Comparison Program) adjusts for price levels, which is why India ranks 3rd. PPP estimates are better for comparing living standards; market rates are better for external purchasing power, debt service and trade.
  • Fiscal year versus calendar year. The IMF reports India's FY 2026-27 forecast as 7.0% and the calendar-2026 forecast as 7.4%, a common source of confusion when comparing countries.

8Review checklist: open questions

  1. How large is India's growth confidence band once rebasing and the discrepancies line are taken into account?
  2. Does double deflation make quarterly manufacturing GVA too volatile to use for policy?
  3. How should informal-sector output be measured between benchmark surveys?
  4. Is India's potential growth closer to 6.5% or 8%, and what do labour-force participation trends imply?
  5. Should India move to chain-linked volume measures?
  6. How should environmental depletion and unpaid care work enter headline aggregates?
