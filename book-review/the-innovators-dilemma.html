The Innovator's Dilemma

Clayton M. Christensen · first published 1997 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Well-run companies can fail precisely because they do the sensible things good management teaches, which leaves them blind to disruptive technologies.

Summary

Clayton Christensen set out to answer a puzzling question: why do leading companies, run by capable managers, so often lose their position when a new technology arrives? They usually fail not through laziness or poor judgement, but because they follow the rules of good management too faithfully.

He separates two kinds of technical change. Sustaining technologies improve existing products along the lines that current customers already value. Established firms are very good at these, even when the improvements are difficult. Disruptive technologies are different. At first they perform worse on the measures mainstream customers care about, but they are cheaper, simpler, smaller or more convenient, and they appeal to new or less demanding buyers.

Christensen's main evidence comes from the disk drive industry, where each new, smaller drive size was pioneered by newcomers while the leaders stayed with their existing customers. He finds similar patterns in mechanical excavators, steel and other industries.

Leaders struggle with disruption for sound reasons. They listen closely to their best customers, who do not want the new product. Small, uncertain markets cannot satisfy a large company's need for growth. Their cost structures and profit expectations push them towards higher-margin products. And because the new markets do not yet exist, they cannot be analysed with the usual forecasts.

Meanwhile, the disruptive technology keeps improving. Since established products often improve faster than customers need, the newcomer eventually becomes good enough for the mainstream, and the incumbent is overtaken.

The remedy Christensen proposes is organisational. Give responsibility for a disruptive technology to a separate unit small enough to get excited about small wins, attached to customers who want it, and free to learn through trial rather than detailed plans. Managers must accept that they are discovering a market, not executing a known one.

The book's lasting lesson is that good management, applied in the wrong situation, can be the cause of failure.

314 words

One-line takeaway: Watch for cheaper, simpler products that your best customers dismiss, and give them a separate home where they can grow on their own terms.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What is the 'dilemma' at the heart of the book?

    Answer C. Christensen argues that listening to customers and chasing high margins, normally good practice, is exactly what makes incumbents vulnerable.

  2. A sustaining technology is one that:

    Answer A. Sustaining innovations make existing products better for current customers, and established firms usually lead them.

  3. How does a disruptive technology typically perform when it first appears?

    Answer D. Disruptive products start out inferior on what mainstream buyers value, yet offer other benefits to new or less demanding customers.

  4. Which industry provides Christensen's main body of evidence?

    Answer B. He studied the disk drive industry in depth, where successive smaller drive sizes repeatedly toppled the leaders.

  5. Why does listening closely to the best customers become a trap?

    Answer B. Mainstream customers reject the initially inferior product, steering the firm's resources away from it.

  6. Why are small emerging markets unattractive to large established firms?

    Answer D. A big company needs large new revenues to grow, so a tiny uncertain market seems not worth the effort.

  7. Why do disruptive products eventually threaten the incumbent's core market?

    Answer A. As the disruptive technology improves, it meets mainstream needs while keeping its cost or convenience advantage.

  8. What does Christensen mean by performance oversupply?

    Answer C. When products exceed what customers need, the basis of competition shifts, opening space for simpler, cheaper rivals.

  9. Why are traditional market forecasts of little help for disruptive technologies?

    Answer D. Markets that have not formed cannot be measured, so managers must learn by experimenting rather than planning.

  10. What organisational remedy does the book recommend?

    Answer B. A small independent unit can value small wins, serve the right customers and work with a cost structure suited to the new market.
