    Fluid mechanics is one great PDE system — mass, momentum and energy conservation — and almost nobody solves it whole. Engineering progress came from knowing what to throw away: viscosity (Bernoulli), inertia (Stokes, Reynolds lubrication), the downstream direction (boundary layers), or two space dimensions (pipes). Each card shows one such simplification.

     ρ·(∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = −∇p + μ·∇²u + f ∇·u = 0 (incompressible) u velocity field · p pressure · μ viscosity · f body force · Reynolds number Re = ρUL/μ = inertia/viscous

    Newton's second law for every fluid particle: acceleration (including the nonlinear "carried along" term u·∇u) equals pressure push plus viscous rubbing plus gravity. The nonlinearity makes turbulence possible; whether smooth solutions always exist in 3-D is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems. The Reynolds number decides the character of the flow: below about 2300 in a pipe, smooth and laminar; far above, turbulent.

    For flow along x over a plate that starts moving at speed U, only u(y, t) survives: ∂u/∂t = ν·∂²u/∂y². It is the heat equation (ν = μ/ρ plays the role of α), so u = U·erfc(y/(2√(νt))): momentum diffuses into the fluid, and the "boundary layer" thickens like √(νt). The lab shows this exact special case.
