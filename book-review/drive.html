Drive

Daniel H. Pink · first published 2009 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

For creative, complex work, people do their best not for rewards and punishments but out of a desire for autonomy, mastery and purpose.

Summary

Daniel Pink argues that most organisations run on an outdated idea of what motivates people. He describes motivation as an operating system. The first version was about survival. The second, which still shapes most workplaces, assumes people seek rewards and avoid punishments, so managers pull the levers of pay, bonuses and penalties. Pink says this carrot-and-stick model fits poorly with how modern work is done.

Drawing on decades of research in psychology and economics, he shows that conditional rewards work reasonably well for simple, routine tasks with a clear set of steps. For work that needs judgement, creativity or problem-solving, however, offering a prize for performance often narrows people's focus and makes results worse. Such rewards can also crowd out genuine interest, encourage shortcuts and cheating, become habit-forming, and push people towards short-term thinking.

Pink proposes an upgrade built on intrinsic motivation: the drive to do something because it is interesting, satisfying and meaningful. He contrasts two behaviour patterns. One is fuelled mainly by external rewards; the other by the inherent satisfaction of the activity. The second, he argues, can be learned and tends to produce better performance, health and well-being.

This upgraded approach rests on three elements. Autonomy means giving people control over what they work on, when, how and with whom. Mastery is the urge to get better at something that matters, which demands effort, persistence and challenges pitched just beyond current ability. Purpose is the wish to serve a cause larger than oneself, and organisations that place purpose beside profit tend to engage people more deeply.

Pink does not dismiss money. Pay must be fair and adequate so that it stops being a distraction. Once that baseline is met, leaders get more from creating conditions for self-direction, growth and meaning than from tightening controls.

297 words

One-line takeaway: Pay people fairly enough to take money off the table, then motivate creative work through autonomy, mastery and purpose rather than carrots and sticks.

    Answer C. The research Pink cites shows if-then rewards can lift performance on rule-based, routine work but not on complex, creative work.

  3. What often happens when people are offered a performance reward for a creative problem?

    Answer A. Contingent rewards narrow attention, which hurts tasks that need broad thinking and fresh approaches.

    Answer D. Pink's concerns are about reduced interest, unethical shortcuts, addiction to rewards and short-termism, not about measuring routine work.

  5. What is intrinsic motivation?

    Answer C. Intrinsic motivation comes from the activity itself rather than from outside rewards or threats.

  6. Which three elements form the core of Pink's upgraded approach to motivation?

    Answer B. Pink builds his model on people's desire to direct their own lives, to improve, and to serve something larger.

  7. In Pink's account, autonomy at work includes control over:

    Answer D. He describes autonomy across task, time, technique and team.

  8. What kind of challenge best supports the pursuit of mastery?

    Answer A. Mastery grows when tasks stretch people without overwhelming them, keeping them engaged and improving.

  9. What role does money play in Pink's view of motivation?

    Answer C. Pink says baseline pay must be fair so that people can focus on the work itself rather than on compensation.

    Answer B. Pink argues that connecting work to a larger cause, alongside profit, deepens commitment and effort.
