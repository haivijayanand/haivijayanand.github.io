@sub How much of the AI-driven surge in data-centre electricity can better operation and efficiency upgrades absorb, layer by layer, with worked numbers and a Chennai case
@outline The size and shape of the surge | PUE and the stalled average | Software, models and IT utilisation | Cooling: affinity laws, warmer water, liquid | Power delivery: fewer conversions, 800 V DC | Grid, heat and water | India and a Chennai retrofit case | How much efficiency can absorb
@tnotes Data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, and the IEA expects that to more than double by 2030. Grids and power plants take far longer to build than data halls. So the practical question is: how much of this surge can we absorb by running existing and new facilities better? This talk works through it layer by layer, with numbers.

K: The surge | 415 → 945 TWh | fig2
- 2024: ≈415 TWh, 1.5 % of world electricity
- +17 % in 2025 alone (all electricity +3 %)
- IEA Base Case: ≈945 TWh by 2030; AI-focused demand roughly triples
N: Data-centre electricity reached about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024 and grew 17 per cent in 2025 alone. The IEA base case more than doubles it by 2030. At world scale this is still a few per cent of demand, but it bites locally: connection queues, delayed projects and strained city grids.

F: Five layers between the grid and useful work | fig1
- Software and models → IT hardware → cooling → power delivery → grid
- Each layer loses energy and has its own levers
- A watt saved in software is also saved in conversion and cooling
N: The paper treats a data centre as a chain of five layers. Each loses energy and each has its own efficiency levers. The order matters: savings at the top of the chain, in software and IT, are multiplied because every watt not used there is also not converted and not cooled.

E: Power usage effectiveness | eq1,eq2,eq3
W: PUE = facility energy / IT energy. Overhead share = (PUE − 1)/PUE; site saving from PUE₁ to PUE₂ = 1 − PUE₂/PUE₁. At PUE 1.54, 35 % of site energy is overhead; at 1.30, 23 %.
N: PUE is facility energy divided by IT energy. Two simple consequences are useful: the overhead share of site energy, and the saving from improving PUE. At the current global average of 1.54, more than a third of site energy never reaches a server.

F: The stalled average | fig3
- Global average PUE: 2.50 (2007) → 1.58 (2018) → ≈1.54 since 2020
- Best facilities operate near 1.1
- Fleet at 1.30 would cut 2024 use by 64.7 TWh (15.6 %)
N: The Uptime Institute surveys show the average PUE fell quickly until about 2018 and has hardly moved since. Yet the best sites run near 1.1. Simply lifting the global fleet to 1.30 would save about 65 terawatt-hours a year, roughly 15.6 per cent. That is the size of the opportunity in the facility alone.

T: Software and model-level levers | tab3 | cols=0,1,2
N: The cheapest watts are in software. Routing queries to the smallest adequate model, quantisation, batching, caching and efficient scheduling can cut energy per query by large factors. One worked example shows routing alone cutting energy by 63 per cent, while a shift to agentic workloads using a hundred times more compute per task can multiply demand by eleven.

F: Servers that do nothing | fig5
- Linear power model: P(u) = P_idle + (P_max − P_idle)·u
- At 12 % utilisation each unit of work costs far more energy than at 50 %
- Consolidating 100 servers at 12 % onto 24 at 50 %: −66 % IT power
- 25 % comatose servers in a 4 000-server hall: ≈2 700 MWh/yr
N: Servers draw a large fraction of peak power even when idle. At 12 per cent utilisation, each unit of work is very expensive. Consolidating a hundred lightly used servers onto twenty-four busier ones cuts IT power by two-thirds. And comatose servers, still powered but doing nothing, are common; removing them in one example hall saves 2 700 megawatt-hours a year.

E: Cooling: fans, pumps and chillers | eq5,eq6
W: Affinity laws: flow ∝ N, head ∝ N², power ∝ N³. Carnot COP = T_e/(T_c − T_e): raising the evaporating temperature cuts the lift and the compressor power.
N: Cooling is the largest overhead and it obeys the classical laws of rotating machinery and refrigeration. Fan power goes with the cube of speed, so running all N plus one fans slowly instead of N fans fast saves about 30 per cent. Chiller COP depends on the temperature lift, so raising chilled water from 7 to 18 degrees cuts compressor power by about a third.

F: Chiller COP and the case for warmer water | fig7
- ≈3.7 % compressor power per kelvin of chilled-water temperature
- Liquid cooling allows 30–45 °C water: chiller-free most of the year
- In hot, humid Chennai, dry coolers + warm water beat evaporative towers on water
N: This chart shows chiller COP rising steeply with chilled-water temperature. Each kelvin is worth about 3.7 per cent of compressor power. Direct liquid cooling of AI chips accepts water at 30 to 45 degrees, which can remove the chiller for most of the year even in a hot climate, and saves water too.

T: Cooling-plant faults that waste energy first | tab4
N: Rotating-machinery faults usually waste energy long before they cause a failure. Fouled condenser tubes raise the chiller lift, misaligned or unbalanced fans and pumps draw more power, and stuck valves upset the water temperatures. Vibration and performance monitoring, the same techniques used on test rigs, catch these early and keep the efficiency gains from drifting away.

F: Power delivery: fewer conversions | fig8
- Legacy chain delivers 83.1 % of grid power to the chips
- Upgraded chain (eco-mode UPS, Titanium PSUs, fewer stages): 93.6 %
- 100 MW IT: −13.6 MW, −119 GWh/yr
- 800 V DC: −51 % conductor loss vs 415 V AC
N: Every conversion stage loses a few per cent, and the losses multiply. A legacy chain delivers only 83 per cent of grid power to the chips; a modern one with eco-mode UPS, Titanium-grade power supplies and fewer conversions delivers about 94 per cent. For a 100 megawatt campus that is 13.6 megawatts. Moving to 800 volt DC distribution halves conductor losses and makes 200 kilowatt racks feasible.

B: Working with the grid, heat and water
- **Flexibility:** curtailing 0.25 % of hours (≈22 h/yr) eases connection
- **Smoothing:** 0.75 MWh storage tames a 30 MW AI training swing
- **Heat reuse:** 20 MW IT + heat pump (COP 4) → 20 MW heat, 166 GWh/yr
- **Water:** WUE 1.8 → 0.1 L/kWh cuts 4.3 to 0.24 million L/day for 100 MW IT
N: Working with the grid may save little energy but connects new load faster. A campus that agrees to curtail for about 22 hours a year can often be connected years sooner. A small battery smooths the rapid swings of AI training. Waste heat can be upgraded by heat pumps for district heating, and closed-loop cooling cuts water use by over 90 per cent, which matters in water-stressed cities.

F: India and Chennai | fig9
- India's IT capacity heading above 4 GW by 2030
- Chennai: second hub, cable landings, hot humid water-stressed coast
- Fleet PUE 1.3 instead of 1.6: −10.7 TWh/yr (≈1.4 GW coal) by 2030
- Chennai cluster could reach 5.3 % of Tamil Nadu peak
N: India's data-centre capacity is growing fast, towards more than 4 gigawatts by 2030, and Chennai is the second hub after Mumbai thanks to its submarine cable landings. A fleet PUE of 1.3 instead of 1.6 would save about 10.7 terawatt-hours a year by 2030, the output of about 1.4 gigawatts of coal plant. The Chennai cluster alone could rise from under 2 to over 5 per cent of Tamil Nadu's peak demand.

T: Worked site case: 4 MW colocation hall in Chennai | tab8
N: Here is a step-by-step retrofit of a 4 megawatt IT hall at PUE 1.75. Removing comatose servers, containment and higher set points, slow-running fans and variable speed drives, warmer chilled water, a better UPS and power supplies, analytics and lighting together cut site power by 25.8 per cent, about 15 800 megawatt-hours and 12.6 crore rupees a year, and free 1.25 megawatts of headroom for new IT load without a new grid connection.

E: Measure what you manage | eq9
W: Chiller load from flow and temperature difference: Q = ṁ c_p ΔT. Example: 120 kg/s at ΔT 6 K → COP 5.0 (0.70 kW/TR); a 'low ΔT' of 4 K can need up to 3.4× the pump power.
N: None of this is possible without metering. A proper hierarchy of meters and heat meters lets you trend chiller COP, pump and fan specific power, and the chilled-water temperature difference. Low delta-T syndrome, for example, silently multiplies pump power, and only shows up if you measure.

K: How much of the surge can efficiency absorb | ⅓ to ⅔ | fig11
- Indicative 2030 estimate beyond the IEA Base Case
- Low push removes ≈169 TWh; high push considerably more
- Efficiency buys time; it does not replace new clean generation and grid
N: On the author's indicative estimate, a determined efficiency push across all layers could offset between one-third and two-thirds of the growth expected to 2030. Three caveats: rebound, as cheaper computation invites more of it; uncertainty in the forecasts; and the fact that efficiency does not remove the need for new clean supply. But it is the fastest and cheapest resource available.

C: Conclusions
- Surge is real and fast: 415 → ≈945 TWh by 2030; sharpest effect is local
- Fleet PUE stuck near 1.54; 1.30 alone saves 15.6 %
- Biggest savings in IT and software: multiplied through conversion and cooling
- Cube law, chiller lift and conversion chain each recover several per cent
- India: warm-water liquid cooling, closed-loop heat rejection, solar-hour load shifting
FW: Recommendations
- Report PUE, WUE and useful-work metrics together
- Retire comatose servers; consolidate; route to smallest adequate model
- Warmer water, liquid cooling, 800 V DC in all new AI halls
- Flexible-connection agreements with utilities
N: To conclude, efficiency could offset one-third to two-thirds of the expected growth to 2030. It is the fastest, cheapest and most certain resource we have, and it buys the time that building new generation and transmission requires.
