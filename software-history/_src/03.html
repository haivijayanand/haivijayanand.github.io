01The requirement: the planner's paper ledger

Before 1979, a financial plan was a large sheet of columnar paper. Change one assumption — the growth rate, a price, a salary — and every dependent figure had to be recalculated by hand with a calculator and rubbed out with an eraser. Mainframe planning languages existed but needed programmers and computer time.

Dan Bricklin, a Harvard MBA student who had worked as a programmer, imagined a "magic blackboard": a grid where changing one number instantly changed all the others. With Bob Frankston he wrote it for the Apple II at their company Software Arts. Published by Personal Software (later VisiCorp) as VisiCalc, it sold for about US$ 100 and turned a hobbyist machine into a business tool.

04Version explorer

Blue chips: VisiCalc and Lotus 1-2-3. Amber chips: Excel.

The pattern: VisiCalc won the Apple II, but was slow to exploit the IBM PC's 640 KB. Lotus won the PC, but was late and weak on Windows. Excel was built for graphical interfaces from day one and was ready when Windows 3.0 arrived. Each leader lost at the next platform shift.

05Patents and lawsuits

  • No patent: Bricklin was advised in 1979 that software was unlikely to be patentable, so the spreadsheet idea itself was never protected. Anyone could copy the concept.
  • Lotus v. Paperback Software (1990): Lotus won against a close 1-2-3 clone, discouraging direct copies.
  • Lotus v. Borland (1990–1996): Borland's Quattro Pro offered a 1-2-3-compatible menu tree so users' macros kept working. Lotus won at first, but the appeals court ruled in 1995 that a menu command hierarchy is a "method of operation" and not copyrightable; the Supreme Court split 4–4 in 1996, leaving that ruling in place. It remains a key case on software interfaces.

07Retirements

  • VisiCalc: Lotus bought Software Arts in 1985 and withdrew VisiCalc soon after.
  • Lotus 1-2-3: IBM bought Lotus in 1995. The last build was 1-2-3 Millennium Edition (9.8). IBM withdrew it from sale in June 2013 and support ended on 30 September 2014.
  • Excel: still the industry standard, now as a desktop, web and mobile application within Microsoft 365.

09Legacy hiding in your spreadsheet

  • The 1900 leap-year bug: Excel treats 29 February 1900 as a real date — copied deliberately from Lotus 1-2-3 so that old date serial numbers would match.
  • The slash key: Excel still offers "Lotus 1-2-3 help" and opens its menu shortcuts with /.
  • A1 notation, @SUM → =SUM, $ absolute references all trace back to VisiCalc.
  • Spreadsheet risk: because anyone can build a model, errors in unchecked spreadsheets have caused real financial losses — a reason engineers document and audit their sheets.

10Lessons for engineers

  1. Copy the user's mental model. The spreadsheet looked like the paper the accountant already used.
  2. Speed is a feature. 1-2-3 beat rivals by being visibly faster on the same PC.
  3. Platform shifts reset leadership. Being best on the old platform can make you late on the new one.
  4. Compatibility outlives products. A 1983 date bug still ships in 2026.
