How to Win Friends and Influence People

Dale Carnegie · first published 1936 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

People respond to warmth, respect and sincere attention, so dealing well with others is a skill built from small, learnable habits.

Summary

Dale Carnegie wrote this book after years of teaching adults to speak and deal with others more effectively. His central observation is that success at work and in life depends less on technical knowledge than on handling people well, and that this skill can be practised like any other. People are driven by feelings, pride and a deep wish to feel important, far more than by logic.

He begins with the basics of handling people. Criticism rarely changes anyone; it makes them defensive and resentful. Instead of condemning or complaining, offer honest, specific appreciation, and before asking anything of someone, think about what they want and show how your request serves it.

The next part explains how to make people like you. Take a real interest in others, smile, and remember and use their names. Listen well and encourage people to talk about themselves. Speak about what interests them, and make them feel valued, provided the respect is sincere rather than flattery.

Carnegie then turns to persuasion. Arguments are rarely won, because the loser simply digs in, so the wise course is to avoid them. Respect other people's opinions and never bluntly tell someone they are wrong. When you are mistaken, admit it quickly. Open in a friendly way, start with points of agreement, let the other person do most of the talking, and allow them to feel an idea is their own. Try to see the issue from their side and appeal to their better motives.

The final part addresses leading and correcting others. Begin with praise, point out mistakes indirectly, and mention your own errors first. Ask questions rather than issue orders, let people save face, praise every improvement, and give them a good reputation to live up to.

Underneath every rule is one idea: treat people with genuine consideration, and cooperation follows.

305 words

One-line takeaway: Replace criticism with sincere appreciation, listen more than you talk, and persuade by respecting the other person's point of view.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What does Carnegie see as the main driver of how people behave?

    Answer C. He argues that people are creatures of emotion and want above all to feel valued.

  2. Why does Carnegie advise against criticising, condemning and complaining?

    Answer A. Criticism wounds pride and provokes resentment rather than improvement.

  3. What should replace criticism, according to the book?

    Answer D. Genuine appreciation meets the deep need to feel important and encourages better effort.

  4. Which habit does Carnegie recommend for making people like you?

    Answer B. Good listening and interest in others make people feel valued and warm towards you.

  5. How does Carnegie distinguish appreciation from flattery?

    Answer C. He insists that praise must be honest and heartfelt, or people will see through it.

  6. What is Carnegie's view on winning arguments?

    Answer A. Even when you win on points, the other person feels defeated and holds on to their view.

  7. What should you do when you realise you are wrong?

    Answer B. Prompt admission disarms the other person and builds trust.

  8. Why does Carnegie suggest letting people feel an idea is their own?

    Answer D. Ownership of an idea makes people far more willing to act on it.

  9. When you must correct someone as a leader, Carnegie suggests you should:

    Answer A. Starting with honest praise and an indirect approach lets people accept correction without humiliation.

  10. What does giving someone 'a fine reputation to live up to' aim to do?

    Answer B. People tend to work hard to justify the positive qualities others see in them.
