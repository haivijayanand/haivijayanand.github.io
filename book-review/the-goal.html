The Goal

Eliyahu M. Goldratt · first published 1984 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

An organisation's performance is limited by a few constraints, so real improvement comes from identifying those bottlenecks and managing the whole system around them rather than optimising every part separately.

Summary

The Goal is a business novel. Alex Rogo manages a struggling manufacturing plant where orders ship late, inventory piles up and costs keep rising. His division head gives him three months to turn things around or the plant will be closed. Under pressure at work and with his marriage under strain, Alex turns for help to Jonah, a physicist he once knew, who guides him with questions rather than answers.

Jonah first asks what the goal of the company actually is. Alex realises it is to make money, now and in the future, and that busy machines and high local efficiency do not guarantee it. Jonah offers three simpler measures: throughput, the rate at which the system earns money through sales; inventory, the money tied up in things meant to be sold; and operational expense, the money spent turning inventory into throughput.

On a hike with his son's scout troop, Alex sees how a line of walkers behaves like his plant. Each step depends on the one before, and ordinary variations in speed add up, so the whole group can move only as fast as its slowest member.

Back at work, Alex and his team find their bottlenecks, the resources with less capacity than demand. They learn that time lost at a bottleneck is lost for the entire plant, while time saved elsewhere changes little. So they keep bottlenecks working on the right jobs, check quality before parts reach them, shift load away from them and release new work in step with their pace.

Results improve quickly. Alex later steps back to turn these lessons into a repeatable way of thinking: find the constraint, make the most of it, align everything else to it, raise its capacity, and then look for the next one.

294 words

One-line takeaway: Find the one constraint that limits your whole system, protect and strengthen it, and judge every decision by its effect on overall throughput.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. In what form is The Goal written?

    Answer B. Goldratt teaches his ideas through the story of Alex Rogo, who must save his failing manufacturing plant.

  2. According to the book, what is the goal of a manufacturing company?

    Answer D. Alex concludes that everything else, including efficiency and output, matters only if it helps the company make money.

  3. How does the book define throughput?

    Answer A. Throughput counts money earned through sales, not items produced that may simply sit in storage.

  4. What does Jonah mean by inventory?

    Answer C. Inventory is the money tied up in items meant for sale, which is why excess work-in-progress is a cost, not an achievement.

  5. What lesson does Alex learn from the scout hike?

    Answer D. The hike shows how dependent events combined with statistical fluctuations limit the whole line to its slowest point.

  6. What is a bottleneck in the book's sense?

    Answer A. A bottleneck cannot keep up with the work required of it, so it sets the pace for the whole system.

  7. Why does an hour lost at a bottleneck matter so much?

    Answer B. Because the bottleneck limits total output, any time it stands idle reduces throughput for the whole plant.

  8. What does the book suggest about saving time at a non-bottleneck resource?

    Answer C. Non-bottlenecks already have spare capacity, so making them faster does not increase what the system as a whole delivers.

  9. Which practice did Alex's team adopt to protect their bottlenecks?

    Answer A. Inspecting parts before the bottleneck avoids wasting its scarce time on items that would later be scrapped.

  10. What is the final step in the improvement process Alex develops?

    Answer D. Improvement is ongoing: after one constraint is lifted, another becomes the limit and the cycle begins again.
