Threaded Fasteners and Bolted Joints

Bolts hold most machines together. Learn thread standards, why preload is the secret of a reliable joint, the joint diagram, how much of an external load the bolt actually sees, tightening torque, and how to stop joints from separating or loosening.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: Bolted-joint diagram❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Read metric thread designations and use the tensile-stress area
  • Know property classes 8.8, 10.9 and 12.9 and proof load
  • Draw the joint diagram and calculate the joint constant C
  • Find bolt force, joint separation load and the load factor
  • Estimate tightening torque and choose a locking method

2Threads and strength

Metric ISO threads (IS 4218 / ISO 261) have a 60° profile. M12 means coarse pitch 1.75 mm; M12×1.25 is fine pitch. The stress in a bolt is calculated on the tensile-stress area At, between the root and pitch areas.

Property class 8.8: first digit × 100 = Sut (800 MPa); product of the digits × 10 = Sy (640 MPa). Proof strength Sp (≈ 0.9 Sy, 580–600 MPa for 8.8) is the stress the bolt must carry without permanent set. Nuts are graded to match (class 8 nut for 8.8 bolts).

3Why preload

Tightening the nut stretches the bolt and squeezes the clamped parts (members). When an external load P pulls the joint apart, the bolt stretches a little more and the members relax a little — but because the members are much stiffer than the bolt, most of P just reduces the clamping force; the bolt sees only a fraction C of it.

C = kb / (kb + km) (typically 0.15–0.3 for steel joints)
Bolt force Fb = Fi + C·P Member force Fm = Fi − (1 − C)·P
Separation load P₀ = Fi / (1 − C)
Load factor (proof) n = (Sp·At − Fi) / (C·P)
kb ≈ E·At / l km = 0.5774·π·E·d / (2·ln(5(0.5774l + 0.5d)/(0.5774l + 2.5d)))

Two consequences: (1) a properly preloaded bolt sees only a small alternating load, so it does not fail in fatigue; (2) the joint stays closed, so it does not leak, slip or hammer. Recommended preload: Fi ≈ 0.75 × proof load for reusable joints, 0.9 for permanent.

4Tightening torque

T = K · Fi · d K ≈ 0.20 dry steel, 0.15–0.18 oiled, 0.12 with MoS₂ paste

Torque is a poor measure of preload: friction scatter gives ±25 %. Better methods: torque-angle (turn-of-nut), direct tension indicators, hydraulic tensioners, ultrasonic bolt-length measurement — used on critical joints such as cylinder heads, flywheels and test-rig couplings.

5Bolts in shear and locking

A joint loaded sideways should carry the load by friction from the preload (μ·Fi per surface), with fitted bolts or dowels if slip is not acceptable. Do not rely on a clearance bolt in shear — the joint will slip and the bolt will bend.

Loosening is caused mostly by transverse slip. Remedies in order of effectiveness: enough preload and long, slender bolts; wedge-locking washer pairs; prevailing-torque nuts (nylon insert, all-metal); thread-locking adhesive; safety wire or tab washers for critical parts. Plain spring washers do little.

6Materials and cost

8.8 is the general-purpose grade; 10.9 and 12.9 give smaller, lighter joints but are more sensitive to hydrogen embrittlement (careful with electroplating) and to fatigue at the thread. Rolled threads (after heat treatment) are about twice as good in fatigue as cut threads. Stainless A2-70/A4-70 bolts have lower strength (Sy 450 MPa) and gall easily — use anti-seize. A standard bolt costs rupees; a special bolt costs hundreds — design around catalogue sizes and lengths.

7Worked example

M12 class 8.8, grip 40 mm, steel members, external load 15 kN per boltProof load = 580 × 84.3 = 48.9 kN; Fi = 0.75 × 48.9 = 36.7 kN; T ≈ 0.2 × 36.7 × 12 = 88 N·m kb ≈ 507 kN/mm, km ≈ 2235 kN/mm → C = 0.185 Bolt force = 36.7 + 0.185 × 15 = 39.5 kN (bolt sees only 2.8 kN of the 15 kN) Member force = 36.7 − 0.815 × 15 = 24.5 kN (joint still closed) Load factor = (48.9 − 36.7)/(0.185 × 15) = 4.4; separation at P₀ = 36.7/0.815 = 45 kN

8Procedure

  1. Find the external load per bolt, including prying and moment effects for eccentric joints.
  2. Choose size and property class; find At and proof load.
  3. Set the preload (0.75 Fp), calculate kb, km and C.
  4. Check load factor against proof, separation factor, and bolt fatigue: σa = C·P / (2At) against the thread endurance limit (≈ 130–160 MPa for rolled 8.8–12.9).
  5. Calculate tightening torque and choose a tightening method and locking device.
  6. Check thread engagement (nut height ≥ 0.8d in steel; ≥ 1.5–2d in aluminium or cast iron).

10Pro tips and common mistakes

  • A soft gasket in the joint makes km small and C large — the bolt then sees most of the load. Use metal-to-metal contact with O-rings in grooves.
  • Longer bolts (with sleeves or reduced shanks) lower kb and C — better fatigue life.
  • Never reuse torque-to-yield bolts.
  • Do not use 12.9 bolts in tapped aluminium without thread inserts.

11Practice

  1. Repeat the example with an aluminium housing. What happens to C and the separation load?
  2. A cylinder cover with 8 bolts carries 160 kN of pressure force. Choose bolt size and class for a separation factor of 2.
  3. Estimate the tightening torque for M16 10.9 bolts with oiled threads at 75 % proof load.
