Siblings Without Rivalry

Adele Faber and Elaine Mazlish · first published 1987 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Brothers and sisters fight less and grow closer when parents accept their hard feelings about each other, treat each child as a unique person instead of keeping score, and teach them to settle their own conflicts.

Summary

Adele Faber and Elaine Mazlish wrote this book after their earlier work on talking with children and the parent workshops they led. Its subject is the everyday battle between brothers and sisters, and its message is that parents can do a great deal to lower the heat at home.

The first idea is that children's angry feelings toward a sibling are real and need somewhere to go. When a parent says that a child surely loves the new baby, the resentment does not vanish; it simply goes underground. The authors suggest naming the feeling, letting a child say or draw how much they dislike a brother, while firmly stopping any hurtful action. Feelings are allowed; harming is not.

Next comes comparison. Saying one child is tidier or faster than another, even as praise, plants rivalry. Parents are urged to describe what they see in the child in front of them and leave the sibling out of it.

The authors also question the goal of treating children exactly equally. Identical portions keep children counting. Instead, each child should receive what they need, and feel loved in their own special way rather than the same as everyone else.

Another chapter warns about roles. Labels such as the clever one, the bully or the baby tend to stick, and children start living up to them. Parents can help a child see new possibilities.

When fights break out, the book offers a graded response. Ordinary squabbles can often be ignored. When tempers rise, a parent can acknowledge each child's anger, state each point of view fairly, describe the problem, express confidence that the children can work it out, and step away. Dangerous fights call for separating the children right away. For recurring problems, the family can sit down together to brainstorm solutions.

Cartoons and parents' stories show each skill in action, making the advice feel practical.

312 words

One-line takeaway: This week, when your children squabble, resist judging who is right; instead calmly describe each child's side, say you trust them to find a fair solution, and step back.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What do the authors recommend when a child expresses strong dislike of a sibling?

    Answer C. The book holds that feelings should be accepted and named, while hurtful behaviour is firmly stopped.

  2. Why do the authors warn against comparing siblings, even favourably?

    Answer A. Any comparison, positive or negative, sets children against each other.

  3. What do the authors suggest instead of treating children exactly equally?

    Answer D. Exact equality keeps children counting, so the book favours meeting each child's particular needs.

  4. What is the risk of casting a child in a role such as 'the bully' or 'the clever one'?

    Answer B. Labels tend to stick and shape how children see and behave themselves.

  5. How should parents usually respond to ordinary, minor squabbles?

    Answer C. Normal bickering can often be left alone so children practise resolving it themselves.

  6. When a fight becomes physically dangerous, what does the book advise?

    Answer A. Safety comes first, so dangerous fights call for immediate separation.

  7. When tempers rise, which step is part of the book's suggested response?

    Answer D. After acknowledging feelings and describing the problem, parents show trust in the children and step back.

  8. What tool does the book offer for recurring sibling problems?

    Answer B. Problem-solving sessions let the whole family generate and agree on fair ideas.

  9. What happens to a child's resentment when parents deny it, according to the authors?

    Answer A. Denied feelings do not vanish; they simply become hidden and may come out in other ways.

  10. How do the authors make their advice easy to follow?

    Answer C. The book uses illustrations and workshop parents' stories to show the skills in action.
