1. The problem

A corridor 1.2 m wide meets another corridor 1.2 m wide at a right-angled corner. You carry a ladder horizontally (kept flat, no tilting). What is the longest ladder that can be taken round the corner?

Workshops face this every time a long shaft, pipe or beam must be moved through a building.

2. The answer

3.39 m
for two 1.2 m corridors: (1.22/3 + 1.22/3)3/2 = 2√2 × 1.2.
Min of a max
The longest ladder that gets through equals the shortest line that touches the inner corner and both outer walls.

3. Working it out

Picture the ladder at angle θ to the first corridor, touching the inner corner. The longest straight segment that fits at that angle, from outer wall to outer wall through the inner corner, is

L(θ) = a / sin θ + b / cos θ

where a and b are the corridor widths. A ladder gets round only if it fits at every angle, so it must be no longer than the smallest L(θ). Setting dL/dθ = 0 gives tan³θ = a/b, and substituting back:

Lmax = ( a2/3 + b2/3 )3/2

For equal corridors the critical angle is 45° and Lmax = 2√2·a ≈ 2.83 a.

5. Fine print

  • Tilting the ladder upwards uses the ceiling height and lets a longer one through; with a ceiling height h the answer is roughly √(Lmax² + h²).
  • A ladder or beam has thickness. A quick, safe check is to reduce both corridor widths by the beam's width and use the calculator.
  • The harder cousin is the moving sofa problem: what is the largest area of a shape that can turn the corner? Gerver's 1992 shape (area ≈ 2.2195) was long believed best; a proof that it is optimal was announced in 2024.
