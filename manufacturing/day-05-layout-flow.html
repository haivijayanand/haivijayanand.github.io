Plant Layout and Material Flow

Where machines go decides how far material travels, how much WIP piles up and how safe the floor is. Process, product, cellular and fixed layouts; from–to charts; and the special needs of a converting plant — roll handling, solvent areas and clean zones.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: From–to layout planner❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Choose between process, product, cellular and fixed-position layouts
  • Build a from–to chart and calculate load × distance
  • Use systematic layout planning (SLP) closeness ratings
  • Plan material handling: cranes, roll trolleys, forklifts, AGVs
  • Account for safety, hygiene and future expansion in a layout

3From–to chart and load × distance

List the departments, record how many loads (or tonnes) move between each pair per day, and measure the distance between them. The total load × distance is a single number to compare layouts.

Handling effort = Σ (flow between i and j) × (distance between i and j)
Systematic Layout Planning (Muther). Rate each pair of areas on closeness: A absolutely necessary, E especially important, I important, O ordinary, U unimportant, X undesirable (e.g. a welding bay next to a solvent store). Place the A pairs first, keep X pairs apart, then fit the space.

4Layout of a flexible packaging plant

Rolls, solvents and hygieneMaterial flows as heavy rolls (300 kg to 2 t), so the layout is designed around roll handling: overhead cranes or roll trolleys, wide aisles, roll racks, and short moves between printer, laminator and slitter. The classic flow is store → extrusion → printing → lamination → curing room → slitting → pouch making → QC → packing → dispatch. Special zones: solvent and ink storage away from ignition sources and near the presses' ink kitchens (X-rating with welding and electrical rooms); curing rooms with temperature control next to lamination; food-grade areas for pouch making with controlled access, pest control and no glass. Solvent recovery or incineration plants sit outside near the gravure hall. Plan the cylinder store and engraving next to the presses. Leave space in the line for the next press.

In a machine shop the same thinking leads to cells: a lathe, a milling machine and a grinder for one family of shafts in a U, with one or two operators walking the cell.

5Worked example

Two layouts comparedFlows (rolls/day): Store→Print 40, Print→Lam 36, Lam→Slit 34, Slit→Pouch 20, Slit→Dispatch 14, Pouch→Dispatch 20. Layout 1 (machines where they fitted over the years): total load × distance = 9 860 roll·m/day Layout 2 (in flow sequence, U-shaped): 4 120 roll·m/day → 58 % less travel. At 2 min per 50 m by forklift: about 3 h of handling saved per day, fewer roll edge damages.

6How to plan a layout

  1. Fix the product families and volumes for the next 5–10 years, not just today.
  2. Draw the process flow for each family and build the from–to chart.
  3. Rate closeness (A, E, I, O, U, X) including safety, noise, hygiene and utilities.
  4. Calculate space for each area: machines, operator space, WIP, aisles, maintenance access.
  5. Make two or three block layouts; compare load × distance, safety and expansion.
  6. Choose handling methods and aisle widths; check fire exits and fire-fighting access.
  7. Walk the layout with operators and maintenance before you pour concrete.

7Lab: from–to layout planner

Seven departments sit in seven bays. Swap bays to put high-flow pairs next to each other. Line thickness shows flow; the total load × distance updates.

8Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Design the aisles first. Machines can move; columns, pits and fire exits cannot.
  • Keep WIP space small on purpose — floor space fills up with whatever it is given.
  • Do a spaghetti diagram: trace one operator's or one roll's path on the plan for an hour. The tangle tells the story.
  • Keep incompatible areas apart: solvents and hot work, food zones and maintenance workshops.

9Practice

  1. Draw your plant's flow for its biggest product family on a floor plan.
  2. Build a from–to chart for six areas and compute load × distance.
  3. Propose one move that cuts it by 20 % and list what it would cost.
