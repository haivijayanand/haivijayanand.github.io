Jobs & Unemployment

Who counts as working, who counts as unemployed, why only about a third of Indian women are in the labour force, how many jobs India must create each year, and what theory says about why unemployment exists at all. Pick your level.

Level 1 · For school students

Not everyone without a job is "unemployed". Let's see who is who.

1What is a job, and what is unemployment?

A person is unemployed if they want to work, are able to work and are looking for work, but cannot find any.

Your grandmother who has retired is not unemployed: she is not looking for work. Your elder cousin in college is not unemployed either: she is studying. But your neighbour who finished his diploma and has applied to ten companies without success is unemployed.

3Kinds of work

🌾 FarmingStill employs the largest number of Indians, about 4 in 10 workers, though it produces a much smaller share of GDP (Topic 03).
🏭 IndustryFactories, construction and mining. Construction gives work to many who leave farming.
💻 ServicesShops, transport, IT, banks, hospitals, schools and delivery. The fastest-growing area.
Ways of workingSelf-employed (a shopkeeper, farmer, tailor), salaried (a monthly pay cheque), casual (paid by the day). In India, more than half of workers are self-employed.

4Why are people unemployed?

🔍 SearchingJust finished college, or moving to a new city: it takes time to find the right job.
📅 SeasonFarm work is busy at sowing and harvest, and quiet in between.
🛠️ Skills mismatchJobs exist, but need skills (welding, coding, nursing) that job seekers don't yet have.
📉 SlowdownWhen the economy slows, companies hire less, and some people lose jobs.

5Women and work

In India, only about 1 in 3 women aged 15+ is working or looking for work, against about 3 in 4 men. Many women do hours of unpaid work at home, cooking, caring and fetching water, which doesn't count as a "job" in the statistics. Safety, transport, childcare and social norms also matter. When more women can work, families earn more and the whole country grows faster. The number has been rising slowly: 34.6% in September 2026.

6Jobs of the future

  • Computers and AI are changing many jobs: some tasks disappear, new ones appear, such as data, healthcare, green energy and repair.
  • Skills that last: reading well, maths, communication, teamwork, and learning new things quickly.
  • Hands-on skills (electricians, nurses, technicians) remain in strong demand.

Level 2 · For working people

How India measures jobs, the structure of employment, formal-job signals, the labour codes, the national jobs challenge, and what it means for your career.

1How India measures jobs

SourceWhat it showsNotes
PLFS (NSO, MoSPI)Participation, worker-population ratio and unemployment rateMonthly since 2025 (current weekly status), plus quarterly and annual reports. The official benchmark.
EPFO payrollNet new members in the Employees' Provident FundA proxy for formal jobs. It mixes in job switches and re-joiners, and changes with coverage rules (such as the new ₹25,000 wage ceiling).
ESIC, NPSOther formal-sector enrolmentsPartial coverage
Private surveys (for example, CMIE)High-frequency alternative estimatesDifferent definitions, so the numbers differ from PLFS

The reference period matters: "current weekly status" asks about the last 7 days; "usual status" looks at the past year. Weekly measures usually show higher unemployment than usual status.

The headline rate is low, but it hides the real issues: underemployment (people working a few hours, or in low-productivity farm work), low participation, especially among women, and high unemployment among educated urban youth, who can afford to wait for a good job.

3The structure of Indian employment

  • Agriculture still employs over 40% of workers, while producing under a fifth of output. Low productivity per worker is the core challenge.
  • Self-employment is the largest category, over half of workers. Regular salaried jobs are about a fifth to a quarter; the rest is casual labour.
  • Informality: most jobs lack a written contract, paid leave or social security, even within formal enterprises.
  • Gig and platform work (delivery, ride-hailing, freelancing) is growing fast. The Code on Social Security recognises gig and platform workers and enables social-security schemes funded partly by aggregators.

4Formal jobs and the new rules

  • EPFO adds roughly 18–22 lakh net members a month. Many are young first-time workers, and Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat and Haryana dominate.
  • The EPF wage ceiling rose from ₹15,000 to ₹25,000 a month from 17 September 2026. More employees now get compulsory PF, and the maximum mandatory employee contribution rises from ₹1,800 to ₹3,000 a month. Your take-home pay may dip, but your retirement savings grow (Topic 09).
  • The four labour codes (Wages; Industrial Relations; Social Security; Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions) replaced 29 older laws and are now fully operational. Key changes include a uniform definition of wages, a national floor wage, social security for gig workers, fixed-term employment, and higher thresholds for layoff approvals.

The inputs are illustrative. The point: the answer is dominated by participation. Bringing more women into work is both the biggest opportunity and the biggest jobs challenge. Moving workers out of farming adds further demand for non-farm jobs on top of this.

6What it means for your career

Real wagesA raise below inflation is a pay cut (Topic 01). Track your real wage growth over several years, not one appraisal.
Skills compoundSkills, like money, compound (Topic 09). Certifications, language and digital skills, and domain depth raise lifetime earnings.
Safety netsKeep an emergency fund for job loss (6 months of expenses). Don't withdraw PF when changing jobs; transfer it.
Gig incomeIf you freelance or do platform work, plan your own provident fund (PPF/NPS), health insurance and tax (Topic 06).

7Jobs and the country, and the world

  • The demographic dividend: India has the world's largest working-age population. The dividend pays only if there are productive jobs; otherwise it becomes a demographic burden.
  • "Jobless growth" worries: fast GDP growth (Topic 03) led by capital-intensive and IT sectors creates fewer jobs per rupee than labour-intensive manufacturing (textiles, footwear, electronics assembly) or construction.
  • Policy push: production-linked incentives, the Employment Linked Incentive scheme for first-time formal workers, skilling missions and apprenticeships.
  • Global: remittances from Indians working abroad hit records in 2026 (Topic 05). AI is reshaping white-collar work worldwide, including India's IT and back-office services.

Level 3 · Review for experts

Measurement identities, search and matching, flow dynamics, India's structural transformation, women's participation, labour regulation, and technology.

1Measurement

LFPR = LF / Pop, WPR = E / Pop, UR = U / LF = 1 − WPR/LFPR Check (Sep 2026, CWS): 1 − 52.7/55.5 ≈ 5.0%
  • Status concepts: usual principal status, plus subsidiary status (365 days), current weekly status (7 days) and current daily status (person-days). Current daily status best captures underemployment in casual and agricultural work.
  • The 2025 PLFS redesign (rotational panel, larger sample, rural monthly coverage) enables monthly CWS estimates. It creates breaks with the earlier series, so use the new series for trends after 2025.
  • Women's work: the definition of "work" (SNA production boundary versus ILO's 19th ICLS "employment" concept) changes how unpaid production for one's own use is counted. Time-use surveys (2019, 2024) are essential complements.

2Search, matching and flows

Steady state: s·(1 − u) = f·u ⇒ u* = s / (s + f) (s = separation rate, f = job-finding rate) Matching: M = A·U^α·V^(1−α); tightness θ = V/U; Beveridge curve: U and V inversely related
  • DMP (Diamond–Mortensen–Pissarides): unemployment arises from search frictions even in equilibrium. Policy works through matching efficiency A, vacancy creation and the value of non-work.
  • Natural rate, NAIRU and hysteresis: long spells erode skills and attachment, so cyclical unemployment can become structural (Blanchard–Summers).
  • In India, the "wait unemployment" of educated youth queuing for government or formal jobs reflects large wage and security premia on public-sector work, which lowers effective f.

3Structural transformation

  • The Lewis dual-economy model: surplus farm labour moves to higher-productivity modern sectors. India's movement out of agriculture has been slow and partly reversed (2020–22), with construction and low-end services absorbing the movers rather than manufacturing.
  • Premature deindustrialisation (Rodrik, 2016): manufacturing employment shares are peaking at lower levels and lower incomes than in earlier industrialisers. Services-led growth is less labour-absorbing for workers with little schooling.
  • Productivity gaps: value added per worker in agriculture is a fraction of that in industry and services. Reallocation alone could add substantially to aggregate productivity (Topic 03, growth accounting).
  • Firm size: the "missing middle", where a few large firms coexist with millions of tiny ones, limits formal job creation. Size-dependent regulations historically encouraged staying small.

4Women's labour force participation

  • Goldin's U-shape: participation falls as household incomes rise from low levels (an income effect and status norms), then rises with education and white-collar jobs. India appears to be near the bottom, with recent increases driven largely by rural self-employment.
  • Drivers: norms (purdah, the male-breadwinner ideal), safety and mobility, care burdens, a lack of suitable local jobs, and marriage and migration patterns. Evidence from Indian field experiments (on job information, transport and the framing of norms) shows that participation responds to these constraints.
  • Urban paradox: highly educated urban women have high unemployment (9.2% in September 2026). There are jobs, but not ones matching their qualifications, location and safety requirements.

5Labour regulation

  • The four codes consolidate 29 laws. Industrial Relations raises the threshold for government permission for layoffs, retrenchment and closure from 100 to 300 workers and formalises fixed-term employment. Social Security extends coverage to gig and platform workers, and Wages sets a national floor wage.
  • The flexibility vs security debate: evidence on Indian labour-law rigidity (Besley–Burgess, 2004) suggests pro-worker amendments reduced formal manufacturing output and employment. Critics question the coding of state laws and stress that enforcement, not the letter of the law, binds. Contract labour grew as a workaround.
  • Minimum wages: in monopsonistic labour markets, moderate minimum wages need not reduce employment (Card–Krueger; Manning). In India the bigger issue is compliance, given high informality.

6Technology and the future of work

  • Task-based framework (Acemoglu–Autor): technology substitutes for routine tasks and complements non-routine ones, leading to job polarisation in advanced economies.
  • Generative AI exposes cognitive, non-routine tasks: writing, coding, customer support. This is relevant to India's IT services, BPO and global-capability-centre employment. The net effect depends on new task creation and on how fast AI is adopted.
  • Platformisation reduces search frictions (raising f) but shifts risk to workers. Social-security design for gig workers is a live policy experiment.

7Review checklist: open questions

  1. Is the recent rise in women's participation a sign of distress-driven self-employment or genuine opportunity?
  2. Can services absorb India's farm-labour exit, or is labour-intensive manufacturing indispensable?
  3. Will the labour codes' flexibility provisions spur formal hiring, and how will states implement them?
  4. How should EPFO's expanded wage ceiling be weighed against lower take-home pay and higher labour costs?
  5. How large is AI's displacement risk for India's services-export employment, and what reskilling scale is needed?
  6. Which single labour-market indicator best guides monetary and fiscal policy in a largely informal economy?
