Waves, Fields and the Quantum World

The equations of mathematical physics: waves in bars, the Helmholtz equation of acoustic modes, Laplace and Poisson for potential fields and torsion, Schrödinger's quantum wave, KdV solitons, and the special functions — Bessel, Legendre, Airy — that come out of solving them, ending with the Euler–Lagrange equation that generates them all.

61Wave equation (stress waves in a bar)

PDElinearhyperbolicd'Alembert 1747 · Part I, Day 13: Waves and Laplace
∂²u/∂t² = c²·∂²u/∂x² bar: c = √(E/ρ), impact stress σ = ρ·c·v

u axial displacement · c wave speed · v particle (impact) velocity

Part I met the wave equation on a string; in a solid bar the same equation carries stress. Hit the end of a bar and a stress pulse runs along it at c = √(E/ρ) — 5 km/s in steel — carrying a stress ρcv, independent of the bar's length. At a free end it reflects as tension, which is why brittle rods can snap at the far end when hit (spalling).

A steel bar is struck end-on at 1 m/s. Stress in the pulse? Time to cross a 1 m bar?c = √(210e9/7850) = 5170 m/s σ = ρcv = 7850 × 5170 × 1 = 40.6 MPa per m/s of impact speed Transit time = 1/5170 = 0.19 ms — the basis of the split Hopkinson bar test.
Where you meet itPile driving, impact and drop testing, Hopkinson bar material testing, ultrasonic thickness gauges and flaw detection, seismic waves, rock drilling, torsional and axial shaft resonances.

62Helmholtz equation (acoustic modes)

PDElinearellipticeigenvalueHelmholtz 1860
∇²p + k²·p = 0, k = ω/c rectangular room: f = (c/2)·√((nx/Lx)² + (ny/Ly)² + (nz/Lz)²)

p pressure amplitude · k wave number · n mode numbers (0, 1, 2 …) · c speed of sound (343 m/s)

Assume a wave oscillates at one frequency, p(x, t) = p(x)·eiωt, and the wave equation becomes the Helmholtz equation for the spatial shape. In a closed space only certain frequencies fit — the room's modes. At low frequency these standing waves make a test cell or listening room boom at some notes and go dead at others, and make microphone readings depend on where you stand.

A gearbox test cell is 6 × 4 × 3 m. Its lowest acoustic modes?(1,0,0): 343/(2 × 6) = 28.6 Hz (0,1,0): 343/8 = 42.9 Hz (0,0,1): 57.2 Hz (1,1,0): (343/2)·√(1/36 + 1/16) = 51.5 Hz Below about 100 Hz the modes are sparse — noise measurements there need several microphone positions.
Where you meet itRoom and test-cell acoustics, exhaust and intake silencers, ducts and organ pipes, ultrasonics, antennas and waveguides (same equation for EM fields), vibrating membranes and plates.

63Laplace's equation (potential flow round a cylinder)

PDElinearellipticLaplace 1782 · Part I, Day 13: Waves and Laplace
∇²φ = 0, u = ∇φ flow past a cylinder: uθ = −2U·sin θ on the surface, Cp = 1 − 4·sin²θ

φ velocity potential · U free-stream speed · Cp = (p − p∞)/(½ρU²)

For irrotational, inviscid, incompressible flow the velocity is the gradient of a potential satisfying Laplace's equation — the same equation as steady heat (Part I, Day 13). Superposing simple solutions (uniform flow + doublet) gives flow round a cylinder: the speed doubles at the sides, and the pressure drops to −3 times the dynamic pressure. Real flow separates on the back, but the front half is predicted well.

Wind at 20 m/s across a chimney. Peak surface speed and suction?Peak speed = 2U = 40 m/s at θ = 90° C_p = 1 − 4 = −3 → suction = 3 × ½ × 1.225 × 20² = 735 Pa (Separated real flow: about −1.2 at the sides — Laplace overestimates the suction.)
Where you meet itIdeal-flow aerodynamics (panel methods), electrostatics and capacitance, steady heat conduction, groundwater (Darcy), stress functions in elasticity, image processing (inpainting).

64Poisson equation (torsion of non-circular shafts)

PDElinearellipticPoisson 1813; Prandtl 1903 (membrane analogy)
∂²Φ/∂x² + ∂²Φ/∂y² = −2·G·θ′, Φ = 0 on the boundary T = 2∬Φ dA, τ = |∇Φ|

Φ Prandtl stress function · θ′ twist per length · T torque · τ shear stress

Laplace's equation with a source term. For a twisted shaft of any cross-section, Prandtl showed the stress function is the shape of a soap film stretched over a hole of that shape and blown up by pressure: the torque is twice the volume under the film, the shear stress is its slope. Steepest slope = highest stress, which is why re-entrant corners (keyways, splines) concentrate stress.

100 N·m on a square shaft of side 20 mm versus a round shaft of 20 mm diameter. Peak shear stress?Square (Poisson solution, tabulated): τ_max = T/(0.208·a³) = 100/(0.208 × 8e-6) = 60.1 MPa, at the middle of each side Round: τ_max = 16T/(πd³) = 63.7 MPa The square has more material yet only 6 % lower stress — its corners carry almost nothing.
Where you meet itTorsion of keyed, splined and rectangular shafts, electrostatics with charges, gravity fields, pressure in Darcy flow with sources, heat generation (equation 34), deflection of membranes.

65Schrödinger equation

PDElinearcomplexSchrödinger 1926
i·ħ·∂ψ/∂t = −(ħ²/2m)·∂²ψ/∂x² + V(x)·ψ particle in a box: En = n²h²/(8mL²)

ψ wave function (|ψ|² = probability density) · ħ reduced Planck constant · V potential energy

The wave equation of quantum mechanics — first order in time, complex, and dispersive. Confinement makes energy levels discrete (the box), and a particle can cross a barrier higher than its energy with a probability that falls exponentially with barrier width: tunnelling, which flash memory, tunnel diodes, the scanning tunnelling microscope and nuclear fusion in the Sun all depend on.

An electron confined in a 1 nm box (a quantum dot). Ground-state energy? Wavelength of light emitted from n = 2 → 1?E₁ = h²/(8mL²) = (6.626e-34)²/(8 × 9.109e-31 × 1e-18) = 6.02×10⁻²⁰ J = 0.376 eV E₂ − E₁ = 3E₁ = 1.13 eV → λ = 1240/1.13 = 1100 nm (infrared) Halve the box → four times the energy: quantum-dot colours are tuned by size.
Where you meet itSemiconductors and transistors, LEDs and lasers, quantum dots (display colours), MRI and spectroscopy, chemistry (molecular orbitals), tunnelling devices, quantum computing.

66Korteweg–de Vries equation (solitons)

PDEnonlineardispersiveKorteweg and de Vries 1895; Zabusky and Kruskal 1965
∂u/∂t + 6·u·∂u/∂x + ∂³u/∂x³ = 0 soliton: u = (c/2)·sech²( (√c/2)·(x − c·t) )

u wave height (scaled) · c speed — taller solitons are faster and narrower

Nonlinearity steepens a wave (as in Burgers, equation 49); dispersion spreads it. In KdV they balance exactly, giving a solitary wave that keeps its shape for ever. Even more remarkable, two solitons collide and emerge unchanged except for a shift in position — they behave like particles, hence "soliton". The equation turned out to be exactly solvable (inverse scattering), a landmark of 20th-century mathematics.

A solitary wave 0.5 m high in a 2 m deep channel. Its speed compared with an ordinary small wave?Shallow-water soliton speed ≈ √(g(h + a)) = √(9.81 × 2.5) = 4.95 m/s Small linear wave: √(gh) = √(9.81 × 2) = 4.43 m/s — the hump outruns ordinary waves by 12 %.
Where you meet itSolitary waves in canals and the ocean (internal waves, tsunami fronts near shore), pulses in optical fibres (nonlinear Schrödinger cousin), plasma waves, blood-pressure pulses, crystal lattice vibrations.

67Bessel's equation (drums and round domains)

ODElinearsecond orderspecial functionBernoulli 1732; Bessel 1824
x²·y″ + x·y′ + (x² − n²)·y = 0 → y = Jn(x) drum: fmn = jmn·c/(2πa)

Jn Bessel function of order n · jmn zeros: j₀₁ = 2.405, j₁₁ = 3.832, j₂₁ = 5.136, j₀₂ = 5.520 · c = √(T/σ) membrane wave speed

Separate the wave or heat equation in polar coordinates and the radial part obeys Bessel's equation. Its solutions are like decaying cosines whose zeros are not evenly spaced — so a drum's overtones are not harmonics, which is why it has a vague pitch. Bessel functions also give FM radio sidebands, heat in cylinders and the vibration of circular plates.

A drum of radius 0.3 m, membrane tension 2000 N/m and 0.1 kg/m². First three frequencies?c = √(2000/0.1) = 141.4 m/s f₀₁ = 2.405 × 141.4/(2π × 0.3) = 180 Hz; f₁₁ = 3.832 × … = 287 Hz; f₀₂ = 5.520 × … = 414 Hz Ratios 1 : 1.59 : 2.30 — not 1 : 2 : 3.
Where you meet itDrums and loudspeaker cones, circular plates and diaphragms (pressure sensors), heat flow in pipes and fins, FM spectra, fibre-optic modes, antennas, vibration of rotating discs.

68Legendre's equation (spherical problems)

ODElinearsecond orderspecial functionLegendre 1782
(1 − x²)·y″ − 2x·y′ + n(n + 1)·y = 0 → y = Pn(x), x = cos θ
P₀ = 1, P₁ = x, P₂ = (3x² − 1)/2, P₃ = (5x³ − 3x)/2

Pn Legendre polynomials · θ polar angle on a sphere

Separate Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates and the angular part obeys Legendre's equation. Its polynomial solutions describe how any field varies over a sphere: gravity of the oblate Earth, the shape of atomic orbitals, the flow round a sphere and antenna patterns. Accurate GPS needs the P₂ term of Earth's gravity (J₂).

Earth's oblateness adds a gravity term ∝ J₂·P₂(cos θ) with J₂ = 1.083×10⁻³. Where is the effect largest, and what does it do to orbits?P₂ = 1 at the poles (θ = 0) and −½ at the equator: gravity is stronger relative to a sphere over the poles' direction. The J₂ torque makes an orbit's plane precess. Choosing altitude and inclination so the precession is one turn per year gives a sun-synchronous orbit (about 98° inclination at 700 km) — used by Earth-imaging satellites.
Where you meet itGeodesy and satellite orbits, atomic and molecular orbitals (spherical harmonics), multipole expansions in electrostatics, antenna radiation patterns, flow round spheres, 3-D sound (ambisonics).

69Airy equation (turning points)

ODElinearsecond orderspecial functionAiry 1838 (the rainbow)
y″ − x·y = 0 → y = Ai(x), Bi(x) Ai(0) = 0.3550, Ai′(0) = −0.2588, first zero at x = −2.338

Ai decays for x > 0 and oscillates for x < 0 · the switch happens at the turning point x = 0

The simplest equation that changes from oscillating to decaying. Airy derived it to explain the extra faint bands inside a rainbow (supernumerary bows) where geometrical optics fails. It appears wherever a wave meets a "turning point": light near a caustic, a quantum particle in a uniform field, a ship's wake, radio waves bending in the ionosphere.

A neutron bouncing on a mirror in gravity (a real experiment, 2002): its energy levels follow from the zeros of Ai.E_n = a_n·(m g² ħ²/2)^(1/3) with a₁ = 2.338, a₂ = 4.088, … For a neutron (m = 1.675e-27 kg): (mg²ħ²/2)^(1/3) = 0.602 peV → E₁ = 1.41 peV, height scale ≈ 13.7 µm The measured quantum heights matched these Airy-zero predictions.
Where you meet itOptics near caustics and rainbows, quantum particles in uniform fields (semiconductor heterostructures), WKB matching in wave problems, ocean waves near beaches, beam propagation (Airy beams).

70Euler–Lagrange equation

ODEnonlinearvariationalEuler 1744; Lagrange 1755
d/dt(∂L/∂q̇) − ∂L/∂q = 0, L = T − V double pendulum: two coupled nonlinear ODEs

L Lagrangian (kinetic minus potential energy) · q generalised coordinates (angles, lengths…)

Nature picks the path that makes the action ∫L dt stationary. Writing down energies is easier than drawing free-body diagrams with constraint forces, so Lagrange's method is the standard way to get equations of motion for linkages, robots and multi-body machines. It also solves geometric optimisation problems: the brachistochrone (fastest slide), the catenary (hanging chain), geodesics.

Deriving the pendulum in two lines

L = ½mℓ²θ̇² + mgℓ cos θ. Then ∂L/∂θ̇ = mℓ²θ̇ and ∂L/∂θ = −mgℓ sin θ, so mℓ²θ̈ + mgℓ sin θ = 0 — equation 16, with no forces drawn. For the double pendulum, L includes the coupled kinetic energy ½m₂(ℓ₁²θ̇₁² + ℓ₂²θ̇₂² + 2ℓ₁ℓ₂θ̇₁θ̇₂cos(θ₁ − θ₂)), giving the chaotic system in the lab.

Brachistochrone: a bead slides from rest down 2 m while moving π m sideways. Cycloid versus straight wire?The optimal curve is a cycloid with a = 1 m: time = π√(a/g) = 1.003 s Straight wire: length √(π² + 4) = 3.72 m, acceleration g × 2/3.72 = 5.27 m/s² → time √(2 × 3.72/5.27) = 1.19 s The curve that dips steeply first wins by 16 %.
Where you meet itEquations of motion for robots, vehicles and mechanisms, optimal control and trajectory design, cable and chain shapes, light paths (Fermat), general relativity, finite-element formulations (energy methods).
