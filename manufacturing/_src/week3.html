    What quality means

    Quality is meeting the customer's requirements every time — the stated ones on the specification and the unstated ones (a pouch must not smell of solvent, a gear must not whine). It is built in by the process, not inspected in at the end. Deming's message to managers: about 85 % of quality problems come from the system — methods, machines, materials, training — which only management can change.

    PlanSet objectives and processes; understand customer requirements and risks.
    DoRun the processes as planned, with trained people and controlled documents.
    CheckMeasure, audit, analyse data — are we meeting the requirement?
    ActCorrect, prevent recurrence, improve, update the standard.

    Cost of quality

    Cost of quality = prevention + appraisal + internal failure + external failure
    CategoryExamples
    PreventionTraining, FMEA, poka-yoke, supplier development, preventive maintenance, process design
    AppraisalIncoming inspection, in-process checks, lab tests, calibration, final audit
    Internal failureScrap, rework, re-inspection, downgrading, machine time lost on bad product
    External failureCustomer rejections, returns, claims, free replacement, line-stoppage charges at the customer, lost business
    The 1–10–100 rule. A defect caught at the source costs 1, caught at final inspection costs 10, caught by the customer costs 100 — and the last one may cost you the account. Shift spending left: prevention is the cheapest quality.

    Quality in flexible packaging

    What customers check and what goes wrongCritical characteristics: thickness and GSM, colour (ΔE against the approved standard), print register, bond strength between layers, seal strength, coefficient of friction (for the customer's filling machine), barrier (OTR, WVTR), residual solvent (food safety), odour, dimensions of pouches, zipper function, pin-holes, and foreign matter. Big external failures: a laminate that delaminates on the customer's line, a pouch that leaks after filling, wrong artwork or barcode, or solvent odour in food. Each can cost many times the value of the material — the customer's product, filling time and recall exposure ride on it.

    Worked example

    Cost of quality in one year (₹ lakh)Prevention 18 (training 4, FMEA/projects 6, PM 8) Appraisal 42 (lab, inspectors, retained samples) Internal failure 165 (scrap 5.5 % of ₹30 crore material) External failure 60 (claims, returns, free replacement) Total 285 lakh = 4.0 % of ₹72 crore sales — 79 % is failure cost. Doubling prevention to 36 and halving failure gives total ≈ 190 lakh: about ₹95 lakh saved.

    Building the system

    1. Write down the customer requirements (specifications, contracts, regulations) for each product family.
    2. Map the processes and their owners; define the checks at each step (control plan, Day 18).
    3. Control documents and records — the latest specification at the machine, retained samples, traceability by roll and batch.
    4. Train and qualify operators and inspectors; calibrate gauges (Day 16).
    5. Measure cost of quality monthly by category.
    6. Handle every complaint with a structured method (Day 17) and audit the system yearly.

    Lab: cost of quality model

    Move the prevention spend. Appraisal and failure costs respond (the curves are a simple model, not a law). Find where total cost is lowest.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • A certificate is not quality. The system must be used daily, not prepared for the audit.
    • Count scrap in rupees, not only in percent, and show it to everyone monthly.
    • Traceability pays for itself the first time a complaint can be limited to two rolls instead of a month's production.
    • Managers: never ask "who did it?" first. Ask "what in the system allowed it?"

    Practice

    2. List the top five critical characteristics of your main product and how each is checked.
    3. Pick one complaint from last year and calculate what it really cost.

    Two kinds of variation

    Common causeMany small, always-present sources: roll-to-roll film variation, ambient humidity, normal machine play. The process is "in control" — predictable. Only changing the system reduces it.
    Special (assignable) causeSomething specific and new: a worn doctor blade, a new resin lot, a heater that failed, a new operator. It shows up as an unusual point or pattern. Find it and remove it.
    Two mistakes. Reacting to common-cause noise as if it were special (tampering — adjusting the machine after every reading) increases variation. Ignoring a special cause lets defects pile up. A control chart tells you which is which.

    X̄–R chart

    Take small subgroups (n = 4 or 5 consecutive pieces) at regular intervals. Plot the subgroup mean X̄ and range R.

    Centre lines: X̿ = mean of X̄, R̄ = mean of R X̄ chart limits: X̿ ± A₂·R̄ R chart limits: D₃·R̄ and D₄·R̄ n = 4: A₂ 0.729, D₃ 0, D₄ 2.282 n = 5: A₂ 0.577, D₃ 0, D₄ 2.114
    Signal (one of several rule sets)Meaning
    1 point beyond 3σ limitsSudden change
    2 of 3 points beyond 2σ on the same sideShift starting
    8 points in a row on one side of the centreMean has shifted
    6 points in a row steadily rising or fallingTrend — wear, drift, temperature
    DataChart
    Measured, in subgroupsX̄–R (or X̄–s for n > 10)
    Measured, one at a time (batch results, lab tests)I–MR (individuals and moving range)

    SPC in a converting plant

    What to chartExtrusion: film thickness across the web and gauge profile (I–MR or X̄–R from the gauge scanner), dart impact, COF. Printing: colour ΔE for key brand colours per roll (I–MR), viscosity of inks per hour, register error. Lamination: adhesive coat weight (g/m²), bond strength after cure. Pouch making: seal strength per hour (X̄–R, n = 5 pouches), pouch dimensions, leak-test results (p chart). Charts must be at the machine and plotted by the operator, with a written reaction plan — what to do when a rule is broken.

    Worked example

    Seal strength, subgroups of 5 pouches20 subgroups: X̿ = 42.0 N/15 mm, R̄ = 4.6 N X̄ limits: 42.0 ± 0.577 × 4.6 = 39.35 to 44.65 R limits: 0 to 2.114 × 4.6 = 9.72 Specification: minimum 30 N/15 mm. The process is far above spec — but subgroup 21 at X̄ = 39.0 is below the LCL: something changed (jaw temperature, film lot). Stop, find the cause, record it.

    Introducing SPC

    1. Pick one critical characteristic with a known problem — not twenty at once.
    2. Check the measurement system first (Day 16); a noisy gauge makes a noisy chart.
    3. Collect 20–25 subgroups under normal running; calculate the limits.
    4. Remove points with known special causes and recalculate.
    5. Put the chart at the machine with a reaction plan; train the operators.
    6. Review signals and actions weekly; recalculate limits only when the process has changed for good.

    Lab: X̄–R chart simulator

    A process makes subgroups of five. Press P to run it; inject a mean shift, a trend or extra spread and see how fast the chart notices. The first 20 subgroups set the limits.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Never put specification limits on an X̄ chart. Spec limits apply to individual pieces; control limits describe subgroup means.
    • "In control" does not mean "good" — a stable process can still make scrap. That is capability (Day 15).
    • A chart nobody reacts to is wallpaper. The reaction plan matters more than the chart.
    • Managers: don't ask for a reason for every bad day if the process is in control — you are asking people to explain noise.

Cp, Cpk, Pp, Ppk and parts per million out of spec — how to run a capability study, read the histogram and decide whether to centre the process or reduce its spread.
  • Calculate Cp and Cpk from the mean, standard deviation and specification limits
  • Relate Cpk to expected PPM defective
  • Distinguish short-term (Cp, Cpk) from long-term (Pp, Ppk) capability
  • Decide whether to centre the process or cut its variation
  • Know typical customer targets: Cpk ≥ 1.33, 1.67 for critical characteristics

    Capability indices

    Cp = (USL − LSL) ÷ 6σ — potential, if perfectly centred Cpk = min(USL − μ, μ − LSL) ÷ 3σ — actual, allows for off-centre One-sided spec (e.g. minimum seal strength): Cpk = (μ − LSL) ÷ 3σ Pp, Ppk: the same formulas with overall (long-term) σ instead of within-subgroup σ (R̄/d₂)
    CpkOut of spec (one side, normal)Verdict
    0.67≈ 23 000 ppmNot capable — 100 % inspection needed
    1.00≈ 1 350 ppmBarely capable
    1.33≈ 32 ppmCapable — usual minimum
    1.67≈ 0.3 ppmRequired for critical / safety characteristics
    2.00≈ 0.001 ppm"Six sigma" short-term
    Prerequisite: stability. Capability means nothing for a process that is out of control (Day 14). Get it stable first; then measure capability.

    Centre or tighten?

    Cp high, Cpk lowThe spread fits but the process is off-centre. Cheap fix: adjust the aim — set-point, die gap, recipe.
    Cp lowThe spread itself is too wide. Needs real work: better material, machine condition, control loops, method — or a wider specification agreed with the customer.

    Capability in converting

    Thickness, coat weight and the cost of give-awayBlown film sold by the kg but used by the m² creates a trade-off: running thicker than nominal is "give-away" — free material to the customer. With a film specified at 50 ± 5 µm and σ = 2.5 µm (Cp 0.67), the plant must aim high to keep thin spots out, giving away several percent. Cutting σ to 1.2 µm (Cp 1.39) with automatic die-gap control lets the aim move down towards nominal — on a 5 000 t/year line, 1 µm of average thickness is about 2 % ≈ 100 t of resin a year. The same logic applies to adhesive coat weight in lamination and ink laydown in printing.

    Worked example

    Pouch widthSpec 150 ± 1.0 mm. Sample of 125 pouches: mean 150.35 mm, σ (within) 0.22 mm Cp = 2.0 / (6 × 0.22) = 1.52 Cpk = min(150.35 − 149.0, 151.0 − 150.35) / (3 × 0.22) = 0.65 / 0.66 = 0.98 Capable in spread but off-centre: move the cut-off aim by −0.35 mm → Cpk = Cp = 1.52.

    Running a capability study

    1. Confirm the gauge is good (Day 16) and the process is in control (Day 14).
    2. Collect at least 100 readings (25 subgroups of 4–5) over normal variation: shifts, operators, material lots.
    3. Plot a histogram and check it is roughly normal; if not, find out why (mixed streams, skew, truncation by sorting).
    4. Calculate Cp, Cpk (within σ) and Pp, Ppk (overall σ).
    5. Compare with the customer's target; decide centre, tighten or renegotiate the spec.
    6. Repeat after any process change.

    Lab: capability study

    Set the specification, the process mean and standard deviation. The histogram of 500 simulated readings and the normal curve sit between the spec limits; Cp, Cpk and PPM update. Try centring versus tightening.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Cpk from 30 readings is a guess. With small samples the confidence interval is wide.
    • A histogram cut off sharply at a spec limit means somebody is sorting — the process is worse than it looks.
    • Do not quote Cpk for a characteristic you cannot measure well; the gauge spread is in your σ.
    • Managers: ask what the give-away costs. Capability is money, not just a number for the auditor.

    Practice

    1. Calculate Cp and Cpk for one critical characteristic from last month's data.
    2. Decide: centre or tighten? What would each cost?
    3. Estimate the material give-away in kg per year from thickness or coat-weight capability.

    Measurement system analysis

    PropertyQuestion
    ResolutionDoes the gauge read finely enough? Rule of thumb: 1/10 of the tolerance.
    BiasIs the average reading equal to the true value (reference standard)?
    LinearityIs the bias the same across the range?
    StabilityDoes it read the same over weeks?
    Repeatability (EV)Same operator, same part, same gauge — how much do readings vary?
    Reproducibility (AV)Different operators on the same parts — how much do their averages differ?
    GRR = √(EV² + AV²) %GRR (of tolerance) = 6·GRR ÷ (USL − LSL) × 100 < 10 % good · 10–30 % acceptable with care · > 30 % not acceptable Number of distinct categories ndc = 1.41 × (part variation ÷ GRR) — should be ≥ 5

    Calibration against standards traceable to national standards (in India via NABL-accredited laboratories to NPL) handles bias; gauge R&R handles spread.

    Acceptance sampling

    When 100 % inspection is impossible (destructive tests) or uneconomic, check a sample: inspect n items from the lot; accept if the number of defectives is ≤ c. Standards: ISO 2859-1 / IS 2500 (attributes), ISO 3951 (variables).

    P(accept) = Σ (k = 0 … c) C(n,k)·pᵏ·(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ — plotted against lot fraction defective p = the OC curve
    AQLAcceptable quality level: a good lot quality the plan should accept most of the time.
    Producer's risk αChance of rejecting a lot at AQL (usually ≈ 5 %).
    LTPD / LQBad lot quality the plan should usually reject.
    Consumer's risk βChance of accepting a lot at LTPD (usually ≈ 10 %).
    Sampling does not add quality. It only sorts lots, and with risk. An AQL of 1 % says you agree to accept lots with about 1 % defective. For critical defects (wrong artwork, foreign matter in food packaging) the acceptance number should be zero — and the process must prevent them.

    Measurement and sampling in converting

    Tests that need a good gaugeThickness by micrometer or online gauge; GSM by cut-and-weigh; bond strength and seal strength on a tensile tester (destructive — always sampled); COF; colour on a spectrophotometer (ΔE — inter-instrument agreement matters when you and the customer use different instruments); residual solvent by gas chromatograph. Run a gauge R&R on the tensile tester with three operators: specimen cutting and clamping often dominate the variation. Customer incoming inspection of pouches usually runs on ISO 2859 with general level II and AQLs of 0.65–2.5 % by defect class.

    Worked example

    OC curve of n = 80, c = 2p = 1 %: P(accept) = 0.953 → producer's risk ≈ 5 % p = 3 %: P(accept) = 0.568 p = 6.5 %: P(accept) = 0.101 → LTPD ≈ 6.5 % at β ≈ 10 % So a lot that is 3 % defective is accepted more often than not — sampling is a weak filter.

    Setting up measurement and inspection

    1. List every gauge and test; give each an ID, range, resolution and calibration interval.
    2. Calibrate against traceable standards; keep certificates and labels on the gauge.
    3. Run gauge R&R (10 parts × 3 operators × 2–3 trials) on gauges for critical characteristics.
    4. Write test methods precisely: specimen size, conditioning, speed, clamp.
    5. Choose sampling plans by defect class (critical / major / minor) and agree them with customers.
    6. Move from inspection to prevention: as capability rises (Day 15), sample less.

    Lab: OC curve of a sampling plan

    Set the sample size n and acceptance number c. The OC curve shows the chance of accepting a lot against its true defective rate. Mark your AQL and LTPD to read the two risks.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Measure the measurer first. Many "process problems" are gauge problems.
    • Bigger samples sharpen the OC curve; the lot size matters much less than people think.
    • Zero-acceptance plans (c = 0) are strict on paper but reject good lots often unless the process is very good.
    • Managers: an inspector who passes everything and one who fails everything both cost you money. Check agreement between inspectors (attribute agreement analysis).

    Define the problem

    A good problem statement states the gap in facts: "Bond strength of PET/Al/PE laminate on laminator 2 fell below 2.5 N/15 mm on 7 of 40 rolls (17.5 %) in the week of 3 March, against 2 % normally." Not: "lamination quality is bad". Use is / is not: where and when does it occur, and — just as useful — where and when does it not?

    The basic tools

    ToolUse
    Check sheetCollect defect data by type, time, machine
    Pareto chartRank causes; typically ~20 % of types make ~80 % of the loss
    Fishbone (Ishikawa)Brainstorm possible causes under 6M: Man, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement, Mother nature (environment)
    5 WhyAsk "why?" repeatedly, each answer based on facts, until you reach a cause you can act on in the system
    Scatter diagramTest a suspected relationship (e.g. humidity vs bond strength)
    Histogram, control chartDays 14–15
    StratificationSplit the data by machine, shift, lot, operator — the problem often lives in one stratum

    8D: the customer's format

    1. D1 Team — people with process knowledge and authority.
    2. D2 Describe the problem in numbers, with is / is not.
    3. D3 Contain — protect the customer now: hold, sort, replace stock in transit and at the customer.
    4. D4 Root cause — of the occurrence and of why it escaped detection; verified by turning it on and off.
    5. D5 Choose permanent corrective actions and prove they work.
    6. D6 Implement and remove containment once the data confirm the fix.
    7. D7 Prevent recurrence — update FMEA, control plan, standards, training; spread to similar machines and products.
    8. D8 Recognise the team.

    A converting example

    Delamination complaint, traced to its rootPareto of last quarter's laminate rejects: low bond 46 %, wrinkles 18 %, coat-weight variation 12 %, others. Fishbone on low bond: Material — PET corona treatment level decayed (old stock), PE slip additive migrated; Method — adhesive mix ratio, cure time cut short to meet dispatch; Machine — nip temperature, coat-roll wear; Environment — high humidity in monsoon; Measurement — bond tested before full cure. Data stratified by film lot showed low bond only on PET older than 4 months with surface energy below 40 dyn/cm. 5 Why: low bond → poor wetting → low surface energy → corona treatment decayed → old stock used first-in-last-out → no FIFO and no dyne check at issue. Permanent action: FIFO in the film store, dyne test at issue, re-treatment for old stock; FMEA and control plan updated.

    Worked example

    5 Why on a machine stopWhy did the pouch machine stop? → The main drive tripped on overload. Why overload? → The seal-bar bearing was seized. Why seized? → It was not lubricated.     • Pareto by cost, not just count. Ten cheap defects may matter less than one expensive one.
    • "Operator error" is never a root cause. Ask why the system allowed the error.
    • Verify root cause by making the problem come and go. A brainstormed cause is a hypothesis.
    • Close the 8D only when the data after the fix show it worked for weeks, not days.

    Process FMEA

    A failure mode and effects analysis asks, for each process step: how could it fail (failure mode)? what would the customer see (effect)? why would it happen (cause)? how do we prevent it and how would we detect it (controls)? It is done by a team before production starts and updated whenever something changes or fails.

    Rating110
    Severity (S)No effect noticedSafety or legal non-compliance without warning
    Occurrence (O)Failure prevented by designVery frequent (≥ 1 in 10)
    Detection (D)Certain to be detected (error-proofed)No detection possible
    RPN = S × O × D (1 to 1 000) Action priority (AIAG–VDA 2019): High / Medium / Low from a table that gives Severity the most weight, then Occurrence, then Detection.
    Why action priority replaced RPN thresholds. RPN 9 × 2 × 5 = 90 (a safety issue) and RPN 3 × 6 × 5 = 90 (a cosmetic one) look equal. Any severity of 9–10 with more than very low occurrence must be acted on, whatever the RPN.

    Control plan and poka-yoke

    The control plan lists, for each process step, the characteristics to control, the specification, the method, the sample size and frequency, who checks and the reaction plan. It is the FMEA's controls written as shop-floor instructions.

    Poka-yoke typeHow it worksExample
    ContactShape or sensor makes wrong orientation impossibleAsymmetric cylinder key; core-chuck sensor
    Fixed valueCounts or weighsPouch counter that stops the stacker at 100
    Motion stepSteps must happen in orderLaminator interlock: no start until mix ratio confirmed
    Warning vs controlWarning alerts; control stops the processPrefer control for high severity

Wrong adhesive or mix ratio (S 9 — delamination, migration): metering-mixing machine with ratio alarm, batch record. Solvent retention above limit (S 9 — food safety): oven settings by recipe, GC test per job. Mixed rolls of different jobs (S 8): roll labels with barcode, scan at each stage. Seal weakness (S 7): temperature monitoring, seal test per hour. Foreign matter (S 9): glass and blade control, metal detection where required, pest control.

    How to run a PFMEA

    1. Draw the process flow diagram, step by step, including storage, transport and rework loops.
    2. For each step list the functions and requirements (what must the step achieve?).
    3. List failure modes, effects (on the next process, the customer, the end user) and causes.
    4. Record the current prevention and detection controls; rate S, O, D.
    5. Prioritise (AP High first); assign actions with owners and dates.
    6. Re-rate after actions; transfer controls to the control plan and work instructions.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Severity only falls by design change — controls and inspection change O and D, not S.
    • An FMEA filed in a cupboard is worthless; it is a living document reviewed after every complaint.
    • Prefer prevention controls (lower O) to detection controls (lower D).
    • Managers: fund the poka-yoke. It costs less than one complaint.

    Practice

    1. Draw the process flow for one product and write a PFMEA for three steps.
    2. Find the highest action-priority item and design a poka-yoke for it.
    3. Check that your control plan matches the FMEA controls.
