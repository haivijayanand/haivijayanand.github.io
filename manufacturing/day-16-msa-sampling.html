Measurement Systems and Acceptance Sampling

Can you trust your numbers, and how many do you need to check? Measurement system analysis — bias, linearity, repeatability and reproducibility (gauge R&R) — and acceptance sampling plans with their OC curves, AQL and producer's and consumer's risk.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: OC curve of a sampling plan❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Explain resolution, bias, linearity, stability, repeatability and reproducibility
  • Interpret a gauge R&R result (% of tolerance, number of distinct categories)
  • Know calibration and traceability requirements
  • Read a sampling plan (n, c) and its OC curve
  • Explain AQL, LTPD, producer's risk α and consumer's risk β

2Measurement system analysis

PropertyQuestion
ResolutionDoes the gauge read finely enough? Rule of thumb: 1/10 of the tolerance.
BiasIs the average reading equal to the true value (reference standard)?
LinearityIs the bias the same across the range?
StabilityDoes it read the same over weeks?
Repeatability (EV)Same operator, same part, same gauge — how much do readings vary?
Reproducibility (AV)Different operators on the same parts — how much do their averages differ?
GRR = √(EV² + AV²) %GRR (of tolerance) = 6·GRR ÷ (USL − LSL) × 100 < 10 % good · 10–30 % acceptable with care · > 30 % not acceptable Number of distinct categories ndc = 1.41 × (part variation ÷ GRR) — should be ≥ 5

Calibration against standards traceable to national standards (in India via NABL-accredited laboratories to NPL) handles bias; gauge R&R handles spread.

3Acceptance sampling

When 100 % inspection is impossible (destructive tests) or uneconomic, check a sample: inspect n items from the lot; accept if the number of defectives is ≤ c. Standards: ISO 2859-1 / IS 2500 (attributes), ISO 3951 (variables).

P(accept) = Σ (k = 0 … c) C(n,k)·pᵏ·(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ — plotted against lot fraction defective p = the OC curve
AQLAcceptable quality level: a good lot quality the plan should accept most of the time.
Producer's risk αChance of rejecting a lot at AQL (usually ≈ 5 %).
LTPD / LQBad lot quality the plan should usually reject.
Consumer's risk βChance of accepting a lot at LTPD (usually ≈ 10 %).
Sampling does not add quality. It only sorts lots, and with risk. An AQL of 1 % says you agree to accept lots with about 1 % defective. For critical defects (wrong artwork, foreign matter in food packaging) the acceptance number should be zero — and the process must prevent them.

4Measurement and sampling in converting

Tests that need a good gaugeThickness by micrometer or online gauge; GSM by cut-and-weigh; bond strength and seal strength on a tensile tester (destructive — always sampled); COF; colour on a spectrophotometer (ΔE — inter-instrument agreement matters when you and the customer use different instruments); residual solvent by gas chromatograph. Run a gauge R&R on the tensile tester with three operators: specimen cutting and clamping often dominate the variation. Customer incoming inspection of pouches usually runs on ISO 2859 with general level II and AQLs of 0.65–2.5 % by defect class.

5Worked example

OC curve of n = 80, c = 2p = 1 %: P(accept) = 0.953 → producer's risk ≈ 5 % p = 3 %: P(accept) = 0.568 p = 6.5 %: P(accept) = 0.101 → LTPD ≈ 6.5 % at β ≈ 10 % So a lot that is 3 % defective is accepted more often than not — sampling is a weak filter.

6Setting up measurement and inspection

  1. List every gauge and test; give each an ID, range, resolution and calibration interval.
  2. Calibrate against traceable standards; keep certificates and labels on the gauge.
  3. Run gauge R&R (10 parts × 3 operators × 2–3 trials) on gauges for critical characteristics.
  4. Write test methods precisely: specimen size, conditioning, speed, clamp.
  5. Choose sampling plans by defect class (critical / major / minor) and agree them with customers.
  6. Move from inspection to prevention: as capability rises (Day 15), sample less.

7Lab: OC curve of a sampling plan

Set the sample size n and acceptance number c. The OC curve shows the chance of accepting a lot against its true defective rate. Mark your AQL and LTPD to read the two risks.

8Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Measure the measurer first. Many "process problems" are gauge problems.
  • Bigger samples sharpen the OC curve; the lot size matters much less than people think.
  • Zero-acceptance plans (c = 0) are strict on paper but reject good lots often unless the process is very good.
  • Managers: an inspector who passes everything and one who fails everything both cost you money. Check agreement between inspectors (attribute agreement analysis).

9Practice

  1. Run a quick gauge R&R on one test in your lab.
  2. Draw the OC curve of the sampling plan your biggest customer uses on you.
  3. List your critical defects and confirm the plan for each is c = 0 — and that the process prevents them.
