    What a machine designer does

    A machine converts energy into useful work through parts that move relative to each other. Machine design is deciding the shape, size, material, manufacturing method and tolerances of those parts so the machine does its job safely, for its whole life, at the lowest total cost. The parts that keep appearing in every machine — shafts, keys, bolts, springs, gears, bearings, belts — are called machine elements, and they are what this course is about.

    1 · NeedSomebody wants something done: lift 2 t, drive a pump at 1450 rpm, test a gearbox for 1000 hours.
    2 · SpecificationTurn the need into numbers: loads, speeds, life, space, environment, cost target, standards to follow.
    3 · ConceptsSketch several ways of doing it. Belt or gears? Welded or cast? Compare against the specification.
    4 · AnalysisForces on each part, stresses, deflection, life. This is the arithmetic of the next 29 days.
    5 · Detail designMaterials, sizes from standards, tolerances, surface finish, heat treatment, drawings, bill of materials.
    6 · Prototype and testBuild, measure, find what you missed, change, and release the design for production.

    The process is a loop, not a line. Analysis shows a shaft is too thin; you make it thicker; now the bearing does not fit; you change the housing. Good designers expect this and keep early decisions cheap to change.

    Design criteria

    Every element is checked against several ways it can fail. A part can be strong enough and still be a bad design because it deflects too much, wears out in a month or costs three times what it should.

    CriterionQuestionTypical check
    StrengthWill it break or yield?Stress ≤ allowable (Days 4–6)
    FatigueWill it crack after millions of cycles?Goodman, endurance limit (Days 7–8)
    RigidityWill it bend or twist too much?Deflection, slope, angle of twist (Day 14)
    VibrationIs it near a natural frequency?Critical speed (Day 14)
    WearWill surfaces wear or pit?Contact stress, pv value, film thickness (Days 22, 26)
    Life / reliabilityHow long, and how sure?Bearing L10, Weibull (Days 25, 29)
    ThermalWill it overheat?Heat generated vs dissipated (Days 18, 24, 26)
    Manufacture and costCan we make it cheaply?Process, tolerances, batch size (Day 3)
    Safety, ergonomics, maintenanceCan people use and service it safely?Guards, access, standards

    Factor of safety

    n = strength of the part ÷ stress caused by the load or n = load that causes failure ÷ working load

    The factor of safety covers what you do not know: variation in material strength, loads you did not expect, approximations in the analysis, and the consequence of failure. It is not a fixed number.

    SituationTypical n
    Well-known material, loads and analysis; test-backed (aircraft, with strict QA)1.25–1.5
    Ordinary machinery, ductile steel, static loads1.5–2.5 on yield
    Brittle materials (cast iron) — use ultimate strength3–4
    Shock loads, uncertain loads, or failure endangers people (lifts, cranes, pressure vessels)4–6, or what the code demands
    Codes win. Where a code (IS 807 cranes, IS 2825 / ASME VIII pressure vessels, IS 800 steel structures) fixes allowable stresses, use them — not your own factor of safety.

    Standards and preferred numbers

    Standards make parts interchangeable, cut inventory and carry decades of tested experience. In India, the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) publishes IS standards, most of them aligned with ISO. Examples you will meet: IS 1570 (steel designation), IS 919 / ISO 286 (limits and fits), IS 4218 / ISO 261 (metric threads), IS 2292 (keys), IS 4460 (spur gears), IS 3824 (rolling bearing life). A design data book (PSG, or Bhandari's tables) collects the numbers you need daily.

    Preferred numbers (IS 1076, ISO 3). Sizes, speeds and capacities are chosen from geometric series: each term is the previous one multiplied by a fixed ratio. R5 uses 101/5 ≈ 1.6 (1, 1.6, 2.5, 4, 6.3, 10); R10 uses 101/10 ≈ 1.25; R20 ≈ 1.12; R40 ≈ 1.06. A range of motors, pumps or gearboxes built on R10 covers the market evenly with the fewest models.

    Why geometric? Because a 10 kW step matters between 10 and 20 kW but not between 400 and 410 kW. Equal ratio steps give equal relative coverage. Standard module series for gears, standard motor ratings and standard shaft sizes all come from these series.

    Materials and cost at the concept stage

    Most of a product's cost is fixed by decisions made in the first 10 % of the design time — material, process, number of parts, tolerances. Changing them later is expensive. Keep two rough numbers in mind from Day 1: material cost per kg and machine-hour rate. Day 2 and Day 3 turn these into a method.

    Worked example

    Range of hoist capacitiesA company wants a range of chain hoists from 0.5 t to 5 t with as few models as possible, each step no more than 60 % larger than the one before. R5 ratio = 1.585 → 0.5, 0.8, 1.25, 2, 3.15, 5 t (six models) R10 would give 11 models — more cost in tooling and stock for little gain. Choose R5. If customers ask for 1 t, quote the 1.25 t model.

    Design procedure for any element

    1. Write down what the element must do: loads (steady, varying, shock), speeds, life, space and environment.
    2. Draw a free-body diagram. Find every force and moment acting on it.
    3. Choose a material and a manufacturing process (Days 2–3).
    4. Decide the likely mode of failure and the design criterion: yield, fracture, fatigue, deflection, wear.
    5. Choose the factor of safety or take the allowable stress from the governing code.
    6. Calculate the size. Round up to the next standard or preferred size.
    7. Check the other criteria (rigidity, critical speed, heat, life) with the rounded size.
    8. Add tolerances, fits, surface finish and heat treatment. Draw it and list it in the BOM.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Calculate, then standardise. A calculated shaft of 37.4 mm becomes 38 or 40 mm — never 37.4.
    • Use the coarsest series that works: R5 before R10. Fewer sizes means fewer tools, gauges and spare parts.
    • State the basis of every factor of safety on the calculation sheet: on yield or ultimate, static or fatigue.
    • Keep a design calculation file: inputs, assumptions, formulas, source of every data value. Someone will ask in five years.
    • Buy, don't make, wherever a standard part exists — bolts, bearings, seals, couplings, gear motors.

    Which family when

    Plain carbon steelCheapest strength. Easy to machine and weld (low carbon). Medium carbon (C45) for shafts. Rusts.
    Alloy steelHardenable in thick sections; tough after quench + temper. For gears and heavily loaded shafts.
    Cast ironCheap complex shapes, damps vibration, good in compression and sliding. Brittle in tension (grey); SG iron is ductile. Machine beds, housings, pulleys.
    AluminiumOne third the density, one third the stiffness of steel. Castings, housings, fins, aircraft.
    Copper alloysBronze for worm wheels and bushes (conforms, does not seize); brass for fittings.
    PolymersNylon, acetal for quiet, unlubricated gears and bushes at light load. Creep and temperature limit them.

    Choosing a material: performance indices

    Michael Ashby's method: write the quantity to minimise (mass, cost), use the design constraint to eliminate the free size, and what remains is a material index. Maximise the index.

    Light, strong tie (tension rod): maximise σ/ρ
    Light, strong beam (bending): maximise σ2/3/ρ
    Light, strong plate: maximise σ1/2/ρ
    Light, stiff beam: maximise E1/2/ρ
    Cheapest: replace ρ by ρ·Cm (cost per kg)

    On a log–log chart of σ against ρ each index is a straight line of fixed slope. All materials on one line perform equally; those above-left are better.

    Cost of materials

    Rough Indian bar-stock prices (2026, ₹/kg): mild steel 60–75, C45 80–90, alloy steels 110–250, stainless 304 ~250, aluminium 6061 ~320, bronze 800–1000, titanium 3000+. But cost per kg is misleading: what matters is cost per unit of function. A 7075 aluminium beam costs 7× per kg, but you need much less of it — the index with ρ·Cm tells you whether it pays.

    Availability is a cost. A perfect steel that needs a 3-month mill order or a minimum of 5 tonnes is the wrong steel for a prototype. Check what local stockists carry.

    Worked example

    Light cantilever arm: steel or aluminium?Beam in bending, strength-limited, minimise mass → index σ^(2/3)/ρ C45 normalised: 380^(2/3)/7850 = 52.5/7850 = 0.00669 40Cr4 H+T: 650^(2/3)/7850 = 75.0/7850 = 0.00956 Al 6061-T6: 275^(2/3)/2700 = 42.3/2700 = 0.01566 Al 6061 is 1.64× better than 40Cr4 on mass. Per rupee (divide by price): 40Cr4 0.0000797, Al 0.0000489 → 40Cr4 is 1.6× cheaper. Choose aluminium only if mass matters more than cost.

    Material selection steps

    1. Write the function (tie, beam, shaft, spring, bearing surface…) and the objective (minimum mass, minimum cost).
    2. List the constraints that must be met: strength, stiffness, temperature, corrosion, machinability, weldability.
    3. Screen out materials that fail a constraint (e.g. polymers above 100 °C).
    4. Rank the rest by the material index — try both the mass index and the cost index.
    5. Check supporting information: availability, standard sizes, heat treatment, company experience.
    6. Write the material on the drawing with its standard, condition and hardness, e.g. "40Cr4 to IS 1570, hardened and tempered to 250–280 HB".

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Always specify the condition (normalised, hardened and tempered to a hardness range). "C45" alone can be 600 or 900 MPa.
    • Do not choose a high-strength steel to fix a deflection problem — E is the same.
    • Alloy steels need heat treatment to be worth their price. Untreated 40Cr4 is little better than C45.
    • Avoid welding steels above about 0.25 % carbon without preheat — they crack.
    • Look up the ruling section: a big bar of alloy steel does not harden through, so its core strength is lower than the catalogue value for a small test bar.

    What a part costs

    Unit cost = material + machining + (set-up ÷ batch size) + heat treatment + finishing + overhead + profit
    Material = mass of blank × ₹/kg × (1 + scrap)
    Machining = Σ (time on each machine × machine-hour rate)

    Typical machine-hour rates in Indian job shops (2026): conventional lathe ₹400–600, CNC lathe ₹800–1200, VMC ₹1000–1600, cylindrical grinder ₹900–1400, gear hobbing ₹1200–2000. The set-up (loading programs, fixtures, first-off inspection) is paid once per batch, so for one-offs it can dwarf everything else.

    Fixed and variable costs. Tooling, patterns, dies and set-up are fixed; material and cycle time vary with quantity. The break-even quantity between two processes is where their total cost lines cross.

    Surface finish follows the same curve: Ra 6.3 µm (turned) is cheap, Ra 0.8 (ground) costs several times as much, Ra 0.2 (lapped) far more. Put tight tolerances and fine finish only where the function needs them — bearing seats, seal surfaces, sliding fits — and leave everything else to the general tolerance (ISO 2768-m).

    Design for manufacture and assembly (DFMA)

    Fewer partsEach part costs a drawing, a stock item, an inspection and an assembly step. Ask: must it move? Must it be a different material? Must it come apart for service? If not, combine it.
    Standard partsBolts, bearings, circlips, keys, gear motors from catalogues — cheaper and proven.
    Easy machiningThrough holes over blind holes, standard drill and thread sizes, undercuts for thread and grinding run-out, avoid sharp internal corners.
    Easy assemblyTop-down assembly, self-locating features, chamfers on shafts and bores, symmetric parts or obviously asymmetric ones.

    Cost-reduction checklist

    1. Is the material the cheapest that meets the index? Is it stocked locally in this size?
    2. Is the blank close to the finished shape (near-net casting/forging for quantities)?
    3. Can any tight tolerance or fine finish be relaxed? Mark only functional surfaces.
    4. Can two parts become one, or one machined part become a bought standard part?
    5. Can operations be done in one set-up (one clamping) on one machine?
    6. Is heat treatment really needed, and can it be local (induction) instead of whole-part?
    7. Will the batch size justify tooling? Compare process costs at the real annual quantity.

    The basic stresses

    Direct: σ = P / A
    Bending: σ = M·y / I = M / Z (round bar: σ = 32M / πd³)
    Torsion: τ = T·r / J (round bar: τ = 16T / πd³)
    Transverse shear: τ = V·Q / (I·b) (max 4V/3A for a round bar, 3V/2A for a rectangle)
    SectionIZ = I / ymaxJ
    Solid round Ø dπd⁴/64πd³/32πd⁴/32
    Hollow round D, dπ(D⁴−d⁴)/64π(D⁴−d⁴)/32Dπ(D⁴−d⁴)/32
    Rectangle b × hbh³/12bh²/6—

    In a rotating shaft carrying a bending moment and a torque, the most stressed point is on the surface where bending stress is greatest; there σ = 32M/πd³ and τ = 16T/πd³ act together. Transverse shear is zero at that point and is usually ignored for long shafts.

    Stresses on an inclined plane

    A small square cut from the surface carries σx, σy and τxy. Turn the square through θ and the stresses on its faces change:

    σx′ = (σx+σy)/2 + (σx−σy)/2 · cos 2θ + τxy · sin 2θ
    τx′y′ = −(σx−σy)/2 · sin 2θ + τxy · cos 2θ

    At two perpendicular angles the shear vanishes. The normal stresses there are the principal stresses:

    σ₁, σ₂ = (σx+σy)/2 ± √( ((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy² ) tan 2θp = 2τxy / (σx−σy)
    τmax (in plane) = √( ((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy² )

    Mohr's circle

    Otto Mohr noticed that the transformation equations are the equations of a circle in the σ–τ plane: centre ((σx+σy)/2, 0), radius R = √(((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy²). Each point on the circle is the stress on one plane. The circle shows at a glance the largest normal stress (right end), the smallest (left end) and the largest shear (top).

    1. Plot point X (σx, τxy) and point Y (σy, −τxy). Tensile σ to the right.
    2. Join X and Y. The line crosses the σ axis at the centre C.
    3. Draw the circle through X and Y with centre C.
    4. Read σ₁ and σ₂ where the circle meets the σ axis.
    5. Read τmax as the radius.
    6. Angle XCσ₁ is 2θp. A rotation θ of the element is a rotation 2θ on the circle.
    Plane stress has a third principal stress. On a free surface σ₃ = 0. If σ₁ and σ₂ are both tensile, the largest shear is σ₁/2 (in a plane out of the paper), not (σ₁−σ₂)/2. Mohr's three circles show this.

    Materials and the type of stress

    Ductile metals (steel, aluminium) fail by shear — slip along 45° planes; brittle ones (cast iron, hardened steel) fail by tensile stress across the largest principal plane. A cast-iron bar twisted to failure breaks on a 45° helix; a mild-steel bar breaks straight across. That is why Day 5 uses different failure theories for the two.

    Worked example

    Shaft with bending and torsiond = 40 mm, M = 600 N·m, T = 800 N·m σx = 32M/πd³ = 32 × 600 000 / (π × 64 000) = 95.5 MPa, σy = 0 τxy = 16T/πd³ = 16 × 800 000 / (π × 64 000) = 63.7 MPa C = 47.7, R = √(47.7² + 63.7²) = 79.6 MPa σ₁ = 127.3 MPa, σ₂ = −31.9 MPa, τmax = 79.6 MPa θp = ½ atan(2 × 63.7 / 95.5) = 26.6°

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Pick a sign convention and stick to it: tensile positive; τxy positive when it acts in +y on the +x face.
    • Use the section modulus of the net section at a keyway or hole, not of the full bar.
    • For short, stubby members (pins, keys, gear teeth roots) transverse shear is not negligible.
    • Check units: N·m × 1000 = N·mm. Most "stress of 0.095 MPa" mistakes are N·m left in.

    Why theories are needed

    A tensile test gives one number — the yield strength Sy, in a simple uniaxial stress. Real parts have two or three principal stresses at once. A failure theory predicts when that combination is as dangerous as the tensile test at yield.

    The theories

    TheoryFailure whenFor
    Maximum normal stress (Rankine)σ₁ ≥ Sut or σ₃ ≤ −SucBrittle materials (simple, approximate)
    Maximum shear stress (Tresca, Guest)(σ₁ − σ₃)/2 ≥ Sy/2Ductile — simple and conservative
    Distortion energy (von Mises, Hencky)σ′ ≥ SyDuctile — best agreement with tests
    Coulomb–Mohr / modified Mohrσ₁/Sut − σ₃/Suc ≥ 1Brittle with Suc > Sut (cast iron)
    von Mises stress (plane): σ′ = √(σ₁² − σ₁σ₂ + σ₂²) = √(σx² − σxσy + σy² + 3τxy²)
    Shaft (σ, τ): σ′ = √(σ² + 3τ²) Tresca equivalent: √(σ² + 4τ²)
    Factor of safety: n = Sy / σ′

    For a shaft, substituting σ = 32M/πd³ and τ = 16T/πd³:

    Tresca: d³ = (32n / πSy) · √(M² + T²)
    von Mises: d³ = (32n / πSy) · √(M² + ¾T²)

    Pictures in the σ₁–σ₂ plane

    Plot every combination of σ₁ and σ₂ that just causes failure and you get an envelope: a square for Rankine, a hexagon for Tresca, an ellipse through the hexagon's corners for von Mises. A stress state inside is safe; the distance to the boundary along the load line is the factor of safety.

    Shear yield strength. In pure shear (σ₁ = −σ₂ = τ), Tresca predicts yield at τ = 0.5 Sy, von Mises at τ = 0.577 Sy. That 15.5 % gap is the largest difference between them. Many codes use Ssy = 0.5–0.577 Sy for this reason.

    Choosing by material

    Ductile (elongation > 5 %)Use von Mises (or Tresca for a quick, safe answer). Base n on Sy.
    Brittle (cast iron, hardened tool steel)Use Coulomb–Mohr or modified Mohr with Sut and Suc. Base n on ultimate strength, n ≥ 3.
    Repeated loadsNone of these alone — use fatigue methods (Days 7–8). Static theories give the first-cycle yield check.

    1. Find the critical point and all stresses there (Day 4).
    2. Calculate principal stresses σ₁, σ₂, σ₃ (σ₃ = 0 on a free surface).
    3. Decide ductile or brittle from the elongation.

    Stress flow

    Imagine stress as water flowing through the part. At a sudden change of section the "flow lines" crowd together and the local stress peaks. The ratio of the peak to the nominal stress (calculated on the net section) is the theoretical stress concentration factor:

    Kt = σmax / σnom Kts = τmax / τnom

    Kt depends only on geometry and type of load — not on the material or size. Peterson's Stress Concentration Factors is the standard source; design data books reproduce the common charts.

    FeatureTypical Kt
    Small hole in a wide plate (tension)3.0
    Shaft shoulder D/d = 1.5, r/d = 0.02 (bending)≈ 2.5
    Shaft shoulder D/d = 1.5, r/d = 0.1 (bending)≈ 1.7
    Profile keyway (end-milled), bending / torsion≈ 2.0 / 3.0 (Kf)
    Sled-runner keyway, bending≈ 1.6
    Retaining-ring groove≈ 5
    Thread root (rolled / cut)Kf ≈ 2.2–3.0 / 2.8–3.8

    When it matters

    Ductile, staticThe peak yields locally and redistributes. Usually ignored (Kt = 1).
    Brittle, staticNo yielding to relieve it. Use full Kt. (Grey cast iron is an exception: its graphite flakes are already notches, so Kt ≈ 1.)
    Any material, fatigueCracks start at the peak. Use Kf — almost all fatigue failures start at a stress raiser.

    Notch sensitivity

    In fatigue, a sharp notch hurts less than Kt predicts, because the steep stress gradient means only a tiny volume is highly stressed. Notch sensitivity q (0 to 1) measures how much of Kt is felt:

    Kf = 1 + q (Kt − 1)
    Neuber: q = 1 / (1 + √a / √r) — √a falls as Sut rises
    Peterson: q = 1 / (1 + a/r)

    High-strength steels have q close to 1 — they feel nearly the full Kt. Soft steels and small radii give lower q. If in doubt, take q = 1 (Kf = Kt): safe.

    Reducing stress concentration

    1. Use the largest fillet radius the mating part allows. Bearing seats: use a relief groove or a spacer with a chamfer so a big radius fits under the bearing's corner.
    2. Keep D/d small — big steps concentrate more.
    3. Add relief grooves near a sharp notch to spread the flow lines.
    4. Put holes and keyways where bending stress is low (near the neutral axis or at a support).
    5. Use sled-runner rather than profile keyways at fatigue-critical sections.
    6. Roll threads and fillets, shot-peen, or nitride — compressive residual stress delays cracks.

    Worked example

    Shoulder on a C45 shaft in rotating bendingd = 40, D = 60, r = 2 mm → D/d = 1.5, r/d = 0.05 Kt (Peterson) ≈ 2.04 Sut = 620 MPa → √a ≈ 0.072 √in = 0.36 √mm; q = 1/(1 + 0.36/√2) = 0.80 Kf = 1 + 0.80 × (2.04 − 1) = 1.83 With r = 4 mm: Kt ≈ 1.70, q = 0.85, Kf ≈ 1.59 → 13 % lower peak stress, for free.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Write the fillet radius on the drawing — "sharp corner" left to the machinist means r ≈ 0.2 mm and Kt ≈ 3.
    • Do not combine Kt factors by multiplying when two raisers are far apart; when they overlap (a hole at a fillet), multiplying is a rough upper bound.
    • FEA finds Kt for you, but only if the mesh is fine at the fillet. A coarse mesh under-predicts the peak.
    • Apply Kt to the nominal stress on the net section the chart was made for.
