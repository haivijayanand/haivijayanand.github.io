The Ideal World and the Real World. Are We the Person We Wish Others Would Be?

11 Sep 20268 min read

Most of us carry an invisible wish list about the people around us.

We want a friend who understands us without lengthy explanations.

A partner who senses our stress and gives us comfort rather than adding to it.

A boss who recognises our effort, guides us when we struggle, appreciates our contribution and protects us when necessary.

A co-worker who works at our frequency, communicates openly, shares responsibility and does not play politics.

A junior who understands our expectations, takes ownership and completes work without requiring constant follow-up.

It sounds wonderful.

And occasionally, we are fortunate enough to meet such people.

But that is the ideal world.

The real world is considerably more complicated.

When Frequencies Don't Match

Two people may be intelligent, hardworking and sincere—and still find it extremely difficult to work together.

Why?

Because human relationships are not governed only by competence.

They are influenced by personality, communication style, ego, insecurity, ambition, past experiences, expectations, hierarchy and individual objectives.

What I consider guidance, another person may experience as interference.

What I consider constructive criticism, someone else may experience as humiliation.

What I consider urgency, another person may perceive as unnecessary pressure.

What I consider confidence, someone else may interpret as arrogance.

And what I consider silence to avoid conflict may be interpreted by the other person as indifference.

The difficulty is that both sides often believe that their interpretation is the correct one.

That is where misunderstanding begins.

The Ideal World

In an ideal workplace or relationship, communication would be simple.

When someone makes a mistake, we would correct the mistake without attacking the person.

When someone performs well, we would appreciate the effort.

When someone struggles, we would guide them.

When someone disagrees, we would listen before responding.

When tension develops, both sides would attempt to reduce it rather than win the argument.

When responsibility is unclear, people would discuss it rather than blame one another.

And when somebody is already under pressure, we would avoid unnecessarily increasing that pressure.

Essentially, everyone would behave with maturity, empathy and self-awareness.

Unfortunately, human beings don't always operate that way.

Welcome to the Real World

The real world contains miscommunication.

It contains ego.

It contains different end objectives.

It contains competition for recognition, promotions, resources and authority.

Sometimes there are genuine conflicts of interest.

Sometimes people deliberately withhold information.

Sometimes appreciation is absent.

Sometimes good work is taken for granted while a small mistake receives enormous attention.

Sometimes instructions are unclear, but the person executing them is blamed for misunderstanding.

Sometimes a junior feels unsupported.

Sometimes a manager feels that nobody understands the pressure coming from above.

Sometimes a senior believes he is pushing people towards excellence while the people below him experience only criticism.

And sometimes a simple disagreement escalates because of one unnecessary sentence.

The original problem may have been technical.

The final problem becomes emotional.

Words Can Escalate Faster Than Problems

One of the most underestimated forces in human relationships is language.

Consider the difference:

"This work needs improvement."

and

"You never do anything properly."

The first statement addresses the work.

The second attacks the person.

Once the person feels attacked, the discussion changes.

The technical problem is no longer the central issue.

Now there is anger.

There is humiliation.

There is defensiveness.

There is memory.

And unfortunately, human beings remember emotional wounds remarkably well.

A ten-minute argument can influence a relationship for ten years.

That is why verbal abuse, sarcasm, public humiliation and unnecessary criticism are extremely dangerous managerial and interpersonal tools.

You may win the argument.

You may lose the person.

Encouragement Is Not Weakness

There is another mistake we frequently make.

We assume people already know that we appreciate them.

"Why should I say it? He knows he has done good work."

Perhaps.

But human beings need acknowledgement.

A sincere,

"Good work."

"You handled that situation well."

"I know this was difficult."

"Thank you for taking responsibility."

can sometimes remove enormous psychological pressure.

Encouragement does not mean ignoring mistakes.

Good leadership requires correction.

But correction and encouragement are not opposites.

A mature person can say:

"This part was done very well. This particular area needs correction. Let us improve it."

The objective should be to improve performance—not damage dignity.

Before Asking for Better People, Ask a Difficult Question

We frequently think:

"I wish I had a better boss."

"I wish my colleague understood me."

"I wish my partner were more supportive."

"I wish my juniors took more responsibility."

There is nothing wrong with those expectations.

But occasionally we should reverse the question.

Am I the boss I wish I had?

Am I the colleague I wish I worked with?

Am I the partner I wish I lived with?

Am I the senior whom I would have wanted when I was a junior?

Am I the friend whom I expect my friends to be?

These questions are uncomfortable.

That is precisely why they are valuable.

Your Behaviour Creates an Environment Around You

People gradually adapt to the environment we create.

If a manager repeatedly humiliates people for bringing bad news, employees eventually stop bringing bad news.

The problems do not disappear.

The reporting disappears.

If someone reacts aggressively whenever questioned, people gradually stop questioning them.

It may initially feel like respect.

It may actually be fear.

If a person takes credit for every success and distributes blame for every failure, colleagues eventually stop giving their best.

If someone constantly suspects others, people become defensive around them.

Conversely, when people know that mistakes can be discussed honestly, they communicate earlier.

When good work is recognised, discretionary effort increases.

When disagreement is permitted without retaliation, better ideas emerge.

When dignity is preserved during correction, relationships survive difficult situations.

In other words:

We slowly teach people how to behave around us through the way we behave around them.

What Goes Out Often Comes Back

This does not mean life operates through some guaranteed cosmic accounting system.

Good people sometimes receive poor treatment.

Difficult people sometimes succeed.

But behaviour has consequences.

If you repeatedly create fear, eventually people become cautious around you.

If you repeatedly insult people, eventually they stop trusting you.

If you refuse to appreciate anyone, eventually people stop seeking your appreciation.

If you constantly escalate conflicts, eventually people approach you already prepared for conflict.

And if you consistently demonstrate fairness, patience and respect, you increase the probability that people will respond similarly.

Our behaviour develops a reputation.

Reputation shapes relationships.

Relationships shape cooperation.

Cooperation influences opportunities.

And opportunities influence our future.

So what we give to the environment often finds its way back to us—not through magic, but through human networks and accumulated consequences.

The Ten-Second Self-Check

Before sending an angry message, shouting at a junior, humiliating a colleague, blaming a partner or reacting emotionally to a friend, it may be worth spending ten seconds asking:

What am I trying to achieve?

Am I trying to solve the problem?

Or am I trying to prove that I am right?

Am I correcting someone?

Or am I punishing them emotionally?

Will what I am about to say improve tomorrow's relationship?

Or will it merely satisfy today's anger?

Would I accept these same words if somebody senior to me spoke them to me?

And perhaps the most important question:

If everyone around me behaved exactly the way I am behaving now, would this become a better place to live and work?

If the answer is no, something needs to change.

We Cannot Choose Everyone Around Us

We cannot always choose our boss.

We cannot choose every colleague.

Managers cannot always choose every member of their teams.

Even in families and friendships, personalities evolve and circumstances change.

Therefore, waiting for perfectly compatible people is unrealistic.

The more practical skill is learning to work constructively with people whose frequency does not perfectly match ours.

That requires communication.

Boundaries.

Patience.

Assertiveness.

Empathy.

And sometimes the maturity to walk away from an unnecessary argument.

Not every disagreement needs a winner.

Sometimes the greatest victory is preventing a disagreement from becoming a damaged relationship.

Become a Little More Like the Person You Are Searching For

We may never create the ideal world.

There will always be difficult bosses.

There will be insensitive colleagues.

There will be inexperienced juniors.

There will be misunderstandings between partners.

There will be ego, ambition, pressure and conflicting objectives.

But we still control one significant variable:

ourselves.

Perhaps the objective should not be to search endlessly for people who perfectly match our frequency.

Instead, we can become slightly more conscious of the frequency we transmit.

Listen a little more.

React a little less.

Correct without humiliating.

Appreciate without hesitation.

Disagree without disrespect.

Guide without dominating.

Be firm without becoming cruel.

And before demanding understanding from others, make an effort to understand them.

Because eventually people may forget the details of the problem you solved together.

But they are unlikely to forget how you made them feel while solving it.

The ideal world may never arrive.

But every conscious interaction can make the real world a little closer to it.
