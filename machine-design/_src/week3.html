    Shaft layout

    A shaft carries gears, pulleys, sprockets or couplings, and rests in two bearings. Its diameter steps up from each end so that every component slides on from one side and is located against a shoulder.

    Locate axiallyShoulders, spacers, circlips, lock-nuts, set screws (light duty). One bearing locates (fixed), the other floats for thermal expansion.
    Transmit torqueKeys, splines, interference fits, taper locks (Day 15).
    Keep it shortBearings close to heavy loads; overhung loads as short as possible. Short shafts are stiffer and need smaller diameters.
    Mind the stress raisersPut keyways and circlip grooves where the bending moment is small; give shoulders generous fillets (Day 6).

    Forces and moments

    Resolve every gear and belt force into two planes (vertical and horizontal), find the bearing reactions and bending moment diagram in each plane, then combine:

    M = √(MV² + MH²) T = 9550 × P(kW) / n(rpm) N·m
    Belt pull on shaft ≈ T₁ + T₂; gear forces Ft, Fr, Fa from Day 23

    ASME code equation

    The ASME code (1927, still common in Indian practice and exams) uses maximum shear stress with shock and fatigue factors:

    d³ = (16 / π·τallow) · √((Kb·M)² + (Kt·T)²)
    τallow = 0.30 Syt or 0.18 Sut, whichever is less; × 0.75 if a keyway is at the section
    LoadKb (bending)Kt (torsion)
    Rotating shaft, gradually applied1.51.0
    Suddenly applied, minor shock1.5–2.01.0–1.5
    Heavy shock2.0–3.01.5–3.0

    DE-Goodman (fatigue) equation

    A rotating shaft sees fully reversed bending (Ma = M) and usually a steady torque (Tm = T). Combining von Mises and Goodman (Days 5, 8):

    d = [ (16n/π) · ( 2·Kf·Ma/Se + √3·Kfs·Tm/Sut ) ]1/3
    (general form adds 2·Kf·Mm/Sut and √3·Kfs·Ta/Se when present)

    Use Se with Marin factors for the actual diameter (iterate once: guess d, find kb, recalculate), and Kf, Kfs for the shoulder or keyway at that section. Check every critical section: each shoulder, keyway and groove, not only the point of maximum moment.

    Worked example

    15 kW at 720 rpm, maximum bending moment 450 N·m at a keyed gear seat; C45: Syt 380, Sut 620T = 9550 × 15/720 = 198.9 N·m ASME: τallow = min(114, 111.6) × 0.75 = 83.7 MPa; Te = √((1.5×450)² + (1×198.9)²) = 703.7 N·m d = (16 × 703 700 / (π × 83.7))^(1/3) = 35.0 mm DE-Goodman (Se 230, Kf 1.7, Kfs 1.5, n 2): d = 42.4 mm Choose 45 mm at the gear seat, then check slope at the bearings and critical speed.

    Shaft design procedure

    1. Sketch the layout: bearing positions, component positions, shoulders.
    2. Find torque and all component forces; resolve into two planes.
    3. Find bearing reactions and bending moment diagrams; combine to M at each section.
    4. Pick a material; estimate d by ASME at the worst section.
    5. Check each critical section in fatigue with DE-Goodman using real Kf, Kfs and Se.
    6. Round up to standard sizes; set bearing seat diameters to bearing bore sizes (multiples of 5 mm from 20 mm).
    7. Check deflection, slope at bearings and gears, and critical speed (Day 14).

    Lab: shaft diameter calculator

    Enter power, speed, bending moment and material. Both methods are worked; the larger diameter governs.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Do not size only at the maximum moment — a smaller diameter at a shoulder with Kf 2 often governs.
    • Gear slope must stay below about 0.0005 rad for good tooth contact; deep groove ball bearings tolerate about 0.001–0.003 rad, cylindrical rollers only 0.0005–0.001 rad.
    • Hollow shafts save mass: a tube with di = 0.5 D loses only 6 % strength and 6 % stiffness but 25 % mass.
    • Write the fillet radii and the surface finish of bearing seats on the drawing.

    Lateral rigidity

    Simply supported, central load W: δmax = W·L³ / (48·E·I) slope at bearings θ = W·L² / (16·E·I)
    Load W at a from left (b = L − a), deflection at x ≤ a: y = W·b·x·(L² − b² − x²) / (6·E·I·L)
    Several loads: add the deflections (superposition)
    LimitTypical value
    Deflection at a gear0.01 × module (spur), or 0.0002–0.0005 × span
    Slope at a spur gear≤ 0.0005 rad
    Slope at a deep groove ball bearing≤ 0.001–0.003 rad
    Slope at a cylindrical or tapered roller bearing≤ 0.0005–0.001 rad
    Machine-tool spindlesMuch tighter — set by accuracy

    Torsional rigidity

    θ = T·L / (G·J) J = π·d⁴/32, G ≈ 79–81 GPa for steel
    Common limit: 0.25° per metre for line shafts and machine-tool drives; 1°/m for general shafts

    Torsional stiffness also sets the torsional natural frequency of a drive train (motor, coupling, gearbox, load): f = (1/2π)·√(k/I) for two inertias joined by stiffness k. Gearbox test rigs and engine drives must keep this away from firing and gear-mesh frequencies.

    Critical speed (whirling)

    A rotor is never perfectly balanced. As speed rises, the out-of-balance force bends the shaft, which increases the eccentricity, which increases the force. At the critical speed — the natural frequency of lateral vibration — the deflection becomes large, limited only by damping. Above it the rotor "inverts" and runs more smoothly again (super-critical running, as in turbines).

    Single disc: ωc = √(g / δst) → Nc ≈ 946 / √δst(mm) rpm
    Rayleigh (upper estimate): ωc = √( g·Σmᵢyᵢ / Σmᵢyᵢ² ) (yᵢ = static deflections under all loads)
    Dunkerley (lower estimate): 1/ωc² = 1/ωs² + Σ 1/ωᵢ² (ωᵢ for each mass alone, ωs for the shaft alone)
    Uniform shaft, simply supported: ωs = (π/L)²·√(E·I / (ρ·A))
    Keep away from the critical speed. Run below about 0.7 Nc, or above about 1.3–1.4 Nc and pass through quickly. Rotating machinery standards (API, ISO) require a separation margin of 15–20 % or more.

    Materials and cost

    All steels have the same E, so stiffness costs diameter: δ ∝ 1/d⁴. A 19 % larger diameter halves the deflection. Shorter spans help even more (δ ∝ L³). Moving bearings closer to the gear is usually the cheapest fix of all.

    Worked example

    Ø50 steel shaft, 900 mm span, discs 30 kg at 270 mm and 20 kg at 630 mm, running 1450 rpmI = π×0.05⁴/64 = 3.07×10⁻⁷ m⁴, E = 207 GPa Static deflections under the discs (both loads acting): 77.4 µm and 74.7 µm Shaft alone: ωs → 7469 rpm Rayleigh (combined with the shaft by Dunkerley): 3112 rpm Dunkerley: 3009 rpm Running at 1450 rpm = 48 % of the critical speed — sub-critical with a good margin.

    Procedure

    1. Find the static deflection under each mass by superposition (include the shaft's weight for long shafts).
    2. Calculate the critical speed by Rayleigh (and Dunkerley as a lower bound).
    3. Compare with the running speed range; keep the separation margin.
    4. Check slopes at the bearings and gears against their limits.
    5. If too flexible: shorten spans, enlarge the diameter, add a bearing, or move heavy discs toward the supports.
    6. For important rotors, confirm with FEA or a transfer-matrix program, including bearing stiffness.

    Lab: Dunkerley and Rayleigh

    Move the discs, change the span and diameter. The curve is the static deflected shape; the critical speeds and the running margin update live.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Bearings and housings are not rigid. Real critical speeds are 10–30 % lower than rigid-support calculations.
    • Overhung masses (a pulley or fan outside the bearings) lower the critical speed sharply.
    • Variable-speed drives sweep through many frequencies — check the whole speed range, not just the rated speed.
    • Vibration measurements with a run-up (Bode plot) find the real critical speed of an installed machine.

    Keys

    A parallel (feather) key sits half in the shaft, half in the hub. Its width b and height h come from the shaft diameter (IS 2292 / ISO R773); you choose only the length.

    Shaft Ø over–up tob × hShaft Øb × h
    17–226 × 644–5014 × 9
    22–308 × 750–5816 × 10
    30–3810 × 858–6518 × 11
    38–4412 × 865–7520 × 12
    Force on key at shaft surface: F = 2T / d
    Shear: τ = 2T / (d·b·l) Crushing: σc = 4T / (d·h·l) (half the key height bears)
    Typical allowables (C45 key): τ ≈ 60–80 MPa, σc ≈ 100–150 MPa (lower if the hub is cast iron)

    Other keys: Woodruff (half-moon, self-aligning, for tapered shafts and light torque), taper/gib-head (driven in, for heavy rough drives), splines (many teeth, for high torque, sliding gears and automotive shafts — involute splines to ISO 4156), and keyless taper-lock bushes and shrink discs that clamp by friction with no stress raiser.

    Rigid flange coupling

    Two cast-iron or steel flanges keyed to the shaft ends and bolted together. Torque passes by bolt shear (fitted bolts) or by friction (clearance bolts with preload).

    Bolts in shear: T = n · (π/4)·db² · τb · (D₁/2) → db = √(8T / (π·τb·n·D₁))
    Bolts by friction: T = μ·n·Fi·(D₁/2)
    Hub: Dh ≈ 2d, length ≈ 1.5d; flange thickness ≈ 0.5d, check shear at hub: τ = 2T / (π·Dh²·t)

    Rigid couplings need near-perfect alignment; any offset bends both shafts every revolution — a fatigue failure waiting to happen.

    Materials and cost — the weak link

    Keys are made of C45 and are cheap to replace; couplings can be replaced in an hour. So they are designed to fail before the shaft, the gear or the motor. Torque-limiting couplings and shear pins take this further on crushers and conveyors. Keyways themselves cost fatigue strength (Kf up to 3 in torsion, Day 6) — for high-cycle shafts prefer shrink discs or splines.

    Worked example

    Ø45 shaft, T = 600 N·m, key 60 mm long; 4-bolt coupling on a 150 mm PCDKey: Ø45 → 14 × 9 τ = 2 × 600 000 / (45 × 14 × 60) = 31.7 MPa (allow 70) ✓ σc = 4 × 600 000 / (45 × 9 × 60) = 98.8 MPa (allow 140) ✓; minimum length 42.3 mm Shaft torsional stress 16T/πd³ = 33.5 MPa Coupling bolts: d = √(8 × 600 000 / (π × 60 × 4 × 150)) = 6.5 mm → M8 fitted bolts (M10 to be practical)

    Procedure

    1. Find the design torque = nominal torque × service factor.
    2. Select the standard key for the shaft diameter.
    3. Find the key length from shear and crushing; keep it ≤ hub length, ≥ 1.5d for long life.
    4. For couplings: choose type from misalignment and shock; select size from the catalogue rating.
    5. For a rigid flange coupling, size bolts, flange and hub and check each.
    6. Check that key and coupling fail before the shaft.

    Lab: key and flange coupling check

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Two keys at 180° carry about 1.5× (not 2×) the torque of one — they never share equally.
    • Round-ended keys in sled-runner seats have a lower Kf than square-ended keys in profile-milled seats.
    • Align couplings with a dial indicator or laser — bad alignment is the commonest cause of bearing failures in pumps.
    • Set screws on top of the key stop axial creep; do not rely on them for torque.

    Spring types

    Helical compressionThe commonest. Valves, clutches, dies, buffers.
    Helical extensionHooks at the ends; initial tension. Hooks are the weak point.
    TorsionWound helix loaded by a moment: clothes pegs, door returns, ratchets.

    Stress and rate of a compression spring

    Spring index C = D / d (keep 4 ≤ C ≤ 12)
    Wahl factor Kw = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C
    Shear stress τ = Kw · 8·F·D / (π·d³)
    Rate k = F/δ = G·d⁴ / (8·D³·Na) (G ≈ 79.3 GPa spring steel)

    The Wahl factor adds the direct shear and the extra stress on the inside of the coil caused by its curvature. Small C (tightly coiled) means high Kw and hard coiling; large C means a floppy spring that tangles.

    PlainNad(Nt + 1)

    Buckling, surge and fatigue

    Buckling: a long spring bows sideways. Stable if L₀/D < 2.63 with hinged ends, < 5.26 with both ends flat and parallel (fixed). Otherwise guide it on a rod or in a tube.

    Surge: a spring has its own natural frequency f = (d / 2πD²Na)·√(G/2ρ). If the operating frequency (a cam, a valve) excites it, coils clash and the spring breaks. Keep f above about 13–15 times the operating frequency.

    Fatigue: springs work between a preload and a maximum, so use τm, τa with the Goodman or the Zimmerli data (unpeened Ssa ≈ 241 MPa, Ssm ≈ 379 MPa; peened Ssa ≈ 398, Ssm ≈ 534 MPa). Shot peening raises spring fatigue life dramatically.

    Types

    Single-plateCars, tractors. One plate, two friction faces, spring-loaded.
    Multi-plateMotorcycles, automatic gearboxes, machine tools — compact, often oil-cooled (wet).
    ConeWedge action multiplies the axial force; synchronisers in gearboxes.
    CentrifugalEngages automatically with speed: mopeds, chainsaws.
    ElectromagneticRemote switching: packaging machines, AC compressors.
    Overrunning (sprag/roller)Drives one way only: starters, backstops on conveyors.

    Torque capacity

    For an annular face, inner radius Ri, outer Ro, axial force W, friction μ, n friction surfaces:

    Uniform pressure (new clutch): W = π·p·(Ro² − Ri²) T = n·(2/3)·μ·W·(Ro³ − Ri³)/(Ro² − Ri²)
    Uniform wear (worn-in): p·r = constant; W = 2π·pa·Ri·(Ro − Ri) T = n·μ·W·(Ro + Ri)/2
    Cone clutch: divide T by sin α (semi-cone angle); engaging force is larger than W while sliding

    Wear is proportional to pressure × sliding velocity. Outer parts slide faster, wear faster, and lose pressure until p·r is constant. A clutch spends most of its life worn-in, so design on uniform wear — it gives the lower (safe) torque. The maximum pressure is then at the inner radius.

    Best proportion: for a given Ro and allowable pa, uniform-wear torque is maximum at Ri = Ro/√3 ≈ 0.577 Ro. Practical clutches use Ri/Ro ≈ 0.55–0.7.

    Heat

    During engagement the plates slip until the driven side reaches the driver's speed. The energy dissipated equals the kinetic energy gained by the load (for a load starting from rest, about the same again is lost as heat).

    Heat per engagement ≈ ½·I·ω² (load accelerated from rest) Temperature rise ΔT = E / (m·c)
    Check the average power: heat per engagement × engagements per hour ≤ what the housing can dissipate

    The mating surface is cast iron or steel. Wet clutches have low μ, so they need more plates, but run cooler and last much longer. Lining replacement is the main running cost; specify a generous area to cut wear rate (wear ∝ energy per unit area).

    Single-plate clutch, 2 faces, Do = 300, Di = 180 mm, μ = 0.3, pa = 0.35 MPaUniform wear: W = 2π × 0.35 × 90 × (150 − 90) = 11.88 kN T = 2 × 0.3 × 11 880 × (150 + 90)/2 = 855 N·m → at 1440 rpm: 129 kW Uniform pressure (new): W = 15.83 kN, T = 1164 N·m Ri/Ro = 0.6 — close to the 0.577 optimum.

    Design procedure

    1. Find the design torque: engine/motor torque × service factor (1.3–2.0), plus a margin so the clutch slips before the driveline breaks.
    2. Choose friction material: μ and allowable pa.
    3. Choose Ro from the space; take Ri ≈ 0.6 Ro.
    4. Find the number of friction surfaces needed by uniform wear.
    5. Find the spring force W; choose springs (Day 16) or diaphragm spring.
    6. Check heat per engagement and per hour; temperature rise of the plates.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • A clutch that slips under normal load glazes and dies quickly. Size with margin, but not so much that it never protects the driveline.
    • Cone angles below about 12° can jam (self-lock) — keep α above the friction angle.
    • Oil on a dry lining drops μ by half — seal well.
    • Multi-plate clutches lose some force to spline friction; inner plates see less pressure.

    Types

    Block (shoe) brakeA shoe pressed on a drum by a lever: hoists, railway wagons.
    Band brakeA steel band lined with friction material wrapped round a drum: winches, cranes, scooters (old).
    Internal expanding shoeTwo shoes inside a drum: car and truck rear brakes.
    Disc brakePads clamp a rotating disc: cars, bikes, test rigs — cools well, fades little.
    Electromagnetic / spring-appliedFail-safe motor brakes on lifts and hoists: springs apply, current releases.
    Eddy-current / regenerativeNon-contact retarders and dynamometers.

    Band brake

    Friction builds up round the drum: the tension rises exponentially from the slack end T₂ to the tight end T₁ (the same physics as a belt on a pulley, Day 19, and a rope round a bollard).

    T₁ / T₂ = eμθ (θ in radians)
    Braking torque = (T₁ − T₂)·r
    Simple band brake (tight end at the pivot, slack end at a from the pivot): P·l = T₂·a
    Maximum lining pressure p = T₁ / (w·r)

    When the drum rotates so the friction pulls the band tighter on the lever side, the brake is self-energising — less effort for the same torque. In a differential band brake the arrangement can become self-locking (P ≤ 0), useful for a backstop, dangerous for a brake.

    Block brake

    Normal force N on the shoe; friction F = μ·N; torque = μ·N·r
    Long shoe (contact angle 2θ > 60°): use equivalent μ′ = μ · 4 sin θ / (2θ + sin 2θ)
    Lever: take moments about the pivot — the friction force helps or hurts depending on direction

    Heat — the real limit

    Energy per stop: E = ½·m·v² + ½·I·ω² (+ m·g·h for lowering)
    Temperature rise of the drum/disc for one stop: ΔT = E / (md·c) (c ≈ 460 J/kg·K steel/iron)
    Repeated stops: average heat = E × stops per hour; must be dissipated as h·A·(T − T₀)
    Rubbing-surface check: p·v ≤ ~2 MPa·m/s (heavy duty) for organic linings

    Hot brakes fade: μ drops as the lining overheats. Discs cool better than drums because both faces are exposed and they can be ventilated.

    Materials and cost

    Drums and discs: grey cast iron (good heat capacity, conductivity and damping), sometimes steel or carbon-ceramic for racing. Linings: organic (non-asbestos), semi-metallic, sintered metal, ceramic. Asbestos linings are banned in India and most countries. A larger drum costs a little more but lowers lining pressure, wear and temperature — and lining replacement is a recurring cost for the user.

    Worked example

    Band brake: r = 250 mm, wrap 270°, μ = 0.3, T₁ = 10 kN, a = 60, l = 600 mm, band 80 mm wideθ = 4.712 rad → T₁/T₂ = e^(0.3 × 4.712) = 4.11 → T₂ = 2.43 kN Torque = (10 − 2.43) × 0.25 = 1.89 kN·m Lever force = 2430 × 60/600 = 243 N p max = 10 000 / (80 × 250) = 0.5 MPa Stopping 12 kg·m² from 960 rpm: E = ½ × 12 × 100.5² = 60.6 kJ → 40 kg drum warms 3.3 °C per stop.

    Design procedure

    1. Find the braking torque: from the load torque (hoist) or the deceleration required (I·α), with a factor of 1.5–2.
    2. Choose the brake type and lining (μ, allowable p, temperature).
    3. Choose the drum/disc size; calculate forces, lever ratio and actuator force.
    4. Check lining pressure and p·v.
    5. Calculate energy per stop, temperature rise and average heat; check dissipation.
    6. For hoists and lifts, make it fail-safe: spring-applied, power-released.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • A brake that is self-energising in one direction is weak in the other — think about reverse rotation (vehicles rolling back, hoists).
    • Design the friction pair: hard lining eats the drum, soft lining wears fast.
    • Bedding-in (gentle stops at first) raises the working μ to its design value.
    • In gearbox test rigs and dynamometers, absorbed power over hours is the issue — water-cooled or eddy-current brakes, not friction brakes.
