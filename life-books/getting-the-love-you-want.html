Getting the Love You Want

Harville Hendrix · first published 1988 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

The struggles of a committed relationship are not a sign of failure but an invitation to heal old childhood hurts, and couples who learn to listen and change for each other can grow a conscious, lasting love.

Summary

Harville Hendrix, a marriage therapist, asks a simple question: why do two people who fall deeply in love so often end up hurting each other? His answer is that we are not choosing partners at random. Without realising it, we are drawn to someone who carries a mix of the good and difficult qualities of the people who raised us. He calls this inner picture the imago.

According to Hendrix, a part of us hopes this new person will finally give us what we missed as children, such as attention, safety or freedom. That is why the early romantic stage feels so magical. Sooner or later, though, the same partner touches our oldest sore spots, and a power struggle begins. Each person tries to make the other change, and both feel misunderstood.

Hendrix argues that this conflict is not the end of love but the start of real work. He invites couples to build a conscious partnership, where both people understand their own wounds and agree to help heal each other's, instead of reacting on autopilot.

His best-known tool is a structured conversation often called the couples dialogue. One partner speaks while the other mirrors back what was said, then validates it by saying how it makes sense, and finally tries to imagine the feeling behind it. Slowing down like this turns arguments into understanding.

He also encourages partners to stop using quiet escape routes, such as overwork, endless screens or spending more time with friends, to avoid each other. Couples are asked to turn frustrations into specific, kind requests, and to stretch by offering the very behaviour their partner most needs, even when it feels unnatural.

Finally, Hendrix stresses deliberate kindness: regular caring gestures, small surprises and shared fun. Over time, these habits rebuild safety and warmth. Love, in his view, is not just found; it is made, day by day, by two people choosing to grow together.

319 words

One-line takeaway: This week, set aside fifteen calm minutes with your partner and take turns: one speaks about a small concern while the other simply repeats back what they heard, says why it makes sense, and imagines how it feels, before switching roles.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What does Hendrix mean by the 'imago'?

    Answer B. The imago is the unconscious image, formed in childhood, that guides our attraction to a partner.

  2. According to the book, why does early romantic love feel so powerful?

    Answer C. Hendrix links the intensity of new love to the hope of finally receiving what we missed growing up.

  3. What stage usually follows the romantic stage in Hendrix's view?

    Answer A. He describes a power struggle in which each partner tries to change the other.

  4. How does Hendrix view conflict in a committed relationship?

    Answer D. He reframes conflict as the starting point of a conscious, healing partnership.

  5. What are the three steps of the couples dialogue?

    Answer B. The listener mirrors what was said, validates it, and then empathises with the feeling.

  6. In the dialogue, what does 'mirroring' involve?

    Answer C. Mirroring means reflecting your partner's words back so they feel accurately heard.

  7. What does Hendrix mean by 'exits' in a relationship?

    Answer A. Exits are habits that drain energy away from the relationship to avoid intimacy.

  8. What does 'stretching' mean in the book?

    Answer D. Stretching is growing beyond your comfort zone to meet your partner's needs, which also helps you grow.

  9. How does Hendrix suggest turning frustrations into something useful?

    Answer B. He encourages partners to express needs as clear, positive requests rather than criticism.

  10. Which practice does the book recommend for rebuilding warmth and safety?

    Answer C. Deliberate kindness and playfulness help couples restore a sense of safety and affection.
