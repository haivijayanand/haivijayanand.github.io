The Waiting-Time Paradox
Home · Interesting Math Problems · Problem 10 · Probability traps

1. The problem

Buses on your route come every 10 minutes on average. You arrive at the stop at a random moment. How long do you expect to wait?

Half the gap, so 5 minutes? That is right only if the buses run like clockwork.

2. The answer

Perfectly regular buses
5 min
Bunched: gaps of 2 and 18 min
8.2 min
Completely random buses
10 min
the full average gap!

You are more likely to arrive during a long gap than a short one, simply because long gaps take up more of the clock. So the gap you land in is longer than the average gap.

3. Working it out

A gap of length g catches your arrival with chance proportional to g, and if you land in it you wait g/2 on average. So

E[wait] = E[g²] / (2 E[g]) = (μ/2) · (1 + CV²)

where μ is the average gap and CV is the coefficient of variation (standard deviation ÷ mean) of the gaps. Regular buses: CV = 0, wait μ/2. Random (Poisson) buses: CV = 1, wait μ. Bunched 2 and 18: E[g²] = (4 + 324)/2 = 164, so wait = 164/20 = 8.2 min.

The same trap elsewhere

  • Class size: a college's average class may be 30, but the average student sits in a much bigger class, because big classes contain more students.
  • Friendship paradox: on average, your friends have more friends than you do.
  • Machine downtime: an inspector who arrives at random sees long repairs more often than short ones and overestimates the average repair time.

5. Fine print

  • This is also called the inspection paradox or length-biased sampling.
  • Bus bunching is real: a late bus picks up more passengers, slows down further, and the bus behind catches up. Timetable "headway control" fights exactly this.
