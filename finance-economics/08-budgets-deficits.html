Level 1 · For school students

A budget is a plan: how much will come in, and how much will go out.

1What is a budget?

A budget is a plan of expected income and spending for a period, usually one year.

Your family plans too: Amma and Appa's salaries come in; rent, food, school fees, electricity and savings go out. The Government of India does the same for the whole country, on a giant scale. Every year on 1 February, the Finance Minister presents the Union Budget in Parliament, and Parliament must approve it before the money can be spent.

SurplusIncome is more than spending. The extra can be saved or used to repay loans.
BalancedIncome equals spending.
DeficitSpending is more than income. The gap must be borrowed. India's government, like most, runs a deficit every year.

2Watch it happen: the borrowing snowball

A small country earns ₹100 a year but spends ₹110, so it borrows ₹10 each year at 8% interest. Interest is part of next year's spending. Press Play and watch the debt grow.

3Where the money comes from, and where it goes

⬇ Comes in
  • Taxes: income tax, GST, corporate tax, customs (Topic 06)
  • Non-tax income: dividends from the RBI and government companies, fees, spectrum auctions
  • Borrowing: to fill the gap
⬆ Goes out
  • Interest on past borrowing, the biggest single item
  • States' share of taxes and grants
  • Defence, police and pensions
  • Subsidies: food, fertiliser, cooking gas
  • Building things: roads, railways, ports, housing
  • Schemes: health, education, rural jobs

4Is borrowing bad?

It depends on what you borrow for. Think of two families:

🏠 Good borrowingA loan to build a house or pay for college. It creates something that pays back over many years. For a country: highways, power plants, railways and schools that help everyone earn more in future.
🎉 Risky borrowingA loan to pay for a big party or daily groceries. Nothing is left to repay it with. For a country: borrowing just to pay salaries and subsidies, year after year.

The animation shows the key point: if income grows fast enough, a country can carry some debt comfortably. If income doesn't grow, the interest snowball takes over.

5Why a country is different from a family

  • A country does not "retire". It can keep rolling over old loans by taking new ones, as long as lenders trust it.
  • Most of India's government debt is owed to Indians: banks, insurance companies, provident funds and savers.
  • Government spending is someone else's income. When the government builds a road, workers and suppliers earn money, which helps the economy grow.
  • But if a government borrows too much, lenders demand higher interest, and in the worst cases (Sri Lanka in 2022) a country can run out of money to pay its bills and imports.

Level 2 · For working people

How to read the Budget, the different deficits, how 2026-27 is going, why debt matters to your interest rates and taxes, and how other countries got into trouble.

1Anatomy of the Union Budget

Revenue account (day-to-day)Capital account (assets and liabilities)
ReceiptsTax (net of the states' share) and non-tax revenue (RBI dividend, fees, PSU dividends)Borrowings, loan recoveries, disinvestment (sale of government stakes)
ExpenditureInterest, salaries, pensions, subsidies, grants to states, schemesRoads, railways, defence equipment, loans to states and PSUs

The deficits

Fiscal deficit = Total expenditure − (Revenue receipts + non-debt capital receipts) → the government's total borrowing need Revenue deficit = Revenue expenditure − Revenue receipts → borrowing to fund day-to-day spending Primary deficit = Fiscal deficit − Interest payments → the deficit excluding the cost of past debt

The fiscal deficit is the headline number. A revenue deficit is the warning sign, because it means borrowing to pay running costs rather than to build assets. A primary deficit near zero means the government is borrowing mainly to pay interest on old debt.

3Mid-year check: April–September 2026

  • Deficit: ₹7.14 lakh crore, which is 42.1% of the full-year target, against 36.5% at the same point last year.
  • Spending: ₹25.5 lakh crore (47.7% of the budget). Capital spending was ₹6.55 lakh crore, already 53.6% of its annual budget: infrastructure spending was deliberately front-loaded.
  • Subsidies: major subsidies were up about 10%, and the food subsidy about 16%, as the energy and food price shock raised costs (Topic 01).
  • Receipts: ₹18.35 lakh crore (50.3%). Net tax receipts were only 44.5% of target, but non-tax revenue reached 72.2% thanks to a large RBI dividend.
  • What to watch: whether strong GST and direct-tax collections in October–November (Topic 06) close the gap, or whether spending will need trimming to hold 4.3%.

4Calculator: where is the debt heading?

Debt ratio next year ≈ debt ratio × (1 + interest rate) / (1 + nominal GDP growth) + primary deficit

The inputs are illustrative. The key is the gap between interest and growth: when nominal growth beats the interest rate, debt can fall even with a small primary deficit. Try setting growth below the interest rate.

5Why deficits matter to you

Interest ratesHeavy government borrowing competes with companies and home buyers for savings, which can push up bond yields and loan rates ("crowding out", Topic 02).
InflationBig deficits during a supply shock add to demand and can feed inflation, especially if they are financed by money creation (Topic 01).
Future taxesToday's borrowing is tomorrow's tax bill or spending cut, so debt is a transfer between generations.
Public servicesHigher interest bills squeeze spending on schools, hospitals and roads.
GrowthWell-chosen capital spending can raise growth and jobs, partly paying for itself. Borrowing for consumption usually does not.

6States, and the whole government

  • India's states together spend more than the Centre on health, education, police and agriculture. They run their own deficits, generally capped at around 3% of their GDP (with extra room for reforms).
  • General government (Centre plus states) debt is roughly 80% of GDP, high for an emerging economy, though mostly in rupees and held domestically.
  • Some state concerns: free-power and cash-transfer promises, loss-making power distribution companies, and off-budget borrowing through state-owned companies.

7The global picture

  • USA: runs deficits of around 6% of GDP even in good times, with federal debt held by the public near 100% of GDP. Rising interest costs are a growing political issue, and they help explain the high US bond yields (Topic 02).
  • Japan has gross public debt of well over 200% of GDP, sustainable so far because it is held mostly at home at very low interest rates.
  • Crises: Greece (2010), where debt held in a currency it could not print forced painful austerity; the UK (2022), where an unfunded "mini-budget" sparked a bond-market crash within days; Sri Lanka (2022), which defaulted after tax cuts, the loss of tourism income and dollar debt.
  • Credit ratings: S&P upgraded India to BBB in 2025, citing fiscal consolidation. Better ratings lower borrowing costs for the government and for Indian companies abroad.

Level 3 · Review for experts

Accounting, debt dynamics, fiscal rules and India's FRBM journey, multipliers and the composition of spending, transparency, and global debates.

1Accounting and financing

GFD = Revenue deficit + Capital expenditure − Non-debt capital receipts = Net market borrowing + Short-term borrowing (T-bills) + NSSF/small savings + External + Draw-down of cash
  • Effective revenue deficit excludes grants for creating capital assets, a better measure of "consumption borrowing".
  • Financing composition matters: dated securities lengthen maturity but raise rollover cost; small savings are sticky but costly and administered; T-bills are cheap but short.
  • Below-the-line items (loans to public enterprises, NSSF loans to FCI) historically hid deficits. Bringing food-subsidy financing back on budget (2020-21) improved transparency at the cost of a higher headline deficit.

2Debt dynamics and r − g

d_{t+1} = d_t · (1 + i) / (1 + g) + pd_t Δd ≈ (i − g)/(1 + g) · d + pd Debt-stabilising primary balance: pb* = d · (i − g)/(1 + g) (negative i − g ⇒ primary deficits can be sustained)
  • Blanchard (2019): when r < g persistently, debt has low fiscal costs. But the gap is uncertain and endogenous to the debt level; the post-2022 rise in global yields narrowed it.
  • For India, nominal growth of 10–11% against an effective interest rate of about 7–7.5% gives a strongly negative i − g. That is the main reason debt ratios fell after 2021 despite deficits. Low GDP-deflator inflation (Topic 03) erodes this advantage.

3Fiscal rules: India's FRBM journey

  • FRBM Act 2003: eliminate the revenue deficit and bring the fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP. Repeatedly paused: the 2008–09 stimulus, the 2020 pandemic (the fiscal deficit reached about 9.2% in 2020-21) and escape clauses.
  • N.K. Singh Committee (2017): proposed debt as the primary anchor (60% for general government, 40% for the Centre) with the fiscal deficit as the operating target. It was partly adopted through FRBM amendments in 2018.
  • 2021–2026 glide path: the fiscal deficit below 4.5% by 2025-26 was achieved (4.4%). From 2026-27, the Centre has switched to a debt-to-GDP anchor of 50% ± 1 by 2030-31, with annual deficits set to keep debt on that path.
  • Design trade-offs: debt anchors are more robust to cycles but slower to bind; deficit targets are transparent but procyclical. Credibility depends on escape-clause discipline and independent fiscal councils, which India lacks at the Union level.

4Multipliers and the composition of spending

Indian estimates (for example, NIPFP's Bose and Bhanumurthy, 2015) put the capital-expenditure multiplier around 2.5, much higher than for revenue spending (about 1) or transfers. That supports India's capex-led consolidation strategy, though multipliers depend on slack, monetary accommodation and the quality of execution.

  • Crowding out vs crowding in: public capex can crowd in private investment through better infrastructure, or crowd it out through higher yields. The evidence for India is mixed and depends on the state of the cycle.
  • Ricardian equivalence (Barro, 1974) fails empirically where households are credit-constrained, as in much of India, which strengthens the effects of transfers.
  • Fiscal dominance: large deficits can force the central bank to accommodate them. India's inflation-targeting framework (Topic 01) and the ban on direct RBI financing (outside emergencies) are the safeguards.

5Transparency and quality

  • Off-budget liabilities: borrowing by state power utilities, extra-budgetary resources raised by public sector enterprises, and guarantees, which add to hidden contingent liabilities.
  • Accrual vs cash accounting: cash-based budgets hide pension liabilities and deferred maintenance. The move to the New Pension Scheme (now the Unified Pension Scheme option) aims to contain long-run pension costs.
  • Credibility of estimates: compare Budget Estimates, Revised Estimates and actuals. The 2025-26 deficit came in at 97.5% of the revised estimate, a sign of improved forecasting compared with the 2010s.

6Global debates

  • Sovereign debt limits: Reinhart–Rogoff's "90% threshold" was contested after spreadsheet errors were found. The current view is that safe debt levels depend on interest rates, growth, currency and the investor base, not on a single number.
  • MMT argues that currency issuers face inflation limits, not solvency limits. Critics point to the inflation of 2021–23 and to the importance of central-bank independence.
  • Bond vigilantes: the UK's 2022 gilt crisis and the 2025–26 rise in global term premia show that markets can discipline even advanced economies.
  • Emerging-market sovereign defaults (Sri Lanka, Zambia, Ghana) and the G20 Common Framework show how slowly debt restructuring proceeds when Chinese, bondholder and multilateral creditors must all agree.

7Review checklist: open questions

  1. Is 50% ± 1 for the Centre by 2030-31 ambitious enough, given general government debt near 80%?
  2. How robust is India's negative i − g if real growth slows or the deflator stays low?
  3. Should India set up an independent fiscal council to cost policies and monitor the debt anchor?
  4. How large are state-level contingent liabilities (power distribution companies, guarantees), and should they count towards the anchor?
  5. What is the right mix of capex and social spending in health and education for long-run growth?
  6. How dependent are deficit outcomes on RBI dividends, and is that a sustainable source?
