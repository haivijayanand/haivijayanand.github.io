FFT and Spectrum Analysis: Sampling, Resolution, Windows and Averaging

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
FFTSignal processingVibrationData acquisition

The fast Fourier transform turns a time signal into a spectrum, so that each machine fault appears at its own frequency. Getting a trustworthy spectrum depends on a few set-up choices: how fast to sample, how long to record, which window to apply and how many averages to take. These choices trade frequency resolution against time and amplitude accuracy.

Key takeaways

  • Sample at least 2.56 × the highest frequency of interest (Fmax) with an anti-alias filter; Nyquist is Fs/2.
  • Resolution Δf = Fs/N = 1/T. Finer resolution needs a longer record: 0.1 Hz needs 10 s of data.
  • Lines of resolution = N/2.56. A 3,200-line spectrum to 1 kHz has Δf = 0.3125 Hz.
  • Use a Hanning window for general vibration, flat-top for accurate amplitudes, rectangular only for transients or order-tracked signals.
  • Averaging reduces random noise; overlap (50–67 %) shortens measurement time without losing data.

1. Sampling and aliasing

Nyquist frequency f_N = Fs / 2 Practical rule Fs = 2.56 · Fmax (anti-alias filter roll-off) Record length T = N / Fs Resolution Δf = 1 / T = Fs / N Lines L = N / 2.56 (= Fmax / Δf)

A frequency above Fs/2 that reaches the digitiser folds back and appears as a false lower frequency (aliasing). It cannot be removed afterwards, so an analogue anti-alias filter must act before the converter. Delta-sigma converters include this filter automatically.

2. Window functions

The FFT assumes the record repeats forever. If the signal does not complete a whole number of cycles, the ends do not join and energy leaks into neighbouring lines. A window tapers the ends to reduce leakage at the cost of a wider peak.

3. Averaging and overlap

  • Linear (RMS) averaging of spectra reduces the variance of random noise; 4–16 averages is common.
  • Peak hold keeps the maximum in each line: useful for run-ups and intermittent events.
  • Time-synchronous averaging (triggered by a tachometer) removes everything not locked to shaft speed, isolating gear and shaft signals.
  • Overlap of 50–67 % with Hanning uses data efficiently; total time ≈ T·[1 + (n−1)(1 − overlap)].
