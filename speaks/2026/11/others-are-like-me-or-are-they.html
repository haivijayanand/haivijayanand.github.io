Others Are Like Me. Or Are They?

5 Nov 20264 min readRelationshipSelf-Growth

We share the needs that make us human. We differ in the ways we experience them.

I eat when I am hungry. Others will too.

I sleep when I am tired. Others will too.

I think in a certain way. Perhaps others think the same way.

Maybe they do. Maybe they do not.

There is something natural about understanding the world through ourselves. Our own life is the experience we know most closely. We know what hunger feels like, what keeps us awake, what hurts our pride and what brings us happiness. So, when we try to understand another person, we begin with what we already know.

“If I were in that situation, this is what I would do.”

It is a useful beginning. The difficulty comes when we believe it is the only reasonable response.

The Human Being We Recognise in Each Other

Every person has needs that should surprise no one.

People need food and rest. They want some measure of safety, affection, dignity and belonging. They can feel afraid, become jealous, seek appreciation, make mistakes and hope for another chance.

Yet, we sometimes behave as though these ordinary human needs are weaknesses when they appear in someone else.

We need a break, but wonder why a colleague is tired.

We want our efforts recognised, but dismiss another person’s wish for appreciation as attention seeking.

We explain our irritation through the difficult day we have had, while explaining someone else’s irritation through their character.

Our own behaviour comes with a story. Other people’s behaviour often receives only a verdict.

Remembering that others have an inner life as full as our own can soften that verdict.

The Same Need Can Take Different Forms

We all eat, but we do not all enjoy the same food. We all need sleep, but our routines and responsibilities differ. We all think, but we do not arrive at understanding through identical paths.

One person speaks to discover what they think. Another needs silence before they can speak clearly.

One feels cared for when someone offers advice. Another feels cared for when someone listens without immediately offering a solution.

One person enjoys a crowded celebration. Another leaves exhausted, despite having affection for everyone there.

These differences need not become a contest over whose way is correct.

The trouble begins with statements such as:

“I managed it. Why can’t you?”

“That never bothers me. Why are you so sensitive?”

“I would have understood without being told.”

Each statement takes a personal experience and turns it into a standard for everyone.

Our experience can help us understand another person. It cannot tell us everything about them.

Understanding Requires a Little Curiosity

Suppose a colleague is unusually quiet. We might assume that they are uninterested or uncooperative. Perhaps they are. Perhaps they are worried about something at home, uncertain about the task, or simply taking time to think.

We do not have to invent an excuse for them. We can ask.

The same applies within a family. A child’s reluctance may need a conversation before it needs a lecture. A spouse’s silence may deserve attention before interpretation. An older person repeating a concern may be looking for reassurance that the concern has been heard.

Understanding becomes easier when we leave room for information we do not yet have.

A useful question is: “What am I assuming because this is how I would feel?”

Acceptance Still Leaves Room for Responsibility

Recognising ordinary human traits does not mean approving every action.

Anger is understandable; hurting someone still requires accountability. Fear deserves compassion; it may also need to be faced. Everyone makes mistakes; everyone can be expected to learn from them.

We can acknowledge a feeling and still discuss the behaviour that followed it. That gives people room to improve without making them feel ashamed merely for being human.

We deserve that same fairness ourselves. Another person’s appetite, stamina, confidence or pace need not become the measure of our worth.

A Thought to Carry into Everyday Life

“How I eat, others will. How I sleep, others will. How I think, others will.”

There is a part of that thought worth keeping: other people have needs and feelings that I can recognise through my own.

There is also a part worth questioning: they may experience those needs and feelings differently.

Perhaps a kinder way to move through life is to remember both.

“There is enough of me in others to care about them, and enough that is different to keep listening.”
