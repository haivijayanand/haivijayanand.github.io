Office communication: from the @ sign to Teams

The memo, the telex and the phone call were the office's nervous system for a century. Then in 1971 an engineer put an @ sign between a user name and a computer name, and electronic mail began. Fifty years later, office communication is a mix of e-mail, calendars, chat, video meetings and shared documents — and a constant fight against spam and fake senders.

02How e-mail grew

1965 — MAIL on CTSS. Users of MIT's time-sharing computer could leave messages for each other on the same machine.
1971 — The @ sign. Ray Tomlinson at BBN sent a message between two computers on the ARPANET, using user@host to name the recipient.
1982 — SMTP and RFC 822. The Simple Mail Transfer Protocol and message format defined the e-mail we still use. POP (1984) and IMAP (1986) let users fetch mail from a server.
1989 — Lotus Notes. Ray Ozzie's groupware combined e-mail, shared databases and workflow; bought by IBM in 1995 and sold to HCL in 2019 (now HCL Notes/Domino).
1996 — Hotmail. Founded by Sabeer Bhatia and Jack Smith; free web e-mail anyone could use. Microsoft bought it in 1997; it became Outlook.com in 2013.
1996–97 — Exchange and Outlook. Microsoft's mail server and client became the corporate standard, joined by calendars, contacts and tasks.
2004 — Gmail. Fast search, threaded conversations and 1 GB of storage when others offered a few megabytes.
2010s — Cloud office. Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 moved organisations' mail to the cloud.

04India: government e-mail and messaging

  • NIC e-mail. For decades the National Informatics Centre ran e-mail for central government on gov.in and nic.in addresses.
  • Move to Zoho. After a 2023 tender, the platform was migrated to Zoho's Indian-hosted e-mail and office suite, with NIC keeping control of the domains and the data. The government told Parliament in December 2025 that about 12.68 lakh accounts had been moved.
  • Sandes. NIC's own instant-messaging app for government users, an alternative to WhatsApp for official chat.
  • Arattai. Zoho's consumer messaging app saw a surge of downloads in September–October 2025; it is a separate product, not part of the government e-mail service.
Government employees are bound by the Government of India's e-mail policy, which requires official communication to use the official e-mail service rather than private accounts.

05Try it: how a receiving server checks the sender

SMTP has no built-in proof of who sent a message — anyone can write any "From:" address. Three DNS-based checks were added: SPF (2006, which servers may send for a domain), DKIM (2007, a digital signature on the message) and DMARC (2012, the domain owner's policy, which also requires the checked domain to match the visible From address). Pick a scenario or edit the values.


    

    
Simplified: real checks also handle "include:", "a" and "mx" mechanisms, DNS lookups, subdomain policies and ARC for forwarders. Domains and addresses use reserved example ranges.

09Lessons for engineers

  
    
    
  1. E-mail is a record. Write test approvals and decisions as if they will be read in an audit.
    2. 
    
  2. Chat is for coordination, documents are for decisions. Summarise chat conclusions in a minute or report.
    3. 
    
  3. Check the sender, not the display name. Look at the real address, and treat urgent payment or password requests with suspicion.
    4. 
    
  4. Use official channels for official data. Personal WhatsApp groups and free cloud accounts are not records systems.
