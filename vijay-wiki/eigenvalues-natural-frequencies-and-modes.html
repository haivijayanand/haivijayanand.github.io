Eigenvalues and Mode Shapes: Natural Frequencies of Multi-Degree-of-Freedom Systems

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
EigenvaluesModal analysisLinear algebraVibration

A structure with many masses and springs has as many natural frequencies as degrees of freedom, each with its own pattern of motion called a mode shape. Finding them is an eigenvalue problem: the eigenvalues are the squared natural frequencies and the eigenvectors are the mode shapes. Finite-element modal analysis does exactly this, for thousands of degrees of freedom.

Key takeaways

  • Free vibration M·ẍ + K·x = 0 with x = φ·sin ωt gives (K − ω²M)·φ = 0, an eigenvalue problem.
  • Natural frequencies come from det(K − ω²M) = 0; mode shapes φ from substituting each ω² back.
  • Modes are orthogonal with respect to M and K, so any motion is a sum of independent modal responses.
  • The lowest mode usually has all parts moving in phase; higher modes have nodes and opposite motion.
  • A tuned absorber (small mass and spring tuned to a troublesome frequency) splits one resonance into two away from the forcing frequency.

1. The eigenvalue problem

M·ẍ + K·x = 0, x = φ·sin(ωt) ⇒ (K − ω²·M)·φ = 0 det(K − λ·M) = 0, λ = ω² For a general matrix A: A·v = λ·v, det(A − λI) = 0

For a 2×2 matrix A = [[a, b], [c, d]]: λ² − (a + d)·λ + (ad − bc) = 0, so λ = (tr ± √(tr² − 4·det))/2. The sum of eigenvalues equals the trace and their product equals the determinant.

3. Useful properties

  • Orthogonality: φᵢᵀ·M·φⱼ = 0 and φᵢᵀ·K·φⱼ = 0 for i ≠ j. Using modal coordinates decouples the equations into independent single-degree-of-freedom systems.
  • Rayleigh quotient: ω² ≈ (φᵀKφ)/(φᵀMφ) for any guessed shape; it gives an upper bound on the lowest frequency and is very accurate with a reasonable guess.
  • Rotors: natural frequencies change with speed (gyroscopic effects); a Campbell diagram plots them against speed to find critical speeds where they cross excitation orders.
  • Experimental modal analysis: impact or shaker tests measure frequency response functions; curve fitting extracts frequencies, damping and shapes to validate finite-element models.

4. Tuned vibration absorber

Adding a small mass ma on a spring ka with √(ka/ma) equal to the forcing frequency makes the main mass stand still at that frequency: the absorber moves instead. The original resonance splits into two peaks either side; damping in the absorber broadens its useful range.
