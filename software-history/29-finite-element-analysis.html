Finite element analysis: from Boeing wings to ANSYS

Will this bracket crack? How hot will this casing get? What are this gearbox housing's natural frequencies? Hand formulas cover only simple shapes. The finite element method cuts a complex part into thousands of small pieces with simple behaviour and solves them together — a job that only computers can do. FEA grew up with the computer, from aircraft companies and NASA to every engineer's desktop.

01The requirement: analyse shapes that formulas cannot

Beam, plate and shell formulas work for idealised shapes. Real parts — a swept aircraft wing, a cast housing, a nuclear pressure vessel — needed either rough approximations, large safety factors or expensive physical tests. In the 1940s mathematicians (Hrennikoff in 1941, Richard Courant in 1943) suggested splitting a continuum into small pieces. In 1956 M. J. Turner, R. W. Clough, H. C. Martin and L. J. Topp at Boeing published a method to compute the stiffness of delta wings from small triangular panels. John Argyris in Germany and the UK developed matrix methods at the same time. In 1960 Clough coined the name "finite element method"; O. C. Zienkiewicz's 1967 textbook spread it worldwide.

02How FEA works, in four steps

1. Pre-processing — geometry from CAD (Episode 30), simplified; a mesh of elements (beams, shells, tetrahedra, hexahedra); material properties; loads and supports.
2. Element equations — each element's stiffness matrix links its node forces to node displacements.
3. Assembly and solution — element matrices are added into one large global system K u = F, often millions of equations, solved by sparse direct or iterative solvers (Episode 27's linear algebra on a huge scale).
4. Post-processing — stresses, strains and mode shapes computed from displacements and shown as colour contour plots.

03Try it: a tapered bar, element by element

A steel bar fixed at the left, tapering from area A₁ to A₂, pulled by force P at the right. Each element is a uniform bar with stiffness k = EA/L. Increase the number of elements and watch the answer approach the exact solution — the essence of mesh convergence.

L = 1 m, A₂ = 100 mm², E = 200 GPa, P = 10 kN

07Trusting the results: verification and validation

  
    
    
  • Mesh convergence. Refine until the answer stops changing — as in the demo above. Stresses converge more slowly than displacements.
    • 
    
  • Hand check. Compare with a simple formula (beam, pressure vessel) before believing a contour plot.
    • 
    
  • Singularities. Sharp corners and point loads give stresses that keep rising with refinement; they are not real.
    • 
    
  • Validation. Compare with strain-gauge and modal test results (Episode 28) — the most convincing evidence.
    • 
    
  • Standards. NAFEMS benchmarks and industry codes (ASME, aerospace standards) define acceptable practice.
    •

09Lessons for engineers

  
    
    
  1. Garbage in, colourful garbage out. Boundary conditions decide the answer more than the solver does.
    2. 
    
  2. Know the element. Linear tetrahedra are too stiff in bending; use quadratic elements or more of them.
    3. 
    
  3. Report the convergence. A result without mesh study and hand check is an opinion.
    4. 
    
  4. Test and analysis together. Modal tests on the rig and FEA of the housing should agree — or tell you which one is wrong.
    5.
