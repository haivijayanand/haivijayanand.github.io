Convert letter notes from a sheet

Many piano sheets give bare letters with no octave numbers and no rhythm — FFF F#F#F# C#D# and so on. Paste those lines here and they become playable tokens. Sheets that already write the octave (D4 G4 A#3) are taken exactly as written, and commas are treated as spaces. Where words and notes share a line, the capitals are read as notes and the rest as words; a whole page pasted as one block is split at the wide gaps between phrases. Write a flat as a small b: Ab is A flat, AB is two notes.

Load a songbook file (.json)

A songbook holds titles, note letters and the words that go with them. Once loaded, its songs join the search box above — by title, and by any word in the text — and the lines appear below so you can play them one at a time. The file is read on your machine and nothing leaves it; reloading the page clears it.

Three ways to make a sound

Click or tap a key. Hold the mouse down and slide sideways for a glissando. On a touch screen, drag along the keys.

Or type. The two rows of your computer keyboard sit under two octaves of the piano, and the letter is printed on each key:

KeysWhat they play

The black keys are the sharps and flats, and they sit where your fingers expect: the letter keys between them are the white notes.

Eight instruments

The row of buttons above the keyboard changes the voice. Everything else — the songs, your recordings, the notes you write — plays through whichever one is selected, so the same tune can be a lullaby on the music box or a hymn on the organ. Picking one sounds middle C so you can hear the change.

InstrumentCharacter
Grand pianoStruck string: bright attack, long ring in the bass
Electric pianoSofter, with a bell-like edge that fades before the body of the note
Music boxMetal comb — very short attack, glassy, no warmth in the low notes
MarimbaWooden bar and mallet: dry, quick, wants fast tunes
Acoustic guitarA real plucked string, computed as a vibrating loop rather than stacked tones
Church organHolds forever with no decay at all, so long notes stay loud
FluteBreathy, gentle attack, vibrato that eases in on held notes
StringsSlow swell of detuned bows — try it on Silent Night

The first five are struck or plucked: they ring and fade on their own, and lifting the key only cuts the tail short. The last three are blown or bowed, so a note lasts exactly as long as you hold the key, and written lengths matter far more. The sustain pedal changes nothing on those three, because there is nothing left ringing to hold.

The wheel

The Wheel tab above the keyboard is the same instrument in a circle. One full turn is one octave, so the twelve wedges are the twelve notes and the ring repeats the way pitch itself does. Press the mouse button anywhere on the ring and keep it down: sliding round plays note after note, and the sound follows your hand without ever running off the edge. Stray into the middle or past the rim and the note you are on keeps sounding — it only changes when you reach a different wedge, and only stops when you let the button go. On a touch screen each finger gets its own note, so you can hold a chord.

The minus and plus buttons move the whole ring an octave at a time, between octave 3 and octave 5. The name of whatever is sounding appears in the middle, where your eye already is.

Chromatic puts the notes round in the order they sit on the keyboard, so a sweep is a true glissando — dramatic, and a little harsh. Fifths reorders them C G D A E B and so on, which places consonant notes side by side: any two neighbours agree with each other, so wandering around the ring sounds musical whatever you do. That one is worth handing to a child.

Whichever view you use, the other lights up too when a song plays — a good way to see where a note on the wheel lives on the keyboard.

What the highlights tell you

A key glows while its note is sounding, the name of the note appears under the keyboard, and the matching chip in the strip below the notes box turns gold. Turn the glow off with the highlight checkbox if it distracts; the name under the keyboard stays either way.

Volume and tempo

Volume is the overall loudness. Tempo only affects written notes and playback, in beats per minute — 60 BPM means one beat per second, 120 BPM means two. Your live playing is never quantised to it.

Letters, not numbers

Music uses seven letters, A B C D E F G, and then starts again at A. Those seven are the white keys. Count up from any C and you get C D E F G A B, then the next C.

Sharps and flats

The black keys sit between white keys and take the name of a neighbour with a sign attached:

  • # means sharp — one key to the right. C# is the black key just above C.
  • b means flat — one key to the left. Db is that same black key, named from above.

So C# and Db are one and the same key. Write whichever you prefer; this piano accepts both. E and B have no black key above them, which is why the black keys come in groups of two and three.

The octave number

The same letter repeats all along the keyboard, so a number says which one you mean. C4 is middle C. C3 is an octave lower, C5 an octave higher — twice the frequency each time you go up one.

The number changes at every C, not at A. That catches people out: B3 and C4 are neighbours, one key apart.

NameMeaning
C4Middle C, the fourth C on a full piano
A4The A above middle C — the 440 Hz tuning note
F#3The black key above F, one octave below middle
Gb3The very same key, spelled from G downward
CNo number given, so octave 4 is assumed

This keyboard runs from C3 to C6, three octaves. Anything you write outside that range still plays; you just won't see a key light up for it.

One token per note

Separate notes with spaces. Each token is a note name, optionally followed by a colon and a length in beats. Leave the length off and the note gets one beat.

TokenPlays
C4Middle C for one beat
G4:2G above middle C, held two beats
E4:0.5Half a beat — a quick note
C4+E4+G4A chord: all three together
-A rest — one beat of silence
-:2Two beats of silence
|A bar line. The player ignores it; it's there to keep long tunes readable

How long is a beat?

It depends on the tempo slider. At 120 BPM a beat is half a second, so C4:2 lasts one second. Change the tempo and every length changes with it, which is how one line of notes can be a lullaby or a march.

Common lengths: :4 a long held note, :2 half as long, :1 the default, :0.5 a running note, :0.25 very quick. Triplets work too — three notes of :0.33 fill one beat.

A worked example

E4 D4 C4 D4 E4 E4 E4:2 is the opening of Mary Had a Little Lamb: six notes of one beat, then a longer one to end the phrase.

Sheets with bare letters

A lot of piano sheets circulating online give pitch letters only — no octave numbers, no rhythm, lyrics interleaved with the notes. The Paste & convert tab at the top of this column turns that shape into playable tokens: paste the lines, choose which octave to start in and how long each note should be, and it writes the result into the notes box. Lines containing words are recognised as words and skipped, so you can paste a whole page.

Two things it has to guess, and how it guesses them. Octave: with "keep steps small" ticked, each note goes wherever it lands closest to the one before, which is what melodies usually do, and every line restarts in your chosen octave so a long sheet cannot creep away. Untick it and every note goes into the one octave. Rhythm: it cannot be guessed at all, so every note gets the same length and you fix the long ones by hand — that is what the :beats suffix is for, and the score strip makes it quick to find them.

There is also a transpose box, for sheets written a semitone or two away from where you want to play.

Pasting is enough — the panel decodes as soon as text lands in it, so you can copy a page from wherever you keep your sheets and drop it straight in. Where the paste has words above its note lines, those lines appear underneath the panel as a sheet: words on top, notes below, and clicking any line plays that phrase alone.

Some sheets put the words and the notes on the same line, and some arrive as one long block with wide gaps instead of line breaks. Both are handled: a block is split at the gaps, and on a mixed line the capitals are taken as notes and everything else as words. That last part is a guess based on case, and it can misfire — a syllable written in capitals that happens to be spelled from the letters A to G will be read as notes. Check the score strip against the sheet before trusting a long paste.

If something is wrong

The line under the buttons says what it found — an unknown note name or a bad length, with the token quoted. The strip of chips only appears while the whole line reads correctly, so it doubles as a check while you type.

Everything is counted in semitones

A semitone is the smallest step here: the very next key along, black or white. Two semitones make a whole tone. Every scale below is just a particular pattern of those two steps, and the pattern is what you recognise, not the starting note.

All twelve semitones in a row
Nothing but whole tones — no home, no rest

The major scale

Seven notes, then the eighth is the first one again an octave up. Counting in semitones the steps are 2 2 1 2 2 2 1 — two whole tones, a semitone, three whole tones, a semitone. Start that pattern on C and it lands on the white keys only, which is why C major is where everyone begins.

ScaleNotes
C majorC D E F G A B C — no black keys
G majorSame pattern from G, so F must be sharpened
F majorFrom F, and now B has to be flattened
D majorFrom D: two sharps, F# and C#

That is the whole business of keys: one shape, moved to a different starting note, needing whatever black keys keep the pattern intact. Nursery songs live almost entirely in these scales, which is why they sound settled and cheerful.

Minor scales

The natural minor changes the pattern to 2 1 2 2 1 2 2. The third note is now closer to the first, and that one semitone is most of the difference between bright and heavy.

ScaleNotes
A natural minorA B C D E F G A — again all white keys
A harmonic minorRaise the seventh to G#: the leap up to A gains a pull, and the gap before it sounds eastern
A melodic minorRaise the sixth as well going up, to smooth that gap out
C minorThe minor pattern from C: E, A and B all flattened

C major and A minor use exactly the same seven notes — they only differ in which note feels like home. Play them one after the other and listen for the change of centre, not of notes:

The third decides it

Take the first, third and fifth notes of a scale and you have a chord. Everything hangs on that third note: four semitones above the root gives major, three gives minor. One key's difference turns the same chord over.

Moods to try

Nothing here is a rule — these are the habits listeners have learned. Change the instrument as well as the notes: the same sad line is grief on strings and merely thoughtful on marimba.

Two shortcuts worth keeping. Any notes from the calm pentatonic set can be played in any order and still agree with each other — set the wheel to Fifths, stay on C D E G A, and a child cannot make a wrong sound. And the Fifths ordering on the wheel is this same theory drawn as a circle: neighbours there are the notes that share the most scales, which is exactly why they sound agreeable together.

The seven swaras

South Indian practice names the notes Sa Ri Ga Ma Pa Da Ni instead of C D E F G A B. The important difference is that Sa is not a fixed key — it is wherever you decide to start, and everything else is measured from it. Here Sa sits on C4 so that the exercises land under one hand, but move Sa to D and every exercise moves with it.

Ri, Ga, Da and Ni each come in more than one position, and which ones you pick is what makes one raga differ from another.

Mayamalavagowla, and why beginners start there

Those seven make the raga Mayamalavagowla. It is the traditional starting point because it is symmetrical — a semitone step at the bottom of each half, then a wider gap — so the hand learns an even shape, and because its wide intervals make sloppy tuning obvious. On this keyboard it uses two black keys, Db and Ab.

The practice ladder

Work down this list in order. Each step is the same seven notes in a harder pattern, and each is meant to be played slowly and evenly long before it is played fast. Start at 60 on the tempo slider, not the tempo given here.

All three are in the song list too — search varisai or carnatic — so you can load one, slow the tempo, and use the score strip to follow along. Try them on the flute voice: a held instrument punishes uneven timing in a way the piano hides.

Ragas as scales you can hear

A raga is far more than a scale — it carries characteristic phrases, ornaments and a sense of direction that no keyboard can show. But the scale is where it starts, and hearing them side by side tells you a great deal about why one melody feels festive and another grave.

RagaNotes from Sa
MohanamFive notes, no semitones — the same set as the major pentatonic
ShankarabharanamNote for note the Western major scale
KalyaniMajor with a raised Ma — the Lydian sound, bright and expectant
KeeravaniLowered Ga and Da with a raised Ni — the harmonic minor
HindolamFive notes with no Ri and no Pa — grave, and hard to make sound wrong
MadhyamavatiFive notes, traditionally sung at the close of a concert

Mohanam is worth dwelling on: five notes, no semitone anywhere, so anything you play within it agrees with itself. Set the wheel to Fifths, keep to C D E G A, and a beginner can improvise for an hour without a wrong note. A great many Tamil melodies, film and folk alike, sit on exactly those five.

Moving Sa

To practise from a different Sa, rewrite the exercise a step higher — Sa on D means Ri becomes Eb, Ga becomes F#, and so on. The pattern of gaps is what stays the same. This is the same idea as the key signatures in the Scales tab, arrived at from the other direction.

Recording what you play

Press Record, play, press it again to stop. The take keeps the exact moment you struck each key and how long you held it, so playing it back sounds like you rather than a grid.

Send to notes box turns a take into written notes. It rounds each note to the nearest quarter beat at the current tempo, adds rests for the gaps, and groups notes struck together into chords — so set the tempo before you convert, and expect the written version to be tidier than the played one.

The song list

The search box above the notes offers forty-one entries: traditional tunes, and the Carnatic drills and raga scales from the practice tab. Type any part of a title, or a word about it: try star, sleeping, christmas, farm, beethoven, varisai or raga. Picking one writes its notes into the box, sets the tempo it was written for, and plays it.

Nothing is locked. Edit a loaded song, change its tempo, add a chord, and play it again. Clicking any chip in the strip plays just that note or chord, which is the quickest way to check one spot.

Songbook files

The Paste & convert tab also loads a .json songbook: titles, note letters and the words that go with them. Its songs join the same search box as the built-in tunes, findable by title or by any word in the text, and the lines appear underneath the notes so you can play one at a time while learning a phrase. Everything is read on your own machine and nothing is stored — reloading the page clears it.

The file looks like this, and is easy enough to write by hand for tunes you work out yourself:

{ "songs": [ { "title": "…",
  "lines": [ { "text": "…", "letters": "CCGG AAG" } ] } ] }

Only title and lines are needed, and within a line only letters. Optional extras are scale, transpose and a section label per line. The letters are run through the converter settings above, so the octave, note length and transpose you set there apply — change them and load the file again to redo the guesses.

Practice view

The Practice button beside Play clears the guide and the converter out of the way, leaving the keyboard across the full width with the notes and the sheet beneath it. While a song plays, the line being sung lights up and scrolls itself into view, so you can follow a page without touching anything. Press it again to bring the rest back.

Next to it, Loop repeats whatever is playing with one beat between passes, until you press Stop — for a written line, a loaded song or a recorded take alike. Tick or untick it while a song is running: switching it on carries straight into the next pass, switching it off lets the current one finish and stops there. A single phrase on a loop, played slowly, is how a hard bar becomes an easy one.

Practice view adds one thing the normal view does not have: the key coming next is shown in a cool blue while the key sounding now is gold. The blue appears a note ahead, including before the first note of a phrase, so a hand can be moving to the right place rather than chasing what has already been played. Slow the tempo until the blue arrives early enough to act on. It follows the highlight checkbox, so turning highlighting off removes both.

With nothing playing, that blue key waits for you. Whatever is in the notes box is marked from its first note, and it moves on only when you play the note it is showing — at your own pace, with no clock running. A wrong note is ignored rather than marked wrong, so you can hunt for the right one; a chord waits until every note of it is down, in whatever order you find them. The chip in the strip and the line in the sheet follow along, and reaching the end starts you again at the beginning. Press Play and the song takes the marks back; press Stop and they return to your hands.

Where the sound comes from

No audio is downloaded. Every note is built as it plays: most instruments stack a handful of harmonics under an envelope of their own, and the guitar models a real string as a loop of vibration that loses a little energy each time round. Save this file anywhere and it works with no network at all.
