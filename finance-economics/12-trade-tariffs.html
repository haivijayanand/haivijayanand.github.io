Level 1 · For school students

Trade means swapping. Countries swap for the same reason friends do: both end up better off.

1What is trade?

Trade is buying and selling between people, and between countries. Selling to other countries is called exporting; buying from them is importing.

Look around your home: the phone may be assembled in India from parts made in Korea and Taiwan; the cooking oil may come from Indonesia; the petrol from the Gulf. And India sells medicines to Africa, software to America, rice to the Middle East and cars to Europe. No country makes everything it needs.

3What India sells and buys

📦 India exportsSoftware and IT services, medicines, mobile phones and electronics, petroleum products, engineering goods, gems and jewellery, rice, textiles and clothes, cars
🚢 India importsCrude oil and gas, gold and silver, electronic parts, machinery, edible oils, fertilisers, coal
⚖️ The balanceIndia usually buys more goods than it sells (a trade deficit), mostly because of oil and gold. But it sells far more services than it buys, which covers much of the gap.

4What is a tariff?

A tariff (or import duty) is a tax on goods coming into a country.
  • If India puts a 20% tariff on imported toys, a ₹100 toy costs ₹120 here. Indian toy makers can now compete more easily, but children's families pay more.
  • When the USA put a 50% tariff on many Indian goods in 2025, American shops had to pay much more for Indian shirts and shrimps, so they bought less from India, and Indian exporters suffered.
  • Tariffs protect some local jobs but make things costlier for everyone who buys them, and other countries may hit back with their own tariffs: a trade war.

5Trade deals: making swapping easier

Countries sign free trade agreements (FTAs) to lower tariffs on each other's goods. In 2026, India finished a big deal with the European Union (27 countries), and its deal with the United Kingdom came into force. More Indian clothes, shoes, gems and food can now reach those shops at lower tax, and some European and British goods (like cars and whisky) become cheaper in India.

Exports to the US fell sharply during the 50% months, then recovered. Labour-intensive sectors (textiles, gems and jewellery, shrimp, leather) were hit hardest; smartphones and pharmaceuticals were largely spared.

3India's new trade deals

  • India–EU FTA (concluded 27 January 2026): the most comprehensive trade pact India has signed, covering goods, services and rules. It awaits ratification. The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (in its definitive phase since 1 January 2026) can still add carbon costs to Indian steel and aluminium.
  • India–UK CETA (in force from 15 July 2026): duty-free access for most Indian exports such as textiles, leather, gems and marine products, with phased Indian tariff cuts on cars, whisky and others.
  • Earlier deals: UAE (2022), Australia (2022), EFTA (2024), plus Oman and New Zealand (2025). India opted out of the Asian RCEP pact in 2019, fearing Chinese imports.
  • Rules of origin matter: to get the lower tariff, a product must contain enough local value. Exporters must keep certificates of origin and supplier records.

5Who really pays a tariff?

The importing country's buyersStudies of the 2018–19 and 2025 US tariffs found that most of the cost was passed on to US importers and consumers through higher prices.
ExportersThey lose orders, or cut prices to keep customers, absorbing part of the tariff. Indian shrimp and textile exporters did both in 2025.
WorkersJob losses are concentrated in export clusters such as Tiruppur (knitwear), Surat (diamonds) and Andhra Pradesh (shrimp). Governments responded with export credit and interest relief.
India's own tariffsIndian duties on electronics parts, edible oils or solar cells raise costs for Indian manufacturers and consumers too: an import tariff is a tax on your own residents.

6Trade and the country

  • Jobs: labour-intensive exports (garments, leather, toys, food processing) create the most jobs per rupee (Topic 11).
  • Global value chains: "China+1" and the PLI schemes moved iPhone and electronics assembly to India. Deeper participation means importing parts to export finished goods, so high input tariffs hurt.
  • Prices and inflation: imports of oil, edible oils and pulses keep prices in check; export bans on food (Topic 10) shield consumers but hurt farmers.
  • The rupee: exports earn the dollars that pay for oil and gold (Topic 05).

7The global picture

  • From globalisation to fragmentation: since 2018, and sharply since 2025, the world has shifted towards tariffs, "friend-shoring", export controls on chips and critical minerals, and security-driven trade policy.
  • The WTO's dispute settlement has been paralysed since 2019, when its Appellate Body lost its quorum, so trade rules are enforced less and bilateral deals matter more.
  • Shipping shocks: Red Sea and Hormuz disruptions (2024–26) raised freight costs and insurance for Indian trade to Europe and the Gulf.

Level 3 · Review for experts

Why trade, the welfare economics of protection, effective protection, gravity, new trade theory and firms, the political economy of trade, and the rules-based system under strain.

1Why countries trade

  • Ricardo (1817): comparative advantage. Gains arise from differences in opportunity costs, not absolute productivity. The kites-and-bags example: Asha's opportunity cost of a bag is 2 kites, Bala's is 1. Any terms of trade between 1 and 2 kites per bag benefit both.
  • Heckscher–Ohlin: countries export goods intensive in their abundant factor (labour for India). Stolper–Samuelson: trade raises returns to the abundant factor, but the Leontief paradox and India's capital- and skill-intensive export pattern (IT, pharma) complicate the story.
  • New trade theory (Krugman, 1979–80): scale economies and love of variety explain intra-industry trade between similar countries. Melitz (2003): only the most productive firms export, and trade liberalisation reallocates market share towards them, raising aggregate productivity.

2The welfare economics of tariffs

Small open economy: ΔW = −½·t²·(ε_d·D + ε_s·S)/P (production + consumption distortion triangles) Large country, optimal tariff: t* = 1 / ε* (inverse of the foreign export-supply elasticity) → beggar-thy-neighbour
  • A small country loses from any tariff. A large country can gain via improved terms of trade, but only if partners do not retaliate. In the Nash equilibrium of a tariff war, everyone is worse off (Johnson, 1953).
  • Empirical estimates for the 2018–19 US tariffs (Amiti, Redding and Weinstein; Fajgelbaum et al.) found near-complete pass-through to US import prices. The deadweight loss was modest relative to GDP but concentrated on particular sectors and regions.
  • Second-best arguments: infant industry, externalities, national security, and strategic trade (Brander–Spencer). These are valid in theory but vulnerable to capture in practice.

3Effective rate of protection

ERP = (t_output − a·t_input) / (1 − a) a = share of imported inputs in the output's value at free-trade prices

India's "inverted duty structure", where inputs are taxed more than outputs (as in parts of electronics, solar and textiles), gives negative effective protection to domestic value addition. It is a recurring argument for cutting input tariffs in India's manufacturing push.

4The gravity model and trade costs

X_ij = G · Y_i^α · Y_j^β / D_ij^θ · e^(FTA, common language, border …) structural form: multilateral resistance (Anderson–van Wincoop)
  • Gravity is the most robust empirical relation in trade. India historically "under-trades" relative to its size, which points to high trade costs (logistics, tariffs, non-tariff barriers).
  • Non-tariff measures (quality control orders, licensing, standards) increasingly matter more than tariffs. India's expansion of quality control orders since 2020 has been debated as hidden protection.
  • FTA utilisation by Indian exporters has historically been low (rules-of-origin complexity, small preference margins), which tempers the expected gains from new agreements.

5Distribution and political economy

  • The "China shock" (Autor, Dorn and Hanson, 2013): import competition caused persistent local labour-market losses in the US, which helps explain the political backlash against trade.
  • India's liberalisation (1991) offers a natural experiment. Topalova (2010) found slower poverty reduction in districts more exposed to tariff cuts, alongside aggregate productivity gains. That is the case for adjustment assistance and mobility.
  • Lobbying (Grossman–Helpman, "Protection for Sale"): organised sectors secure protection, consumers are diffuse. This helps explain why tariffs persist despite aggregate losses.

6The rules-based system under strain

  • WTO pillars: MFN non-discrimination, national treatment, bound tariffs, dispute settlement. The Appellate Body has been non-functional since December 2019; the MPIA is an interim workaround that India has not joined.
  • The US 2025–26 legal sequence: IEEPA tariffs were struck down (20 February 2026), then came a time-limited Section 122 balance-of-payments surcharge, then Section 301 and Section 232 actions. This illustrates both the constraints on executive tariff power and its persistence.
  • Carbon border measures (EU CBAM), export controls on semiconductors and critical minerals, and subsidy races (US IRA, EU and Indian PLIs) are blurring trade, climate and industrial policy.

7Review checklist: open questions

  1. Will the India–EU FTA's gains be eroded by CBAM and standards, and how high will utilisation be?
  2. Should India cut input tariffs unilaterally to deepen its role in global value chains, or keep them as negotiating leverage?
  3. Is India's quality-control-order regime protecting consumers or incumbents?
  4. How should India respond to US Section 301 tariffs: concessions, retaliation, diversification, or WTO litigation?
  5. Does "China+1" deliver durable manufacturing employment, or mainly final assembly with thin value added?
  6. Should India reconsider RCEP or other Asian trade frameworks?
