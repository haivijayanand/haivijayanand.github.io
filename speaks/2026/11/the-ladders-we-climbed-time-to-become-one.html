The Ladders We Climbed — Is It Time to Become One?

2 Nov 202611 min readSelf-GrowthManagement

We spent decades rising with the help of others. At what point should our success begin creating opportunities for someone else?

1. Nobody Climbs Alone

When we look back at our lives in our forties, we often feel a certain satisfaction.

We have completed our education, established ourselves professionally, achieved some financial stability, and earned a measure of recognition in society.

We may have overcome considerable difficulties along the way.

Naturally, we take pride in our accomplishments.

But a deeper reflection raises an interesting question.

How much of what we have achieved was possible entirely through our own efforts?

A teacher helped us understand a difficult subject.

A professor introduced us to a new field.

A senior colleague patiently explained something we could not understand.

A manager trusted us with a responsibility before we had fully proved ourselves.

A friend informed us about an opportunity.

Someone encouraged us when our confidence was low.

Others corrected our mistakes, challenged our assumptions, or simply demonstrated what excellence looked like.

We may have worked hard, but our efforts were supported by the knowledge, generosity, experience, and goodwill of many people.

Every one of them became a rung in the ladder we climbed.

Some helped us for years. Others influenced us through a single conversation.

And many probably never realised how significant their contribution had been.

2. When We Become the Experienced Ones

In our younger days, we actively searched for people who could guide us.

We wanted to understand which course to study, which career to pursue, how to develop professional competence, and how to make better decisions.

A conversation with an experienced professional could provide tremendous clarity.

But twenty years later, the situation has reversed.

We have become the experienced professionals.

We possess accumulated technical knowledge, professional judgement, financial understanding, practical wisdom, and familiarity with the consequences of different decisions.

The questions that once confused us now appear relatively straightforward.

Yet there is an uncomfortable possibility.

While we continue searching for people who can help us climb higher, are we overlooking those who are searching for someone like us?

A student may need guidance on selecting an educational path.

A young engineer may be struggling with a technical challenge.

A colleague may be uncertain about his professional future.

An aspiring entrepreneur may need practical advice before investing his savings.

For us, these may appear to be small matters.

For them, they may be life-changing decisions.

3. The Extraordinary Value of Ordinary Advice

Consider a student who is confused about choosing between two engineering disciplines.

An experienced professional may understand the career possibilities, limitations, and opportunities associated with both.

A discussion lasting twenty minutes might provide the student with a clarity that could otherwise take months to develop.

Now consider a young engineer who is struggling with a problem.

A senior colleague who has encountered similar problems over many years may recognise the issue almost immediately.

A short explanation could save several days of unsuccessful effort.

The cost to the experienced person is small.

The value to the recipient may be enormous.

This is one of the most remarkable features of accumulated knowledge.

What has become ordinary knowledge to us may still be extraordinary wisdom to someone else.

We often underestimate the value of what we know precisely because we know it so well.

Our experience has made certain problems appear trivial.

But their simplicity to us does not reduce their importance to others.

In fact, it increases our ability to help.

4. Should We Stop Climbing?

This brings us to a philosophical question.

Having reached a reasonably comfortable position in life, should we pause our personal ambitions and concentrate on giving back?

Should we stop looking for additional ladders and become a ladder ourselves?

I believe this is not an either-or decision.

There is no reason why personal growth and helping others cannot happen simultaneously.

A scientist can continue pursuing advanced research while mentoring younger engineers.

A successful entrepreneur can expand his business while guiding aspiring entrepreneurs.

A senior manager can work towards greater organisational responsibilities while developing capable successors.

A professor can continue contributing to research while inspiring students.

Indeed, remaining intellectually active and professionally relevant can make our guidance more valuable.

The world changes. Technologies evolve. New opportunities emerge. Advice based entirely on past experience may eventually become outdated.

Therefore, stopping our own development is not necessarily the best way to help others.

We do not have to stop climbing to become a ladder.

Instead, we should begin asking whether our continued growth is also creating opportunities for people around us.

5. From Personal Success to Collective Progress

For much of our early professional life, our efforts are directed towards accumulation.

We accumulate qualifications, experience, financial resources, professional relationships, skills, and recognition.

These are legitimate and necessary pursuits.

But after reaching a certain level of competence and stability, another possibility emerges.

We can begin multiplying what we have accumulated.

Knowledge becomes more valuable when it improves the capabilities of others.

Professional experience becomes more meaningful when it helps someone avoid preventable mistakes.

Leadership becomes more effective when it develops independent, capable individuals rather than merely securing personal authority.

A successful professional need not measure achievement only through personal milestones.

There is another question worth asking:

How many people have become more capable, confident, or successful because of our presence in their lives?

This is a different dimension of success.

It is less visible than a promotion, qualification, or financial achievement.

But its impact may extend far beyond our own careers.

6. The Responsibility of Those Who Have Climbed

When we occupy a senior position, it becomes easy to assume that our main responsibility is to manage work, deliver results, and achieve organisational objectives.

These responsibilities are unquestionably important.

But seniority also provides an opportunity to shape the development of others.

A young professional may not require constant supervision. He may need exposure to a challenging task.

Another may not lack technical capability. She may lack confidence.

Someone else may need a senior person to recognise a strength that has remained unnoticed.

A promising student may simply need to understand that attending an ordinary college does not place a permanent ceiling on future achievement.

Our contribution may not require money or extraordinary effort.

It may involve sharing knowledge, offering encouragement, providing honest feedback, creating opportunities, or introducing someone to a useful professional connection.

Sometimes, giving another person a chance to prove himself is more valuable than providing advice.

The strength of a leader is not measured only by what he accomplishes, but also by the capabilities he helps develop in others.

7. Giving Back Without Creating Dependence

There is, however, an important distinction between helping someone and making that person dependent upon us.

Experience can tempt us to believe that we already know the best answers.

We may begin advising younger people to repeat the choices that worked for us.

But circumstances change.

A career path that was attractive twenty years ago may not be equally attractive today.

An educational choice that suited us may not suit someone with different interests and capabilities.

Even our professional successes may have depended partly on opportunities and conditions that cannot be reproduced.

Therefore, giving back should not mean imposing our own journey upon others.

A good mentor does not manufacture copies of himself.

He helps people discover their own strengths.

Instead of saying, "You must do what I did," we should be willing to ask, "What do you want to achieve, and what possibilities are available to you?"

Our experience should help people make better decisions, not take away their freedom to decide.

The ultimate objective of mentoring is independence.

A good ladder helps someone rise. It does not insist that the person remain standing on it.

8. Paying Forward What We Cannot Repay

There is another interesting aspect of giving back.

Many people who contributed to our success may no longer be part of our lives.

Some teachers have retired.

Some seniors have moved to other organisations.

Some mentors may no longer be alive.

Others may not even remember the guidance they once provided.

How do we repay their contributions?

Perhaps the answer is not always to repay them directly.

Perhaps it is to pass their generosity forward.

A teacher who encouraged one student may indirectly influence hundreds of people through that student's subsequent contributions.

A senior engineer who shared critical technical knowledge may have helped prevent future failures that he never personally witnessed.

A manager who identified potential in a young professional may have initiated a chain of achievements extending across decades.

Their contributions did not end with the immediate recipient.

They continued through that person.

Similarly, the student we help today may eventually guide dozens of others.

The colleague we encourage may become an inspiring leader.

The young engineer we mentor may develop a technology that benefits thousands.

We may never see the complete results.

But that does not diminish the importance of our contribution.

Wisdom passed from one person to another can create a chain of progress that outlives its original contributors.

9. What Can We Actually Give Back?

Giving back need not involve establishing a foundation, organising a major social initiative, or donating substantial amounts of money.

We can begin with resources we already possess.

We can give our time to someone seeking direction.

We can share knowledge that took us years to acquire.

We can explain failures and mistakes that others might otherwise repeat.

We can provide professional exposure to deserving young people.

We can recommend capable individuals when genuine opportunities arise.

We can encourage those whose confidence has been weakened by rejection or disappointment.

We can also give back through everyday conduct.

By treating junior colleagues with dignity.

By recognising effort rather than merely celebrating visible success.

By providing constructive criticism without humiliation.

By sharing credit fairly.

By creating an environment in which people are encouraged to ask questions, experiment, and learn.

These may appear to be ordinary actions.

But organisations and societies improve through the accumulation of such behaviour.

10. When Giving Back Becomes a Way of Life

Perhaps we make giving back unnecessarily complicated by treating it as a separate activity.

We imagine that we must first become exceptionally wealthy, reach the highest professional position, or retire from active work before contributing meaningfully to others.

But there is no such requirement.

A person does not need to reach the summit before helping someone climb.

We can offer support from wherever we currently stand.

The important change is not necessarily in our financial capacity or professional designation.

It is in our attitude.

Instead of asking only, "What can I gain from this interaction?" we can occasionally ask, "What can I contribute?"

Instead of treating knowledge as a source of personal advantage, we can recognise its value as a shared resource.

Instead of worrying that someone younger may eventually surpass us, we can take satisfaction in having contributed to that achievement.

When this attitude becomes natural, giving back stops being an additional responsibility.

It becomes part of the way we work and live.

11. The Ladders We Leave Behind

There is a tendency to associate success with height.

How high did we rise in our profession?

How much wealth did we accumulate?

What position did we eventually occupy?

How many people recognised our achievements?

These are familiar questions.

But perhaps another question deserves equal importance.

After we climbed, did we make the journey easier for anyone else?

Our careers will eventually end.

Our designations will pass to someone else.

The authority attached to our positions will disappear.

Even many of our professional achievements may gradually become part of institutional history.

But the people we encouraged, the knowledge we shared, and the opportunities we created may continue influencing the future.

Someone may remember a conversation that changed his direction.

A young engineer may apply a principle we once patiently explained.

A student may discover confidence because we took his aspirations seriously.

And perhaps some of those people will eventually extend the same support to others.

That is how a ladder becomes more than an instrument of personal ascent.

It becomes a bridge between generations.

We spent the first part of our lives climbing with the help of others.

There is nothing wrong with continuing to climb.

But as we gain experience, influence, and capability, perhaps our ambitions can become larger than our own advancement.

The true measure of our growth may not be how far we have travelled, but how much further others can travel because we were part of their journey.

And perhaps, one day, the greatest satisfaction will not come from remembering the height we reached.

It will come from seeing someone standing higher than us, knowing that we helped make that climb possible.

“The true measure of our growth may not be how far we have travelled, but how much further others can travel because we were part of their journey.”
