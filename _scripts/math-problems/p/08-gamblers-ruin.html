1. The problem

You have ₹100. You bet ₹1 at a time on a fair coin toss: heads you win ₹1, tails you lose ₹1. You stop when you reach ₹200 or go broke.

  • What is the chance you double your money?
  • How many tosses does the game last on average?
  • What changes in a casino, where the odds are only slightly against you?

2. The answer

Fair coin: chance to double
50 %
Fair coin: average length
10,000
tosses (= 100 × 100)
Roulette (win 18/37): chance to double
0.45 %

A tiny house edge of 2.7 % per bet turns a coin-flip into near-certain ruin when you make many small bets. If you must gamble to double your money, one big bet is far better than many small ones.

3. Working it out

Let a be your starting money, N the target, p the chance of winning each ₹1 bet and q = 1 − p.

Fair game (p = ½)

P(reach N) = a / N      E[tosses] = a(N − a)

The fair game is like a balance: your expected money never changes, so the chances of the two exits must average out to what you started with.

Unfair game (p ≠ ½), with ρ = q/p

P(reach N) = (1 − ρa) / (1 − ρN)      E[tosses] = a/(q − p) − (N/(q − p)) · P(reach N)

For roulette ρ = 19/18 ≈ 1.056; ρ100 ≈ 223 and ρ200 ≈ 49,800, so P ≈ 222/49,800 ≈ 0.45 %.

6. Fine print

  • Against an infinitely rich opponent (the casino), a player making repeated fair bets will go broke with probability 1 — eventually. That is the original meaning of "gambler's ruin".
  • The same model describes a random walk between two absorbing barriers: diffusion of a particle, a share price hitting a stop-loss, or a queue emptying.
