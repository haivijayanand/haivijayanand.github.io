Level 1 · For school students

Money has changed from shells and coins to paper notes to numbers on a phone. Crypto is a new kind of digital token. Is it money?

1What makes something money?

Money does three jobs:

🔁 Medium of exchangePeople accept it in return for goods and services.
📏 Unit of accountPrices are written in it: a pencil costs ₹10.
🏦 Store of valueIt keeps its value reasonably well over time.

The rupee does all three because people trust it: the RBI issues it and the law says everyone must accept it. That's called legal tender.

2Money goes digital

  1. Bank money: most rupees already exist only as numbers in bank accounts.
  2. UPI: pay anyone instantly by phone. India makes over 80 crore UPI payments a day.
  3. e-rupee (e₹): a digital rupee issued directly by the RBI, like a digital banknote.
  4. Crypto (bitcoin and others): digital tokens created by computer networks, not by any government or bank.

4Is crypto good money?

  • Prices swing wildly: one bitcoin went from over ₹1 crore to under ₹70 lakh within about a year. That makes it a poor unit of account and a risky store of value.
  • No one stands behind it: if you lose your password, or an exchange is hacked, there is usually no one to help.
  • Scams are common: fake apps and "double your money" offers trap many people.
  • Supporters like that no government controls it and its supply is limited (bitcoin will never exceed 21 million coins).

2Calculator: how crypto tax compares

Crypto: 30% on each gain plus 4% cess (31.2%); losses on one coin cannot be set off against gains on another, or carried forward. 1% TDS is deducted on each sale; it is credited against your tax, but it ties up cash for active traders. Equity comparison: long-term gains at 12.5% above the ₹1.25 lakh exemption, with losses set off. Surcharge ignored.

3Legal position in India

  • Crypto is a "virtual digital asset" (VDA): legal to hold and trade, but not legal tender and with no dedicated law yet.
  • Exchanges serving Indians must register with FIU-IND and follow anti-money-laundering rules; several offshore exchanges were blocked for not registering.
  • From April 2026, platforms face penalties for not reporting transactions properly (₹200 a day for non-filing; ₹50,000 for incorrect filings). India is adopting the OECD's crypto reporting framework (CARF) for cross-border data sharing.
  • Many Indian traders have moved to offshore platforms, which offer even less protection.

4Risks and scams

VolatilityFalls of 50–80% have happened in every crypto cycle. Only invest money you can afford to lose entirely.
CustodyExchanges get hacked or fail (FTX, 2022; WazirX, 2024). "Not your keys, not your coins", but self-custody risks losing keys.
ScamsFake trading apps, "pig-butchering" romance-investment frauds, pump-and-dump tokens, and fake customer-care numbers.
Rules can changeTax and regulation can change suddenly, and you are taxed even on loss-making years overall.

Protect yourself: use only FIU-registered platforms, enable two-factor authentication, never share OTPs or seed phrases, distrust "guaranteed returns", and report fraud at cybercrime.gov.in or helpline 1930.

5UPI and the e-rupee: what's new

  • UPI keeps growing about 20–25% a year. A proposed small merchant fee (MDR) on larger payments was put off during the 2026 festive season; person-to-person payments remain free.
  • UPI now works in several countries (UAE, Singapore, France, Sri Lanka, Mauritius and others) and is linked to cross-border remittance corridors.
  • The e-rupee is being tried for targeted, programmable payments such as food-subsidy coupons that can only be spent on food.

Level 3 · Review for experts

What money is, the economics of permissionless ledgers, valuation debates, stablecoin run risk, CBDC design, and India's policy choices.

1What is money? Theories

  • Commodity and credit views: metallist theories versus chartalist/credit theories (Innes, Knapp); modern money is mostly bank deposits created by lending (McLeay, Radia and Thomas, 2014; Topic 04).
  • Money as memory (Kocherlakota, 1998): money substitutes for a public record of past trades, which a blockchain literally provides.
  • The singleness of money: different forms (central bank money, deposits, e-money) trade at par because of regulation, deposit insurance and settlement in central bank money. Private tokens without this can trade below par.

2Blockchain economics

  • Consensus without trust: proof of work makes rewriting history costly. Budish (2018) shows that security requires flow payments to miners proportional to the value that could be attacked, so security is expensive relative to transaction value.
  • The trilemma: decentralisation, security and scalability are hard to achieve together. Layer-2 networks and rollups trade some decentralisation for throughput.
  • Ethereum moved to proof of stake in 2022, cutting energy use by over 99%. Bitcoin's proof of work still uses a country-scale amount of electricity.
  • Valuation: bitcoin has no cash flows. Its price rests on expected future demand as a store of value, which is self-fulfilling (sunspot-like, Garratt and Wallace) and so prone to bubbles and crashes.

3Stablecoin run risk: calculator

Par redemption capacity = cash + T-bills·(1 − h₁) + other assets·(1 − h₂) Run if redemptions R exceed capacity before assets can be sold

This is a simplified first-come, first-served model, with liquidity used in order: cash, then T-bills, then other assets. Gorton and Zhang (2021) compare stablecoins to 19th-century private banknotes. The US GENIUS Act (2025) and the EU's MiCA require high-quality liquid reserves to reduce this risk.

4Central bank digital currency design

  • Retail vs wholesale: retail CBDC gives the public direct central bank money; wholesale CBDC settles interbank and tokenised-asset trades.
  • Bank disintermediation: if households shift deposits into CBDC, especially in a panic, bank funding falls. Holding caps, zero remuneration and tiered designs limit this (Bindseil).
  • Privacy vs compliance: account-based designs ease AML but risk surveillance; token-based and offline designs mimic cash.
  • Programmability: purpose-bound money (like food coupons) improves targeting but fragments the singleness of money if overused.
  • India's case is unusual: UPI already delivers instant retail payments, so the e-rupee's value lies more in offline use, programmability and cross-border settlement (BIS mBridge-type projects).

5Macro and policy

  • Currency substitution: dollar stablecoins can dollarise payments in emerging markets and weaken monetary policy and capital flow management. This is the RBI's core concern.
  • Regulatory paths: licensing and full regulation (EU MiCA, US for stablecoins), heavy taxation and tolerance (India), or bans (China). The IMF–FSB synthesis paper (endorsed under India's 2023 G20 presidency) favours comprehensive regulation over outright bans.
  • Taxation as policy: India's 1% TDS works as a transaction tax that moved volume offshore, eroding both revenue and oversight. This is an example of tax incidence and elasticity in a mobile market.

6Review checklist: open questions

  1. Should India license crypto exchanges under a dedicated law, and cut TDS to bring trading onshore?
  2. How should rupee-linked or dollar stablecoins be treated, given capital account controls?
  3. What unique value does a retail e-rupee add alongside UPI?
  4. Is proof-of-work security sustainable as block rewards halve every four years?
  5. Will tokenisation of bonds, deposits and real assets matter more than cryptocurrencies themselves?
