Stress Concentration and Notch Sensitivity

Holes, shoulders, grooves and keyways raise the local stress far above the nominal value. Learn the theoretical stress concentration factor Kt, notch sensitivity q, the fatigue factor Kf, and how a few millimetres of fillet radius can save a part.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: Kt of a stepped shaft❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Explain why stress concentrates at a change of section
  • Read Kt from Peterson's charts for shoulders, holes and grooves
  • Calculate notch sensitivity q and fatigue factor Kf
  • Know when stress concentration matters (brittle, fatigue) and when it does not (ductile, static)
  • Use design tricks to reduce stress concentration

2Stress flow

Imagine stress as water flowing through the part. At a sudden change of section the "flow lines" crowd together and the local stress peaks. The ratio of the peak to the nominal stress (calculated on the net section) is the theoretical stress concentration factor:

Kt = σmax / σnom Kts = τmax / τnom

Kt depends only on geometry and type of load — not on the material or size. Peterson's Stress Concentration Factors is the standard source; design data books reproduce the common charts.

FeatureTypical Kt
Small hole in a wide plate (tension)3.0
Shaft shoulder D/d = 1.5, r/d = 0.02 (bending)≈ 2.5
Shaft shoulder D/d = 1.5, r/d = 0.1 (bending)≈ 1.7
Profile keyway (end-milled), bending / torsion≈ 2.0 / 3.0 (Kf)
Sled-runner keyway, bending≈ 1.6
Retaining-ring groove≈ 5
Thread root (rolled / cut)Kf ≈ 2.2–3.0 / 2.8–3.8

3When it matters

Ductile, staticThe peak yields locally and redistributes. Usually ignored (Kt = 1).
Brittle, staticNo yielding to relieve it. Use full Kt. (Grey cast iron is an exception: its graphite flakes are already notches, so Kt ≈ 1.)
Any material, fatigueCracks start at the peak. Use Kf — almost all fatigue failures start at a stress raiser.

4Notch sensitivity

In fatigue, a sharp notch hurts less than Kt predicts, because the steep stress gradient means only a tiny volume is highly stressed. Notch sensitivity q (0 to 1) measures how much of Kt is felt:

Kf = 1 + q (Kt − 1)
Neuber: q = 1 / (1 + √a / √r) — √a falls as Sut rises
Peterson: q = 1 / (1 + a/r)

High-strength steels have q close to 1 — they feel nearly the full Kt. Soft steels and small radii give lower q. If in doubt, take q = 1 (Kf = Kt): safe.

5Reducing stress concentration

  1. Use the largest fillet radius the mating part allows. Bearing seats: use a relief groove or a spacer with a chamfer so a big radius fits under the bearing's corner.
  2. Keep D/d small — big steps concentrate more.
  3. Add relief grooves near a sharp notch to spread the flow lines.
  4. Put holes and keyways where bending stress is low (near the neutral axis or at a support).
  5. Use sled-runner rather than profile keyways at fatigue-critical sections.
  6. Roll threads and fillets, shot-peen, or nitride — compressive residual stress delays cracks.

6Worked example

Shoulder on a C45 shaft in rotating bendingd = 40, D = 60, r = 2 mm → D/d = 1.5, r/d = 0.05 Kt (Peterson) ≈ 2.04 Sut = 620 MPa → √a ≈ 0.072 √in = 0.36 √mm; q = 1/(1 + 0.36/√2) = 0.80 Kf = 1 + 0.80 × (2.04 − 1) = 1.83 With r = 4 mm: Kt ≈ 1.70, q = 0.85, Kf ≈ 1.59 → 13 % lower peak stress, for free.

8Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Write the fillet radius on the drawing — "sharp corner" left to the machinist means r ≈ 0.2 mm and Kt ≈ 3.
  • Do not combine Kt factors by multiplying when two raisers are far apart; when they overlap (a hole at a fillet), multiplying is a rough upper bound.
  • FEA finds Kt for you, but only if the mesh is fine at the fillet. A coarse mesh under-predicts the peak.
  • Apply Kt to the nominal stress on the net section the chart was made for.

9Practice
