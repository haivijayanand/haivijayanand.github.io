NetWare and office networks

In the 1980s each PC was an island: files moved between desks on floppy disks, and each PC needed its own printer. Local area networks (LANs) joined them, and one company — Novell, with its NetWare server software — ran most of the world's office networks for a decade. Then Windows NT, TCP/IP and the Internet changed the rules. This episode closes Season 6 and the series so far.

02The wires: Ethernet wins

1973 — Ethernet. Robert Metcalfe and David Boggs at Xerox PARC connected Alto computers on a shared coaxial cable at about 3 Mb/s. DEC, Intel and Xerox published 10 Mb/s Ethernet in 1980; it became IEEE 802.3 in 1983. Metcalfe founded 3Com in 1979.
1977 — ARCNET. Datapoint's token-passing network, cheap and reliable; popular in early offices and factories.
1985 — Token Ring. IBM's 4 Mb/s (later 16 Mb/s) ring was deterministic and robust, but expensive.
1985–1990 — Cheaper Ethernet. Thin coax (10BASE2, with T-connectors and terminators) and then twisted-pair 10BASE-T (1990) with hubs made Ethernet cheap and easy to wire.
1990s — Switches and Fast Ethernet. Switches gave each port its own bandwidth; 100 Mb/s (1995) and gigabit (1998) followed. Token Ring and ARCNET faded.

03Novell NetWare

Novell began in 1979 in Utah as Novell Data Systems, a hardware maker. Its contractors at SuperSet Software wrote a file-server operating system to share disks among CP/M and then IBM PCs; launched in 1983 as NetWare, it became Novell's whole business under chief executive Ray Noorda. The key idea was file sharing instead of disk sharing: a dedicated server ran its own fast operating system and served files to PCs that ran a small NetWare client under DOS.

05Try it: shared hub or switch?

On a classic Ethernet hub (or coax), all stations share one 10 Mb/s channel and collisions waste time as traffic grows. A switch gives every port its own full-duplex link. Change the number of PCs and how busy they are.

06Try it: planning a lab network

TCP/IP replaced IPX/SPX and NetBEUI. Every device needs an IP address in the right subnet. Enter an address with a prefix length (CIDR), as you would when setting up a test-lab LAN.

07Networks in India

  • NICNET (National Informatics Centre, from 1987) linked district and state offices by satellite — one of the largest government networks of its time.
  • ERNET (Education and Research Network, 1986) connected research institutions and gave India its first Internet e-mail and .in domain management.
  • NetWare servers were common in Indian banks, PSUs and colleges in the 1990s; Novell certification courses ran in training institutes alongside Tally.
  • Today, government and defence networks use segregated LANs, Active Directory or Linux directory services, and approved firewalls; classified networks stay air-gapped.

09Lessons

  1. Open standards beat proprietary ones. Ethernet and TCP/IP outlasted Token Ring and IPX.
  2. Bundling is powerful. Windows NT came with networking, TCP/IP and a familiar interface; NetWare's technical lead was not enough.
  3. Directories matter. NDS and Active Directory showed that managing users is as important as sharing files.
  4. Plan addresses and segments. A test-lab network with separate subnets for DAQ, control and office traffic is easier to secure and troubleshoot.
This closes Season 6. Across 34 episodes the pattern repeats: a real need, a breakthrough product, competitors and lawsuits, absorption into the operating system or an open standard — and the enduring value of open formats and protocols.
