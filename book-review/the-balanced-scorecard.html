The Balanced Scorecard

Robert S. Kaplan and David P. Norton · first published 1996 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Financial results alone cannot steer a modern company; strategy must be translated into a balanced set of linked measures that people can act on.

Summary

Robert Kaplan and David Norton start from a problem every manager knows. Traditional accounting reports tell you how the business performed last quarter, but they say little about whether it is building the capabilities that will create value next year. In an economy driven by customers, knowledge and innovation, steering only by financial figures is like driving while looking in the rear-view mirror.

Their answer is the balanced scorecard, a framework that keeps financial measures but adds three other perspectives. The customer perspective asks how the firm creates value for the segments it chooses to serve. The internal business process perspective identifies the few processes the company must excel at to satisfy those customers. The learning and growth perspective covers the people, systems and culture that make improvement possible.

The measures are not a random collection of indicators. Each scorecard should tell the story of a strategy through a chain of cause and effect: better skills improve processes, better processes delight customers, and loyal customers produce financial results. The authors also insist on a mix of outcome measures, which show what happened, and performance drivers, which signal what is likely to happen.

The book then moves beyond measurement to management. The scorecard becomes the backbone of four processes: clarifying and translating the vision into concrete objectives, communicating strategy and linking it to team and personal goals, aligning budgets and targets with strategic initiatives, and creating feedback so leaders can test and adjust the strategy itself rather than only checking whether plans were followed.

The lasting lesson is that what gets measured shapes what people pay attention to. A short, well-chosen set of balanced measures turns an abstract strategy into everyday work, and gives the whole organisation a shared language for discussing where it is going and how it will get there.

301 words

One-line takeaway: Translate strategy into a few linked measures across finance, customers, processes and learning, then use them to manage, not just to report.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What core weakness of traditional performance measurement do Kaplan and Norton highlight?

    Answer B. Financial reports describe past results but reveal little about the capabilities that drive future performance.

  2. Which of these is NOT one of the four perspectives of the balanced scorecard?

    Answer D. The four perspectives are financial, customer, internal business process, and learning and growth.

  3. What does the internal business process perspective focus on?

    Answer A. It identifies the few processes that matter most for meeting customer and financial objectives.

  4. How should the measures on a scorecard relate to one another?

    Answer C. The authors want measures connected so that improvements in drivers flow through to customer and financial outcomes.

  5. The learning and growth perspective is mainly concerned with:

    Answer C. It covers the intangible foundations that enable process improvement and innovation.

  6. Why do the authors combine outcome measures with performance drivers?

    Answer A. Lagging outcome measures confirm results; leading driver measures give early warning and direction.

  7. Beyond measurement, how do Kaplan and Norton see the scorecard being used?

    Answer D. The book argues the scorecard should anchor key management processes, not just report numbers.

  8. Linking the scorecard to team and individual goals mainly helps to:

    Answer B. Cascading objectives lets employees see how their work contributes to strategic aims.

  9. What is the purpose of the feedback and learning process in the scorecard approach?

    Answer A. Strategic feedback lets leaders question whether their assumptions about cause and effect still hold.

  10. Which idea best captures the book's overall message?

    Answer C. The scorecard translates abstract strategy into measurable objectives that everyone can act on.
