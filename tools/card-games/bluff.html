How to play Bluff

Also called Cheat or I Doubt It. You play against Arun (left), Meena (top) and Ravi (right). One 52-card deck is dealt out, 13 cards each. Play goes clockwise: You → Arun → Meena → Ravi. First player to get rid of all their cards wins.

A round

  1. The player who starts a round places 1 to 4 cards face down on the pile and names any rank, e.g. "three 7s". The number of cards is always visible; only the rank may be a lie.
  2. Each following player must either place 1–4 cards claiming the same rank, or pass.
  3. After any play, anyone may shout "Bluff!". The cards just played are turned over:
    • If any of them is not of the claimed rank, the liar picks up the whole pile.
    • If they are all genuine, the caller picks up the whole pile.
    The player who did not pick up starts a new round with any rank.
  4. If everyone else passes after a play, the round ends: the pile is discarded (out of the game, unseen) and the last player who placed cards starts the next round.

Winning

A player who places their last cards wins once that play survives — either nobody calls Bluff, or the call shows the cards were genuine. If they were caught lying, they pick up the pile and play continues.

Your Bluff window

After each computer play you get a few seconds with a big Bluff! button (and No call to skip). You get first chance to call; after that the computer players may call.

Variant choices

  • Common Indian rule set: round starter names any rank; others must keep the same rank or pass; all-pass discards the pile.
  • 4 players, one deck, no jokers. (Bigger groups normally use 2 decks; this edition always seats 4 with 1 deck.)
  • Only the most recent play is revealed when Bluff is called.
  • The round leader cannot pass.

Computer players

  • Easy – random claims and random calls.
  • Normal – tracks its own cards and always calls a claim that is impossible from its own hand (e.g. it holds two Kings and you claim three Kings).
  • Hard – remembers every card it has seen (its own plays, revealed cards, who picked up which pile) and calls when the hypergeometric chance the claim is honest is low, always challenging a player about to go out.

Odds & Strategy

1. Does the claimant really hold k cards of that rank?

You hold n cards, m of rank R. The other U = 52 − n cards contain r = 4 − m cards of rank R. A player holding h of those unseen cards has X cards of rank R, hypergeometric:
P(X = j) = C(r,j)·C(U−r,h−j) / C(U,h)   P(honest) ≈ P(X ≥ k)

2. Impossible-claim detector

There are exactly 4 cards of each rank. Count what you know is not in the claimant's hand: the copies you hold (m), copies you played honestly into the pile this round (a), and copies you saw revealed or know were discarded/picked up by someone else (d). The claimant can hold at most 4 − m − a − d.

Strategy tips

  • Lead with your biggest set. Starting a round with three or four genuine cards is safe and sheds cards fast.
  • Pad truth with a lie: two genuine 9s plus one odd card claimed as three 9s is hard to catch — and if called, only the last play is turned over.
  • Call when the count is impossible. If you hold two Kings and someone claims three Kings, they are lying for certain.
  • Probability is on the claimant's side for 1-card claims: with r = 3 unseen copies among 39 cards, a 13-card hand holds at least one copy about 70% of the time.
  • Big piles change the maths. A wrong call on a 20-card pile hurts far more than on a 3-card pile — call only when the chance of a lie is well above 50%.
  • Always consider calling the last play of a player going out — if you let it go, they win.
  • Remember the cards you picked up and the cards you saw revealed: they are no longer in other hands.
