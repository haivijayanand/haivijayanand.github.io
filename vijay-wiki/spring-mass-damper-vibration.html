Differential Equations in Vibration: The Spring–Mass–Damper System

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
Differential equationsVibrationDampingIsolation

The spring–mass–damper is the most useful differential equation in mechanical engineering. Machines on mounts, shafts on bearings, sensors on brackets and even building floors behave, near one resonance, like a mass on a spring with some damping. Solving m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = F(t) gives natural frequency, how fast vibration dies away, and how much is transmitted to the foundation.

Key takeaways

  • Natural frequency ωn = √(k/m); fn = ωn/2π. Stiffer or lighter means higher frequency.
  • Damping ratio ζ = c/(2√(km)); most structures have ζ ≈ 0.01–0.05; ζ = 1 is critical damping (no oscillation).
  • Free vibration decays as e−ζωnt; logarithmic decrement δ = ln(xn/xn+1) ≈ 2πζ.
  • Forced response peaks near r = f/fn = 1 with amplification ≈ 1/(2ζ).
  • Isolation needs r > √2: mount natural frequency must be well below the forcing frequency (ideally r ≥ 3).

1. The equation and its parameters

2. Free vibration and damping measurement

Logarithmic decrement δ = ln(x_n / x_(n+1)) = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²) ζ ≈ δ / 2π (light damping); over N cycles: ζ ≈ ln(x_0/x_N)/(2πN) Half-power bandwidth: ζ ≈ (f2 − f1)/(2·fn)

3. Forced vibration and isolation

Steady-state amplitude X = (F0/k) / √[(1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²] r = ω/ωn Phase lag φ = atan[2ζr / (1 − r²)] Transmissibility T = √[1 + (2ζr)²] / √[(1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²]

4. Solving numerically

For any force history, rewrite as two first-order equations and integrate (Runge–Kutta 4 below): x′ = v, v′ = (F(t) − c·v − k·x)/m. Choose a time step at most about 1/20 of the natural period.
