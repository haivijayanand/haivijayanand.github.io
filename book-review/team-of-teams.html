Team of Teams

Stanley McChrystal · first published 2015 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

In a fast, interconnected world, organisations win through adaptability, built by linking small teams through shared awareness and trusted, decentralised decision-making.

Summary

General Stanley McChrystal, writing with several colleagues, draws on his time leading a US special operations task force in Iraq. His forces were better trained, better equipped and better funded than the insurgent network they fought, yet they kept losing ground. The enemy was loose, fast and able to change shape. The task force was a superb machine built for a slower, more predictable world.

The book traces that mismatch to the management thinking of the industrial age. Breaking work into specialised parts and controlling it from the top made organisations highly efficient. Efficiency works well when problems are merely complicated and can be planned in advance. It fails when problems are complex: when countless interacting factors make the future impossible to predict. In such conditions, the ability to adapt matters more than the ability to optimise.

Small teams already adapt well. Their members trust one another, share a purpose and understand the whole situation, so they can react to surprises without waiting for orders. The trouble is that a large organisation made of excellent teams can still behave badly, because the teams compete, hoard information and see only their own piece of the problem.

The answer McChrystal describes is a team of teams: giving the whole organisation the qualities of a single good team. Two practices made this possible. The first was shared consciousness. The task force opened up information radically, held a daily briefing that thousands of people joined, and placed its people inside partner agencies to build relationships and understanding. The second was empowered execution. Once people understood the big picture, decisions could be pushed down to those closest to the action, which made the organisation far faster.

This changes the leader's role. Instead of directing every move like a chess player, the leader acts more like a gardener, shaping the environment, setting culture and keeping the system healthy so others can act well.

317 words

One-line takeaway: Share information widely so everyone sees the whole picture, then trust people close to the action to decide, and lead by tending the system rather than controlling every move.
