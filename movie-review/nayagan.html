Summary

Nayagan follows one man across about forty years — from a frightened boy who flees Tamil Nadu after avenging his father, to a Dharavi dock worker, a smuggler, a protector of the slum, and finally an ageing don who is loved by the poor, hunted by the police and rejected by his own daughter.

Mani Ratnam borrows the scale of The Godfather but plants it firmly in Bombay’s Tamil migrant community. The film is less about crime than about a question it never resolves: can a man who does wrong for the right people still be called good? Kamal Haasan plays Velu from his twenties to old age, and the performance — the stoop, the slow speech, the dentures that reshape his jaw — is still studied as one of the finest in Indian cinema.

A gangster film whose real subject is fatherhood — the father Velu loses, the father he becomes to a whole slum, and the father his children cannot forgive.

Plot

Spoilers ahead. Acts 4 and 5 are blurred —

Boyhood & flight

Police trick the boy Sakthivel into revealing where his father, a union leader, is hiding. The father is killed. Velu stabs the inspector responsible and runs away to Bombay.

Dharavi & Hussain Bhai

Hussain, a Muslim smuggler in the Dharavi slums, takes him in. Velu grows up on the docks, and when Hussain falls ill he takes over the work — and raises the share paid to the workers, which makes enemies.

Becoming Naicker

Hussain dies in police custody, his death passed off as suicide by the corrupt Inspector Kelkar. Velu kills Kelkar, then quietly supports Kelkar’s widow and disabled son, Ajit. He rescues a schoolgirl, Neela, from a brothel and marries her; they have a son, Surya, and a daughter, Charumathi.

The price of power

After Velu humiliates the rival Reddy brothers, they try to assassinate him and kill Neela instead. Velu takes revenge and sends his children away to Madras. Years later Surya joins the syndicate and dies in an explosion during a police chase. Charumathi cuts off her father.

Judgement

Charumathi marries ACP Patil, the officer determined to bring Velu down. Velu surrenders to protect his men; at trial, no witness will testify and he is acquitted. As he greets the crowd on the court steps, Ajit — who has learned that Velu killed his father — shoots him dead.

Music

Nayagan was Ilaiyaraaja’s 400th film. He used old, folk-rooted instruments so the score would sound like it belonged to the people of the story rather than to a studio. The theme, “Thenpandi Cheemayile”, is the film’s spine: it opens the credits and comes back at every turning point, almost like a narrator mourning Velu before he has done anything.

A neat detail: in the opening titles the theme uses a rough, colloquial line sung by Ilaiyaraaja; when it returns later in the film the wording is more polished, as if the song itself has climbed in class along with Velu.

Technology & craft

Nayagan was made on 35 mm film in 1987, before digital intermediates, CGI or video assist were part of Tamil film-making. Everything the audience sees was built, lit and captured in camera. That is what makes the craft worth studying.

Cinematography

P. C. Sreeram lit the film low and warm, with deep shadows and strong single sources — a look often compared to Gordon Willis’s work on The Godfather. He framed characters inside doorways, windows and railings (frame-within-a-frame), so Velu often looks trapped even at the height of his power.

Set building

Rather than shoot in the real Dharavi for weeks, Thota Tharani recreated the slum at Venus Studios in Madras, filled with thousands of junior artists and even flocks of pigeons. An old building was turned into both the brothel and Velu’s house. The period cars and décor carry the passage of decades.

Make-up & ageing

To age Velu convincingly without prosthetic overload, Kamal Haasan wore dentures to change his jaw line and posture. He asked Janagaraj and Delhi Ganesh to cut their hair so their characters would age alongside his.

Locations & schedule

Shooting began in November 1986. The first ten days were scrapped after Kamal Haasan was unhappy with the footage, followed by a three-day test shoot. The real schedule began in January 1987, including 15 days on the Dharavi set; some scenes were shot in Bombay, and the last day and a half covered the childhood scenes.

Props & action

With money tight, Kamal Haasan brought his own gun and a sugar-glass (breakaway) bottle from the US for the action scenes — at the time a rarity on Tamil sets.

Editing

The first cut ran about three hours. B. Lenin and V. T. Vijayan trimmed it to roughly 155 minutes; the producer later credited the trimming, and the theme song, with giving the film its life.

The censor board in Madras first refused to certify the film because it was based on a living person. It was cleared in Bombay after the producer showed a letter from Varadarajan Mudaliar saying the film was not his life story. Kamal Haasan and Mani Ratnam had met Mudaliar before shooting — and, by one account, it was Mudaliar himself who suggested that Velu should die at the end.

Released on Diwali 1987, it became both a critical and a commercial success, running well over 175 days in theatres.

Emotional arc across the film

Where the emotions come from

The child’s question. When Velu’s grandson asks whether he is a good man or a bad man, and Velu answers that he does not know, the film puts its own unresolved question into the mouth of a child. It hurts because it is honest.
Neela. Saranya’s Neela is the film’s warmth. Her shy courtship and simple life with Velu make her death the moment the story turns from rise to fall, and the theme music turns from folk to lament.
A father’s rejection. Charumathi refusing her father’s world is the cruellest blow because it is not violent. The man who protected thousands cannot keep his own daughter.
Loyalty that blocks justice. At the trial, the people who love Velu protect him by staying silent. The audience feels both comfort and unease: the slum’s loyalty is real, and so are his crimes.
Grace in the villain. Velu secretly supports the family of the man he killed — and it is that same son who ends his life. The film’s irony is quiet, not showy.
Kamal’s ageing. The emotion is carried by the body: the slower walk, the heavier jaw, the eyes that stay still. Few actors have aged on screen this believably without modern prosthetics.

What works

  • Kamal Haasan’s performance across four decades of one life.
  • Ilaiyaraaja’s theme, which works almost like a Greek chorus.
  • A slum built on a studio floor that feels lived-in and real.
  • It refuses to give a clean moral answer.

What doesn’t

  • Some critics, such as historian S. Theodore Baskaran, argue it romanticises a smuggler as a Robin Hood figure.
  • The later decades move quickly; Surya and Charumathi get less room than the story needs.
  • The Godfather debt is obvious in places.

Watch it for: the performance, the music, and the last scene on the court steps. Watch it with: patience — it is a slow-burning epic, not an action film.

  • India’s official entry for Best Foreign Language Film at the 60th Academy Awards (not shortlisted)

Influence

  • Remade in Hindi as Dayavan (1988) with Vinod Khanna
  • Lines, scenes and the theme song are endlessly quoted and parodied in Tamil cinema
  • Mani Ratnam has said he will never make a sequel
