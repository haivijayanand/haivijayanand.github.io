The Profit and Loss Statement

The profit and loss statement tells the story of a period: how much the business sold, what it cost to make and sell, what it paid lenders and the government, and what was left for the owners. Read it top to bottom and each line answers a question. Pick your level.

Level 1 · For school students

The profit and loss statement starts with sales at the top and ends with profit at the bottom.

1A pizza shop's year

2Top line, bottom line

People call sales the top line (it's at the top) and profit after tax the bottom line (it's at the bottom). "Growing the top line" means selling more; "improving the bottom line" means keeping more of it as profit.

Level 3 · For experts — a review

The Indian statutory format, comprehensive income, per-share measures and how to judge earnings quality.

1Schedule III (Division II, Ind AS) statement of profit and loss

I Revenue from operations II Other income III Total income (I + II) IV Expenses: cost of materials consumed; purchases of stock-in-trade; changes in inventories of FG, WIP and stock-in-trade; employee benefits expense; finance costs; depreciation and amortisation; other expenses V Profit before exceptional items and tax (III − IV) VI Exceptional items VII Profit before tax VIII Tax expense: current tax; deferred tax IX Profit for the period from continuing operations (+ discontinued operations) X Other comprehensive income: items that will not / will be reclassified to profit or loss XI Total comprehensive income XII Earnings per equity share: basic, diluted

Expenses are classified by nature (Ind AS 1 allows by function, but Schedule III mandates nature in India). EBITDA is not a defined line; companies present it voluntarily, and SEBI requires consistency in its definition.

2Basic and diluted EPS (Ind AS 33)

EPS

Treasury-stock method for options: only the "free" shares (options × (1 − exercise ÷ market)) are added. A potential share that would increase EPS is anti-dilutive and excluded.

3Quality of earnings

Accruals ratio (balance-sheet) = (Net income − Cash from operations) ÷ Average total assets

Sloan (1996): firms with high accruals subsequently earn lower returns; the market overweights the accrual component. Red flags: revenue growing faster than receivables collection (rising DSO), recurring "exceptional" charges, capitalised costs rising, falling depreciation rates, other income propping up profit, and large gaps between EBITDA and operating cash flow.
