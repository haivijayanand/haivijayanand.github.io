Office and internet: LibreOffice, Firefox, Thunderbird

The office suite and the web browser are the two programs almost everyone uses every day. Open source gave Windows users a free alternative in both — LibreOffice, descended from a German teenager's word processor of 1985, and Firefox, rebuilt from the ashes of Netscape. Their story is also a story of forks, standards wars over file formats, and how free software pays its bills.

01The requirement: office and web without a gatekeeper

By the late 1990s Microsoft Office (Episodes 03–04) and Internet Explorer (Episode 07) dominated Windows. Office was expensive for schools, small businesses and governments in developing countries; its binary file formats were secret, so documents could be read reliably only with Microsoft's software. Internet Explorer 6 stagnated after 2001 while spyware and pop-ups spread through its security holes.

02From StarWriter to LibreOffice

198516-year-old Marco Börries in Germany writes StarWriter for Schneider/Amstrad CPC computers and founds Star Division
1990sStarOffice grows into a full suite for DOS, Windows, OS/2 and Unix, popular in Germany
1999Sun Microsystems buys Star Division and gives StarOffice away free
Oct 2000Sun releases the source code as OpenOffice.org; version 1.0 arrives in 2002
2005–2006OpenOffice.org's XML format becomes OASIS OpenDocument, then ISO/IEC 26300 (2006)
Jan 2010Oracle buys Sun; community developers worry about the project's future
Sep 2010Most community developers form The Document Foundation and fork the code as LibreOffice
2011Oracle donates OpenOffice.org to the Apache Software Foundation → Apache OpenOffice, which has since seen only occasional small updates
2024 →LibreOffice adopts year.month version numbers; 26.2 (February 2026) adds Markdown, drops the "Community" label and offers a mature "Still" branch alongside the newest "Fresh" one
A fork is the safety valve of open source: when the community disagreed with the owner, it could take the code and continue. Almost all active development and users moved to LibreOffice.

03The file-format war: ODF vs OOXML

Governments began to require open document formats for public records. Microsoft responded with its own XML-based format, Office Open XML (.docx, .xlsx, .pptx), introduced in Office 2007, standardised by Ecma (2006) and — after a fiercely contested fast-track vote — by ISO/IEC 29500 (2008). Today both are open, documented standards; OOXML dominates in practice, and LibreOffice reads and writes both.

See it: a document is a ZIP of XML files

Rename any .docx or .odt to .zip and open it — inside are XML files. Type a sentence; the word in [brackets] becomes bold. Compare how each format stores it.

04Firefox and Thunderbird

  
After Netscape released its code in 1998 (Episode 07), the Mozilla project spent years building a heavy all-in-one suite. A small team split off a lean browser: Phoenix (2002), renamed Firebird, then Firefox. Version 1.0 (9 November 2004) offered tabs, a pop-up blocker and add-ons when IE 6 had none; volunteers funded a two-page advertisement in The New York Times. Within five years Firefox had about a third of the world's desktop users, and its pressure forced Microsoft to restart IE development.

  
    
    
  • Funding: the non-profit Mozilla Foundation owns the for-profit Mozilla Corporation, whose income has come mostly from search-engine agreements (largely with Google). Courts in the 2025 US Google search case allowed such payments to continue.
    • 
    
  • Technology: the Gecko engine; "Quantum" (2017) rebuilt much of it with parallel code partly written in Rust, a language Mozilla created. Licence: Mozilla Public License 2.0.
    • 
    
  • Today: Firefox is the main browser not built on Google's Chromium engine — important for keeping web standards open, though its share is now small.
    • 
  

  
Thunderbird (1.0 in December 2004) is the matching e-mail client. Mozilla stepped back from it in 2012; volunteers kept it alive, a subsidiary (MZLA Technologies) took it over in 2020, and donations now fund a paid team. A redesigned version arrived in 2023 and an Android app (from K-9 Mail) in 2024.

10Lessons for engineers

  
    
    
  1. Archive in open formats. Test reports and data should be readable in 30 years — ODF, PDF/A, CSV and HDF5 outlive applications.
    2. 
    
  2. A community can outlast its owner. The LibreOffice fork shows why the licence matters more than the company.
    3. 
    
  3. Standards need more than one implementation. Firefox keeps the web from being defined by a single engine.
    4. 
    
  4. Know the licence before you reuse code. MIT lets you do almost anything; GPL requires sharing your changes when you distribute.
    5.
