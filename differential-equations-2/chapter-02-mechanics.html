Mechanics and Rotor Vibration

Newton's second law in ten disguises: falling with drag, a projectile, the harmonic, damped and base-excited oscillators, the large-swing pendulum, Duffing's hardening spring, Mathieu's parametric pumping, and two rotor-dynamics classics — the Jeffcott rotor and oil whirl in journal bearings.

◆In this chapter

  • Write m·x″ = ΣF for particles and rotors and spot linear versus nonlinear terms
  • Find terminal velocity, ranges, natural frequencies and transmissibility
  • See how amplitude changes frequency in nonlinear oscillators
  • Understand parametric resonance (Mathieu) and why gear-mesh stiffness variation matters
  • Explain critical speeds, self-centring and the 2× critical-speed limit of plain bearings

Every equation here comes from F = m·a. The art is in the forces: drag ∝ v², spring ∝ x or x³, damping ∝ v, unbalance ∝ Ω², and fluid-film forces that push sideways. Part I's Days 5–6 and 10 give the methods; here they meet real machines.

11Free fall with air drag

ODEnonlinearfirst orderNewton, Principia 1687
m·dv/dt = m·g − ½·ρ·Cd·A·v² → v = vt·tanh(g·t/vt), vt = √(2mg/(ρCdA))

v speed · ρ air density 1.225 kg/m³ · CdA drag area · vt terminal velocity

Gravity is constant; quadratic drag grows with speed until it balances weight. The speed then levels off at the terminal velocity. Quadratic drag applies at high Reynolds number (anything bigger than a dust grain moving faster than a walk); tiny particles in oil follow linear Stokes drag instead (equation 45).

A skydiver: m = 80 kg, C_d·A = 0.5 m². Terminal speed and time to reach 95 % of it?v_t = √(2 × 80 × 9.81/(1.225 × 0.5)) = √2563 = 50.6 m/s (182 km/h) tanh(x) = 0.95 → x = 1.832 → t = 1.832 × 50.6/9.81 = 9.5 s Head-down (C_d·A ≈ 0.15) gives 92 m/s — that is how speed skydivers reach 500 km/h.
Where you meet itParachutes and skydiving, ballistics, falling objects and drop tests, raindrops, cars coasting (with rolling resistance added — equation 77).

12Projectile with air drag

ODEnonlinearsystemBashforth 1860s; ballistic tables
x″ = −k·|v|·x′ y″ = −g − k·|v|·y′ k = ρCdA/(2m), |v| = √(x′² + y′²)

x, y position · k drag parameter (1/m)

Without drag the path is a parabola and 45° gives the longest range. Drag couples the two directions through the speed |v|: the trajectory becomes lopsided — a long climb and a steep fall — and the best launch angle drops below 45°. There is no closed-form solution; this was one of the first problems ever put on a computer (ENIAC, 1946, firing tables).

A cricket ball (m = 0.16 kg, d = 72 mm, C_d = 0.5) thrown at 30 m/s and 45°. Range with and without drag?k = 1.225 × 0.5 × 4.07×10⁻³/(2 × 0.16) = 0.0078 per m No drag: R = v²/g = 900/9.81 = 91.7 m With drag (numerical): R = 60.7 m, flight time 3.8 s — a third of the range lost Best angle with drag ≈ 42°, giving 61.0 m.
Where you meet itBallistics and artillery tables, sports science (golf, cricket, javelin), water jets from fire hoses, sprinkler design, debris throw distances in safety studies.

13Simple harmonic oscillator

ODElinearsecond orderHooke 1678; Huygens 1673 · Part I, Day 5: The spring–mass–damper
m·x″ + k·x = 0 → x = A·cos(ωnt + φ), ωn = √(k/m)

x displacement · k stiffness · m mass · ωn natural frequency (rad/s)

The most important equation in physics: any system near a stable equilibrium behaves like a mass on a spring for small motions. Energy sloshes between kinetic ½mv² and potential ½kx², the total constant. The frequency does not depend on the amplitude — which is what makes pendulum clocks and quartz watches keep time.

A pendulum clock beats seconds (period 2 s). How long is the pendulum?For small swings ω = √(g/ℓ), T = 2π√(ℓ/g) = 2 s → ℓ = g·(T/2π)² = 9.81/π² = 0.994 m
A 2 kg sensor bracket deflects 0.5 mm under its own weight. Its natural frequency?k = m·g/δ → ω_n = √(g/δ) = √(9.81/0.0005) = 140 rad/s → f_n = 22.3 Hz (Handy rule: f_n ≈ 15.8/√δ_mm Hz.)
Where you meet itClocks, tuning forks, quartz crystals, LC tanks, molecular vibrations, ear drums, building sway — and every small-amplitude model of a stable equilibrium.

14Damped free vibration

ODElinearsecond orderRayleigh, Theory of Sound 1877 · Part I, Day 5: The spring–mass–damper
m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = 0 ζ = c/(2√(km)), ωd = ωn·√(1 − ζ²)

c damping coefficient (N·s/m) · ζ damping ratio · ωd damped natural frequency

Add a force opposing velocity and the oscillation decays inside an envelope e−ζωnt. Under-damped (ζ < 1) rings; critically damped (ζ = 1) returns fastest without overshoot; over-damped creeps. Part I, Day 5 has the full treatment and the log-decrement test.

Size the shock absorber for one wheel of a car: quarter-car sprung mass 300 kg, spring 20 kN/m, target ζ = 0.3.c = ζ·2√(k·m) = 0.3 × 2√(20000 × 300) = 0.3 × 4899 = 1470 N·s/m f_n = √(k/m)/2π = √66.7/6.283 = 1.30 Hz — a comfortable body bounce
Where you meet itVehicle suspensions, door closers, instrument movements, machine foundations, building dampers, ship roll, measuring damping by tap tests on brackets and casings.

15Base excitation (seismic) response

ODElinearsecond ordervibration isolation theory, 1920s · Part I, Day 6: Forcing and resonance
m·x″ + c·(x′ − y′) + k·(x − y) = 0 |X/Y| = √(1 + (2ζr)²)/√((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²)

y(t) motion of the base (floor, road, vehicle) · x motion of the mass · r = ω/ωn

Here the support moves, not a force on the mass. The spring and damper drag the mass along. The ratio of motions is the same transmissibility as on Part I Day 6: amplification near r = 1, isolation only above r = √2. Seismometers and accelerometers use the same equation the other way round: well above (seismometer) or well below (accelerometer) their own resonance.

A precision balance is isolated with mounts giving f_n = 5 Hz, ζ = 0.05. The floor shakes at 25 Hz with 0.1 mm amplitude. How much reaches the balance?r = 25/5 = 5, 2ζr = 0.5 |X/Y| = √(1 + 0.25)/√(24² + 0.25) = 1.118/24.0 = 0.047 → 0.1 mm × 0.047 = 4.7 µm
Where you meet itIsolation of instruments and test rigs from floor vibration, vehicle ride (road = base input), earthquake response of buildings, accelerometer and seismometer design, packaging for transport.

16The nonlinear pendulum

ODEnonlinearsecond orderGalileo 1602; Huygens; Legendre (elliptic integrals) · Part I, Day 10: Nonlinear dynamics
θ″ + (g/ℓ)·sin θ = 0 T = 4√(ℓ/g)·K(sin(θ₀/2))

θ swing angle · ℓ length · K complete elliptic integral of the first kind

sin θ ≈ θ only for small swings. For larger amplitudes the restoring torque grows more slowly than θ, so the pendulum takes longer: the period depends on amplitude — Galileo's "isochronism" is only approximate. Beyond 180° the pendulum goes over the top and spins.

A 1 m pendulum: small-swing period, and the period when released from 90°?T₀ = 2π√(ℓ/g) = 2.006 s 90°: K(sin 45°) = 1.8541, T = 4√(1/9.81) × 1.8541 = 2.368 s — 18 % longer Small-angle rule: T ≈ T₀(1 + θ₀²/16) gives 2.315 s (θ₀ = 1.571) — already 2 % off
Where you meet itClock escapements (keep the swing small and constant), cranes with swinging loads, ship roll at large angles, Segway and rocket balancing (inverted pendulum), seismometer pendulums.

17Duffing oscillator

ODEnonlinearsecond orderDuffing 1918 · Part I, Day 11: Chaos
x″ + δ·x′ + α·x + β·x³ = γ·cos ωt backbone: ω² ≈ α + ¾·β·A²

β > 0 hardening spring · β < 0 softening · A amplitude

A spring that stiffens with stretch (rubber mounts, thin plates, bolted joints, preloaded bearings) makes the natural frequency rise with amplitude. Near resonance the response curve bends over: for one forcing frequency there can be two stable amplitudes, and slowly sweeping the frequency makes the amplitude jump — up on the way down, down on the way up. Part I, Day 11 shows its chaotic side.

Where the backbone formula comes from

Try x ≈ A cos ωt. Then x³ = A³(¾ cos ωt + ¼ cos 3ωt). Keeping the first harmonic (harmonic balance) gives −ω²A + αA + ¾βA³ = 0 for free vibration, so ω² = α + ¾βA². The neglected cos 3ωt term is why nonlinear vibration shows harmonics in a spectrum.

A hardening mount with α = 1, β = 0.1 (units of ω_n = 1). Natural frequency at amplitude 2?ω² ≈ 1 + 0.75 × 0.1 × 4 = 1.3 → ω = 1.14 — 14 % above the small-amplitude value
Where you meet itJump phenomena in rubber-mounted equipment, MEMS resonators, nonlinear energy harvesters, ship roll, cable vibration, preloaded rolling bearings, bistable (snap-through) structures.

18Mathieu equation (parametric resonance)

ODElineartime-varyingMathieu 1868
x″ + c·x′ + (δ + ε·cos t)·x = 0 first instability near δ = ¼ (pumping at twice the natural frequency)

δ = (ωn/ωpump)² · ε depth of the stiffness variation · c damping

Nothing pushes the mass — instead its stiffness wobbles. If the stiffness varies at about twice the natural frequency, tiny motions grow exponentially. A child pumps a swing by standing and squatting twice per cycle; a pendulum whose pivot is shaken vertically can even be stabilised upside down (Kapitza). In machines it explains instability from time-varying stiffness: gear meshes, cracked shafts, and two-pole rotors.

Stability chart (Ince–Strutt diagram)

Floquet theory says solutions are eμt·(periodic). Instability tongues start at δ = n²/4 (n = 1, 2, 3 …) and widen as ε grows; the first, around δ = ¼, has edges δ ≈ ¼ ± ε/2. Damping lifts the tongues off the δ axis, so a minimum ε is needed before growth begins: roughly ε > 2c for the first tongue.

A rotor with unequal shaft stiffness in two directions has ω_n = 50 Hz. At what shaft speed does parametric instability appear?A two-pole stiffness variation repeats twice per revolution: pumping at 2Ω Instability when 2Ω ≈ 2ω_n → Ω ≈ ω_n = 50 Hz = 3000 rpm — plus the familiar "half critical" response at 1500 rpm from gravity excitation.
Where you meet itSwing pumping, Faraday waves on a shaken liquid, ship parametric rolling in head seas, gear-mesh stiffness variation (equation 27), cracked-shaft diagnostics, quadrupole ion traps (Paul trap).

19Jeffcott rotor (unbalance response)

ODElinearrotor dynamicsFöppl 1895; Jeffcott 1919 · Part I, Day 6: Forcing and resonance
m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = m·e·Ω²·cos Ωt m·y″ + c·y′ + k·y = m·e·Ω²·sin Ωt
|r|/e = (Ω/ωn)² / √((1 − (Ω/ωn)²)² + (2ζΩ/ωn)²)

x, y shaft-centre displacement · e mass eccentricity (unbalance) · Ω running speed · ωn = √(k/m) critical speed

A disc on a flexible shaft with a small unbalance whirls in a circle at running speed. The whirl radius peaks at the critical speed ωn, where the phase lag is 90°. Above it the phase passes 180°: the mass centre moves inside, and the disc spins about its own centre of mass — the rotor self-centres, which is why turbines run happily far above their first critical speed if they get through it quickly.

A 20 kg disc at the middle of a 25 mm steel shaft, 600 mm between simple bearings. Critical speed? Whirl at 3000 rpm with e = 20 µm (balance grade G6.3)?I = π d⁴/64 = 1.92×10⁻⁸ m⁴, k = 48EI/L³ = 48 × 210e9 × 1.92e-8/0.216 = 8.95×10⁵ N/m ω_n = √(k/m) = 211.5 rad/s → 2020 rpm At 3000 rpm: r = 314.2/211.5 = 1.486 → |r| = e·r²/(r² − 1) = 20 × 2.21/1.21 = 36.6 µm (light damping) Running at 1.5× critical, the shaft whirls with less than twice the eccentricity.
Where you meet itTurbines, compressors, fans, spindles, centrifuges, gearbox and accessory-drive shafts, balancing grades (ISO 21940), run-up and coast-down tests with Bode plots.

20Oil whirl and whip (cross-coupled stiffness)

ODElinearrotor dynamicsNewkirk 1925; Lund 1960s
m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x + kxy·y = 0 m·y″ + c·y′ + k·y − kxy·x = 0 kxy ≈ c·Ω/2

kxy cross-coupled (tangential) stiffness from the oil film · Ω shaft speed

A plain journal bearing's oil film turns at about half the shaft speed. Push the shaft sideways and the film pushes back at right angles — forward along the whirl. This cross-coupled force feeds energy into a forward whirl at the natural frequency. Damping drains energy at a rate ∝ ωn; the film feeds it at a rate ∝ Ω/2. When Ω exceeds about 2ωn feeding wins: the orbit grows (oil whip) and locks at the critical frequency however fast the shaft turns.

Stability threshold

With z = x + iy the two equations become m·z″ + c·z′ + (k − i·cΩ/2)·z = 0. Try z = eiωt on the stability boundary: real part −mω² + k = 0 → ω = ωn; imaginary part cω − cΩ/2 = 0 → Ω = 2ωn. Real bearings have stiffness that also changes with speed, so the onset is usually a little below 2× critical, with whirl at 0.42–0.48× running speed before it.

A compressor rotor on plain bearings has its first critical speed at 2400 rpm. Highest safe speed? What would the spectrum show above it?Onset ≈ 2 × 2400 = 4800 rpm (often 4000–4500 rpm in practice) At 6000 rpm: a large sub-synchronous peak at ≈ 2400 rpm (40 Hz), i.e. 0.40× running speed, that does not move as speed increases — the classic oil-whip signature. Cure: tilting-pad, lemon-bore or pressure-dam bearings, which have almost no cross-coupling.
Where you meet itHigh-speed turbomachinery, turbochargers on floating-ring bearings, generators, aircraft accessory gearbox shafts with fluid-film supports; also steam whirl in seals (same mechanism, different source).
