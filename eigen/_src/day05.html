The most famous engineering eigenproblem: K φ = ω² M φ. Every structure and machine has as many natural frequencies as it has degrees of freedom, each with its own mode shape. Find them and you know which running speeds, gear-mesh frequencies and gusts to stay away from.

  • Compute natural frequencies and mode shapes of 2- and n-degree-of-freedom systems
  • Interpret mode shapes: nodes, rigid-body modes, mass-normalisation

    1From Newton's law to an eigenproblem

    For n masses joined by springs, Newton's second law for every mass at once is

    M x″ + K x = 0 M = mass matrix (diagonal here), K = stiffness matrix (symmetric)

    Look for motions in which every mass moves in step, at one frequency, keeping a fixed shape: x(t) = φ·cos(ωt). Substituting, x″ = −ω²x:

    K φ = ω² M φ

    This is a generalised eigenproblem: the eigenvalues λ = ω² are the squares of the natural (angular) frequencies, the eigenvectors φ are the mode shapes. With a diagonal M you can turn it into an ordinary symmetric problem by scaling, M⁻½ K M⁻½ y = ω² y, so the Day 4 theorem guarantees real, positive ω² and a full set of modes.

    Example 1 — two equal floors (a two-storey shear frame), m = 2000 kg, k = 1500 kN/m per storeyM = m·[1 0; 0 1], K = k·[2 −1; −1 1] (bottom storey spring to ground, top one between floors) det(K − ω²M) = 0 → (2 − μ)(1 − μ) − 1 = 0 with μ = ω²m/k μ² − 3μ + 1 = 0 → μ = 0.382, 2.618 √(k/m) = √(1.5e6/2000) = 27.39 rad/s ω₁ = √0.382 × 27.39 = 16.9 rad/s → f₁ = 2.69 Hz, shape φ₁ = (1, 1.618) both floors same way ω₂ = √2.618 × 27.39 = 44.3 rad/s → f₂ = 7.05 Hz, shape φ₂ = (1, −0.618) floors opposite

    The golden ratio is no accident — it is the same characteristic equation as Fibonacci's. Notice the pattern that holds for every system: the lowest mode has no sign change, each higher mode adds one more node, and higher modes are stiffer ways to deform, so they are faster.

    3A drivetrain: torsional modes and a zero eigenvalue

    Exactly the same mathematics applies to rotation. Replace masses by inertias J and springs by shaft torsional stiffnesses kₜ.

    Example 3 — motor driving a dynamometer through a test shaftMotor rotor J₁ = 0.5 kg·m², dynamometer J₂ = 0.2 kg·m², shaft kₜ = 5000 N·m/rad K = kₜ[1 −1; −1 1], M = diag(J₁, J₂) det(K − ω²M) = ω²·[J₁J₂ω² − kₜ(J₁ + J₂)] = 0 ω₁ = 0 → rigid-body rotation: the whole line just turns (φ = (1, 1)) ω₂ = √(kₜ(J₁ + J₂)/(J₁J₂)) = √(5000 × 0.7 / 0.1) = 187 rad/s = 29.8 Hz Mode 2: φ = (1, −J₁/J₂) = (1, −2.5) — the light dyno swings 2.5 × as far as the motor, in opposition. Danger: a torque pulsation at 29.8 Hz — e.g. 1787 rpm × 1 per rev, or 894 rpm × 2 per rev.

    A free–free system (nothing tied to ground) always has a zero eigenvalue: K is singular because turning everything together stores no energy. On a test rig this is why torsional resonance checks list "mode 1 = 0 Hz" and start counting from mode 2.

    Where you meet itCritical speeds of rotors, torsional vibration of engine–gearbox–generator trains, gear-mesh excitation of gearbox housings, floor vibration from footfall, wind- and earthquake-response of buildings (codes ask for modes with ≥ 90 % mass participation), aircraft wing flutter (Day 6), guitar bodies and loudspeaker cones, the ANSYS / NASTRAN "modal analysis" step that precedes every dynamic FE study.

    4Why modes are so useful: decoupling

    Mode shapes are orthogonal with respect to M and K: φᵢᵀMφⱼ = 0 and φᵢᵀKφⱼ = 0 for i ≠ j (the Day 4 proof with M inserted). So if you write the motion as a sum of modes, x = Σ qᵢ(t)φᵢ, the coupled equations split into n independent single-degree-of-freedom oscillators:

    mᵢ qᵢ″ + kᵢ qᵢ = φᵢᵀ F(t) mᵢ = φᵢᵀMφᵢ, kᵢ = φᵢᵀKφᵢ, ωᵢ² = kᵢ/mᵢ

    Every complicated vibration is a superposition of simple ones. A force at frequency Ω pumps energy mostly into the mode with ωᵢ closest to Ω, and only if φᵢᵀF ≠ 0 — a force applied at a node of a mode cannot excite that mode. This is the whole basis of experimental modal analysis (impact hammer, accelerometers, FRF curve-fitting) and of tuned-mass dampers, which add a small mass–spring tuned to the troublesome mode.

    Proof of M-orthogonality

    Kφᵢ = ωᵢ²Mφᵢ and Kφⱼ = ωⱼ²Mφⱼ. Multiply the first by φⱼᵀ and the second by φᵢᵀ: φⱼᵀKφᵢ = ωᵢ² φⱼᵀMφᵢ and φᵢᵀKφⱼ = ωⱼ² φᵢᵀMφⱼ. K and M are symmetric, so the left sides are equal and φⱼᵀMφᵢ = φᵢᵀMφⱼ. Subtracting: (ωᵢ² − ωⱼ²)φᵢᵀMφⱼ = 0. If ωᵢ ≠ ωⱼ then φᵢᵀMφⱼ = 0, and then φᵢᵀKφⱼ = 0 too.
