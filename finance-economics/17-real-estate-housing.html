Real Estate & Housing

A home is the biggest purchase most families ever make, and India's largest store of household wealth. Covers why city homes cost so much, whether to rent or buy, what to check before signing, and the economics of housing markets. Pick your level.

Level 1 · For school students

Land and buildings are called real estate. A home is a need, but in big cities it is also very expensive. Let's see why.

1What is real estate?

Real estate means land and anything built on it: houses, flats, shops, offices, factories and farms. It is "real" because you can't move it.

A family can own a home (buy it, often with a loan) or rent one (pay the owner every month to live there). Both are sensible choices; which is better depends on prices, rents, jobs and plans.

3Why are city homes so expensive?

📍 Land is limitedEveryone wants to live near jobs, schools and metro stations, but there is only so much land there.
🏗️ Building rulesRules limit how tall buildings can be, so less housing fits on scarce land.
💼 Jobs pull people inCities like Bengaluru and Mumbai attract workers, so demand keeps rising.
🧱 CostsCement, steel, labour, approvals and taxes all add to the price.

4Helping families get homes

The government's Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana has helped build over 1 crore homes in cities and more in villages, giving money or cheaper loans to poorer and middle-income families. Rules called RERA protect buyers from builders who delay or cheat.

Level 2 · For working people

The rent-or-buy decision, what a home really costs, the checks before you sign, and the tax rules. This is educational only, not financial, legal or tax advice.

1Calculator: rent or buy?

Each month, whoever spends less (owner: EMI + upkeep; renter: rent) invests the difference at your return. The renter also invests the down payment and buying costs from day one. Owner wealth = home value − loan left − 2% selling cost. Tax benefits are ignored. In Indian metros, rent is only about 2–3% of the price a year, which often favours renting financially; owning buys security and freedom from landlords.

A common affordability guide: a home price below about 5 times annual household income, and EMIs below about 35–40% of take-home pay.

3Checklist before you sign

  • RERA registration of the project (state RERA website): check the approved plan, timeline, litigation and complaints. Builders must keep 70% of buyers' money in a separate account.
  • Carpet area is the usable floor area; RERA requires pricing on carpet area, not "super built-up".
  • Title and approvals: clear title (30-year search), encumbrance certificate, approved building plan, commencement certificate, and for ready homes the occupancy certificate.
  • Builder track record: delivery history matters more than brochures; stalled projects have trapped lakhs of buyers.
  • Loan sanction first, then booking; compare banks and housing finance companies.

4Home loans and tax

  • Home loans are linked to the repo rate (Topic 02). Your rate = benchmark + spread, so a better credit score (Topic 16) earns a lower spread. There is no prepayment penalty on floating-rate loans.
  • Prepaying early saves the most interest, because early EMIs are mostly interest.
  • Tax (old regime): up to ₹2 lakh interest deduction on a self-occupied home (Section 24(b)) and principal under the ₹1.5 lakh Section 80C limit. The new regime (Topic 06) gives no benefit for self-occupied homes, but interest on let-out property can still be set against rent.
  • PMAY-U 2.0: eligible EWS/LIG/MIG first-time buyers can get an interest subsidy (about 4% on the first ₹8 lakh of the loan, subject to limits).
  • Capital gains: profit on sale after 2 years is long-term; reinvesting in another home can save tax (Section 54).

5Real estate as an investment

ProsTangible, can be bought with borrowed money, rental income, inflation hedge over long periods.
ConsIlliquid, high transaction costs, concentration in one asset, low rental yield (2–3%), title and tenant risks.
REITsReal estate investment trusts (listed on NSE/BSE) give a share of office and retail rents from a few hundred rupees, with regular payouts and easy exit.

6Housing and the country

  • Construction is one of India's largest employers, mostly informal. Real estate linkages (cement, steel, paint, furniture) make it a major driver of growth.
  • Physical assets (land, housing, gold) make up most Indian household wealth, far more than financial assets.
  • The market has shifted towards premium homes. Sales of homes under ₹40–50 lakh have shrunk in big cities, even as unsold stock builds up.

Level 3 · Review for experts

User cost and price-to-rent, supply elasticity and land-use regulation, housing cycles and credit, and India's housing policy questions.

1User cost of housing: calculator

User cost u = P·(r + τ + δ + m − g) In equilibrium: rent R = u → P/R = 1 / (r + τ + δ + m − g) r = after-tax cost of funds, τ = property tax, δ = depreciation, m = maintenance, g = expected capital gain

Poterba (1984); Himmelberg, Mayer and Sinai (2005). Low yields can be "justified" only by high expected appreciation g. When g is extrapolated from recent gains, price-to-rent ratios become fragile.

2Supply, land and regulation

  • The price level depends on demand and on supply elasticity (Glaeser, Gyourko and Saiz). Inelastic supply turns demand shocks into price, not quantity.
  • Indian cities have historically low floor space index (FSI) limits, rent control legacies, slow approvals and unclear land titles, all of which restrict formal supply and push growth to the periphery and informal settlements (Bertaud).
  • Reforms: tradeable development rights, transit-oriented higher FSI (Mumbai, Bengaluru metro corridors), digitised land records (DILRMP) and conclusive titling are the main levers.

3Housing cycles and credit

  • Housing is the most credit-sensitive sector. Cycles amplify through collateral values (Kiyotaki–Moore) and bank balance sheets; mortgage credit booms precede many crises (Jordà, Schularick and Taylor).
  • India's 2013–19 slump came from unsold inventory, builder leverage, demonetisation, GST transition and the IL&FS-triggered NBFC funding shock. Recovery after 2021 was led by lower rates and premium demand.
  • Macroprudential tools: loan-to-value caps (75–90% by ticket size) and housing risk weights; India's household mortgage-to-GDP ratio (around 11%) remains low by international standards.

4The rental market

  • A large share of urban households rent, many informally, with weak contracts. The Model Tenancy Act (2021) offers a template (deposits capped, rent authorities), but adoption by states is slow.
  • Institutional rental (co-living, build-to-rent) and Affordable Rental Housing Complexes (ARHC, PMAY-U 2.0) aim to serve migrants. Low yields make purely private rental supply hard to finance.

5Housing, wealth and inequality

  • Housing is the main asset of the middle class and a key driver of wealth inequality (Topic 13). Rising urban prices transfer wealth from young renters to older owners.
  • Stamp duties discourage transactions and mobility. Undervaluation and cash components persist, despite circle rates and Section 56/50C rules.
  • Shortage estimates are dominated by congestion and obsolescence among low-income households, not by a lack of high-end supply. Unsold premium stock coexists with shortages at the bottom.

6Review checklist: open questions

  1. Are current metro price-to-rent ratios consistent with plausible long-run g?
  2. Which land-use reforms (FSI, titling, approvals) would most raise affordable supply?
  3. Should stamp duty be replaced with a recurring property tax?
  4. How can rental housing be made bankable for institutions?
  5. What is the right role of PMAY subsidies against supply-side reform?
