The Practice of Management

Peter F. Drucker · first published 1954 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Management is a distinct, learnable discipline whose job is to make an enterprise perform by serving customers and setting clear objectives.

Summary

Peter Drucker wrote this book when management was barely recognised as a subject of its own. He argues that managers are the basic resource of any business, and that their work can be studied, taught and improved like any other profession. The book is often credited with turning management into a discipline.

Drucker starts with the purpose of a business. It is not to make a profit, he says; profit is a test of performance and a cost of staying in business. The real purpose is to create a customer. From this follow the two core functions of every enterprise: marketing, which starts from what customers value, and innovation, which supplies new and better products and methods.

He urges leaders to keep asking three questions: what is our business, what will it be, and what should it be. The answers are rarely obvious and must come from the customer's point of view rather than the company's.

Because performance has many sides, Drucker proposes setting objectives in several key areas, including market standing, innovation, productivity, resources, profitability, the development of managers, the performance of workers and public responsibility. Chasing profit alone, he warns, leads to short-term thinking.

His best-known idea is management by objectives and self-control. Each manager should help set goals that support the wider business, then measure their own progress against them. Direction comes from shared goals, not from constant orders.

Drucker describes three linked jobs of management: running the business, managing managers, and managing workers and work. He also stresses an organisation's spirit, which shows in high standards, recognising strengths and placing character above cleverness when choosing leaders.

The lasting message is that management is a practice judged by results, built on clear purpose and well-chosen goals.

289 words

One-line takeaway: Define the business from the customer's view, set objectives across every key area, and let managers steer themselves against shared goals.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. According to Drucker, what is the purpose of a business?

    Answer B. Drucker defines purpose from outside the firm: a business exists to create and serve customers.

  2. How does Drucker view profit?

    Answer A. He treats profit as a measure and a necessity, not as the reason a business exists.

  3. Which two functions does Drucker call the basic functions of a business?

    Answer D. Because the purpose is to create a customer, the defining functions are marketing and innovation.

  4. From whose point of view should 'What is our business?' be answered?

    Answer C. Drucker insists the business is defined by what the customer buys and values.

  5. Why does Drucker recommend objectives in several key areas instead of one?

    Answer A. Focusing on one measure such as profit encourages short-term thinking at the cost of the whole.

  6. Which of these is one of the key areas for objectives that Drucker lists?

    Answer D. Market standing appears alongside innovation, productivity, resources and profitability among his key areas.

  7. What is the core idea of management by objectives and self-control?

    Answer B. Shared goals and self-measurement replace control by command.

  8. Which set describes Drucker's three jobs of management?

    Answer C. Drucker frames management as these three linked tasks.

  9. When choosing leaders, what does Drucker place above cleverness?

    Answer D. He argues the spirit of an organisation depends on leaders of sound character.

  10. What is the book's broad contribution to the field?

    Answer A. The book is widely seen as establishing management as a practice open to study and improvement.
