Ten first-order equations that appear everywhere: growth and decay, decay chains, cooling, logistic and Gompertz growth, RC and RL circuits, draining tanks, mixing tanks and loans. One state, one rate law — yet they run medicine, electronics, chemistry and finance.
  • Recognise the "rate ∝ amount" and "rate ∝ distance from target" patterns at a glance
  • Solve each equation and read its time constant or half-life
  • Work real problems: dating, forensic cooling, debounce circuits, tank drain times, loan payments
  • Know when a first-order model is enough and when it is not

    All ten share the form y′ = f(t, y) with one state. Seven of them are linear (Part I, Day 2) and their solutions are exponentials; three — logistic, Gompertz and Torricelli — are nonlinear but still separable. Read the time constant of each: it is the one number that tells you how fast the process happens.

     dy/dt = k·y → y(t) = y₀·ekt y amount · k rate constant (per unit time; k > 0 growth, k < 0 decay) · doubling time ln 2/k, half-life ln 2/|k|

    The simplest law in nature: the rate of change is proportional to how much there is. Each unit of the quantity produces (or loses) more of itself at the same rate — bacteria divide, interest earns interest, nuclei decay independently. The signature is a constant doubling or halving time, and a straight line when ln y is plotted against t.

The same equations describe any chain of first-order steps — a drug and its metabolite, a pollutant and its breakdown product.
