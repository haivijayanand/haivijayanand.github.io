Blue Ocean Strategy

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne · first published 2005 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Lasting growth comes less from beating rivals in crowded markets than from creating uncontested market space where competition becomes irrelevant.

Summary

Kim and Mauborgne, professors at INSEAD, divide the business world into two kinds of space. Red oceans are existing industries with known boundaries, where firms fight over a fixed pool of demand and profits shrink as the water fills with rivals. Blue oceans are markets not yet created, where demand is made rather than fought over. Drawing on many strategic moves across industries and decades, the authors argue that companies grow fastest when they stop benchmarking competitors and start making them irrelevant.

At the heart of the book is value innovation: pursuing differentiation and low cost at the same time. Conventional thinking treats these as a trade-off. The authors show that by questioning what an industry takes for granted, a firm can raise value for buyers while cutting its own costs. Cirque du Soleil is the central example. It dropped animal acts and star performers, kept the tent and the clowns, and added theatrical storylines, drawing adults who would never have bought a circus ticket.

Several practical tools support the approach. The strategy canvas plots how an industry competes across key factors, revealing where everyone offers the same thing. The four actions framework asks which factors to eliminate, reduce, raise and create, and the resulting grid keeps a firm from simply adding features. Six paths suggest where to look for new space, such as alternative industries, other strategic groups, different buyers in the chain, and complementary products.

The authors also urge firms to look beyond current customers to noncustomers, whose shared reasons for staying away can reveal large untapped demand. They recommend a fixed order for testing an idea: buyer utility first, then a price the mass market can accept, then a cost that allows profit, and finally adoption.

Execution matters too. Tipping point leadership focuses scarce resources on the people and actions with outsized influence, while fair process wins the commitment of staff who must carry the strategy out.

320 words

One-line takeaway: Instead of fighting rivals on the industry's terms, rethink which factors to eliminate, reduce, raise and create so that buyers get a leap in value at lower cost.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. In the book's terms, what is a 'red ocean'?

    Answer C. Red oceans are known industries with set boundaries, where rivals fight over the same customers and margins shrink.

  2. What does 'value innovation' mean?

    Answer A. The authors reject the usual trade-off and argue that a blue ocean move raises buyer value while lowering cost.

  3. Which company is the book's best-known example of creating a blue ocean?

    Answer D. Cirque du Soleil blended circus and theatre to attract a new audience instead of competing with traditional circuses.

  4. What is the strategy canvas mainly used for?

    Answer B. The canvas plots the factors an industry competes on and each player's offering level, exposing sameness and opportunity.

  5. Which four actions make up the four actions framework?

    Answer C. Asking what to eliminate, reduce, raise and create lets a firm rebuild buyer value and cut cost at once.

  6. Why do the authors pay special attention to noncustomers?

    Answer B. Looking at the common reasons people do not buy can unlock a much bigger market than fighting over current customers.

  7. Which of these is one of the six paths for finding new market space?

    Answer D. One path is to study industries that serve the same need in a different way and borrow what buyers value from each.

  8. In what order do the authors recommend testing a blue ocean idea?

    Answer A. An idea must first offer real utility, then a mass-market price, then a profitable cost, and finally plan for adoption.

  9. What is the main idea of tipping point leadership?

    Answer C. By focusing on a few high-influence factors, leaders can overcome hurdles to change without vast extra resources.

  10. Why do the authors stress fair process in execution?

    Answer D. Engaging people, explaining decisions and setting clear expectations earns voluntary cooperation in execution.
