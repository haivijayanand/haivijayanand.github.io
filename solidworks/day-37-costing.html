SolidWorks Course Part II · Week 6 · Day 37

Costing and Design for Cost

Know the price before you send the drawing. SOLIDWORKS Costing estimates machined, sheet-metal, weldment, cast, plastic and 3D-printed parts from the model and your shop's rates.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: Cost estimator❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Run Costing on machined and sheet-metal parts
  • Edit templates: material prices, operations, rates
  • Read the cost breakdown and the effect of batch size
  • Design for cost

2How Costing works

Evaluate ▸ Costing (SOLIDWORKS Professional and Premium) recognises features — holes, pockets, bends, cut length — and applies a template of material prices, operations, setup and cycle times and machine rates. Templates are edited in the Costing Template Editor; methods exist for machining, sheet metal, weldments, castings, plastic parts and 3D printing. Assemblies roll up part costs and add purchased-part prices.

Unit cost = material (with scrap) + Σ(cycle time × rate) + Σ(setup time × rate) ÷ batch quantity, then overhead and margin. Setup is what makes one-off parts expensive.

Even without the Costing tool you can do the same estimate in a spreadsheet — the lab does exactly that.

3Do it in SolidWorks

  1. Open the input shaft. Evaluate ▸ Costing → method Machining, template your shop's (copy the default and rename).
  2. Set stock: round bar, diameter and length with allowance; material price ₹/kg.
  3. Read the CostingManager: stock, turning, keyway milling, holes. Change the quantity 1 → 100.
  4. Edit the template rates in the Costing Template Editor (machine ₹/h, setup minutes) and rerun.
  5. Sheet-metal bracket from Day 21: method Sheet Metal — cut length, pierces, bends.
  6. Generate the Costing report (Word/Excel) and compare with a real quote.

4Lab: cost estimator

A turned and ground shaft. Edit operations and rates; slide the batch size and watch setup cost fall away. Values are examples — use your own shop rates.

5Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Calibrate the template with three or four real quotes; then trust its comparisons more than its absolute numbers.
  • Design for cost: standard bar sizes, fewer setups, standard hole sizes, generous tolerances where function allows, no grinding unless needed.
  • Tight tolerances and surface finishes drive cost more than geometry.
  • Always add tooling and pattern costs separately for castings and moulded parts.

6Practice

Cost the reducer housing as a casting and as a fully machined block from plate, at 5, 50 and 500 sets. At what quantity does the casting pattern pay for itself?
