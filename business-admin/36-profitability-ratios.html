Profitability Ratios

Profit in rupees tells you how much; profitability ratios tell you how well: how much is kept from each rupee of sales, and how much is earned on each rupee invested. The second question decides whether a business deserves more money. Pick your level.

Level 2 · For working people

Calculate margins and returns, and see which levers move them most.

1Margins and returns

Gross margin = Gross profit ÷ Revenue EBITDA margin = EBITDA ÷ Revenue Net margin = PAT ÷ Revenue ROA = PAT ÷ Total assets ROE = PAT ÷ Shareholders' equity ROCE = EBIT ÷ Capital employed (equity + total borrowings)

Kaveri Foods sells more but keeps less of each rupee: gross margin fell a point (input costs), and finance costs rose. ROCE (13.6%) is only slightly above its borrowing cost (about 9.7%): a thin cushion for adding more debt.

2Which lever moves profit most?

Start from Kaveri Foods' FY26: revenue ₹1,200 cr, variable costs (materials) ₹780 cr, fixed costs ₹300 cr (staff, overheads, depreciation), other income ₹8 cr, interest ₹30 cr: PBT ₹98 cr. Try a 5% change in each lever.

Profit levers

Price is usually the most powerful lever: a 5% price rise with no volume loss flows entirely to profit. That is why pricing discipline (Chapter 11) and cost control matter more than chasing volume.

Level 3 · For experts — a review

Value creation, invested-capital returns and the economics behind industry differences.

1ROIC vs WACC

ROIC = NOPAT ÷ Invested capital NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) Invested capital = Equity + Debt − Cash − Non-operating investments Value created per year = (ROIC − WACC) × Invested capital

2Margin × turnover

Return on assets = net margin × asset turnover. The same return can come from thin margins turned over fast (grocery retail) or fat margins turned over slowly (utilities, luxury). Curves show constant ROA; dots are illustrative industry positions.

Iso-ROA map

Persistence: competition erodes abnormal returns towards the cost of capital (mean reversion of ROE documented by Fama & French 2000), except where moats (brands, network effects, scale, switching costs) protect them.
