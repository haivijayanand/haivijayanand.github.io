Earthing (Grounding) and Lightning Protection: Electrode Resistance, Soil Resistivity and Rolling Sphere

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
EarthingGroundingLightningElectrical safety

Earthing protects people by making fault currents trip protective devices quickly and by keeping touch voltages low. It also gives a stable reference for sensitive instruments and a path for lightning and surge currents. Electrode resistance depends mostly on soil resistivity, which varies widely with moisture and season, so measurement comes first.

Key takeaways

  • Single rod resistance R ≈ (ρ/2πL)·[ln(8L/d) − 1]; doubling length lowers R much more than doubling diameter.
  • Measure soil resistivity with the Wenner four-pin method: ρ = 2π·a·R.
  • Parallel rods spaced at least their length apart reduce resistance, but not in proportion (mutual interference factor ≈ 1.2–1.5).
  • Bond all metalwork to a common earth (equipotential bonding); keep instrument earths connected to the same system at one point to avoid loops.
  • IEC 62305 lightning protection levels I–IV use rolling-sphere radii of 20, 30, 45 and 60 m and mesh sizes of 5, 10, 15 and 20 m.

2. Electrode resistance and soil resistivity

Rod: R = ρ/(2πL) · [ln(8L/d) − 1] ρ in Ω·m, L and d in m Wenner: ρ = 2π·a·R (four equally spaced pins, spacing a ≈ depth of interest) n rods in parallel (spacing ≥ L): R_n ≈ (R1/n) · (1.2 … 1.5)
SoilTypical resistivity (Ω·m)
Wet clay / marsh5–50
Loam, garden soil20–200
Sand, gravel (moist)100–1,000
Dry sand, rock1,000–10,000+

Example: ρ = 100 Ω·m, rod 3 m × 16 mm → R ≈ 33.5 Ω; 4 rods in parallel ≈ 10.9 Ω.
