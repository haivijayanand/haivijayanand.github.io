Level 1 · For school students

Money is something everyone agrees to accept. Banks are where it sleeps, and where it goes to work.

1What is money?

Money is anything that people agree to accept in exchange for goods and services.

Long ago, people bartered: a farmer swapped rice for a potter's pots. But what if the potter did not want rice? Barter only works when both people want each other's things at the same time. Money solves this problem: the farmer sells rice for money and uses the money to buy pots from anyone.

1 · To payA medium of exchange: everyone accepts it.
2 · To measureA unit of account: we can say a pen costs ₹10 and a book ₹200, and compare them.
3 · To saveA store of value: you can keep it and use it later (though inflation slowly eats it, as Topic 01 showed).

Today's notes are not backed by gold. They have value because the government declares them legal tender, and because all of us trust that others will accept them.

3What does a bank do?

🔒 Keeps money safeYour savings sit in a bank account instead of under the mattress, and they earn a little interest.
🤝 Lends moneyThe bank lends part of everyone's savings to people who need it: to build a house, start a shop or buy a tractor.
📲 Moves moneyCheques, transfers, cards and UPI move money from one account to another in seconds.

5Who makes the money?

  • Notes are issued by the Reserve Bank of India. Look at a note: it carries the Governor's signature and the promise "I promise to pay the bearer the sum of…".
  • Coins (and the ₹1 note) are issued by the Government of India and minted at government mints.
  • Most money today is not notes at all. It is bank deposits, numbers in accounts, and as you just saw, banks create much of it when they lend.
  • The RBI cannot print as much as it likes. Too much money chasing the same goods causes inflation (Topic 01).

6Keeping money safe

  • Money in an Indian bank is insured for up to ₹5 lakh per person, per bank, so even if a bank fails, small savers get their money back.
  • Never share your PIN, OTP or password, not even with someone who says they are from the bank or the police. Real banks never ask.
  • Check before you scan: a UPI PIN is needed only to pay, never to receive money.

Level 2 · For working people

India's money and banking system: what counts as money, who the banks are, how they earn, how safe your deposits are, and why credit matters to the economy. This is education, not financial advice.

The digital shift: UPI, launched in 2016, now handles more than 25 billion payments a month. Notes are still widely used, and the stock of currency has kept rising in rupee terms even as digital payments boomed: people pay digitally but still hold cash as a store of value.

3How a bank earns: a simple P&L

A bank's core income is the net interest margin (NIM): interest earned on loans and bonds minus interest paid on deposits, as a share of assets. Out of this come staff and branch costs and provisions for bad loans.

The model is simplified: deposits fund assets one-for-one, and the 3% CRR earns nothing. Fees and other income are ignored, and tax is taken at a flat 25%. Banks' actual NIMs in India are usually around 3–4%.

Right now, loans are growing faster than deposits (17.9% versus 16.6% in October 2026). Banks are competing harder for deposits, which keeps FD rates up even when the RBI is not hiking.

4Reserves, liquidity and the RBI

  • CRR, 3%: cash a bank must keep with the RBI, earning no interest. Since 16 October 2026, banks must hold at least 99% of the required CRR every day, up from 90%, to keep overnight rates close to the repo rate.
  • SLR, 18%: deposits that must be held in safe, liquid assets, mostly government securities.
  • Liquidity: when the banking system has surplus cash, overnight rates drift below the repo rate. When it is short, the RBI lends through repo auctions. Watching liquidity is as important as watching the repo rate (Topic 02).

5Your money: safety and scams

Deposit insuranceDICGC insures up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank (raised from ₹1 lakh in 2020), covering savings, current, FD and RD accounts. Large savings can be spread across banks.
Not insuredMutual funds, shares, bonds, insurance policies, and deposits with NBFCs or chit funds. Read what you are buying.
Common fraudsFake KYC-update calls, "digital arrest" threats (police, CBI or customs never arrest anyone over a video call), QR codes that ask for a PIN to "receive" money, fake investment apps.
If it happensCall the national cyber-crime helpline 1930 immediately, report at cybercrime.gov.in, and tell your bank. Speed improves the chance of freezing the money.

6Credit: loans, scores and responsibility

  • Your credit score (300–900, from bureaus such as CIBIL) summarises your repayment history. It decides whether you get a loan and at what rate.
  • On-time EMIs and card payments, low credit-card utilisation and a few long-standing accounts all help. Many hard enquiries and missed payments hurt.
  • Being a guarantor or co-borrower makes you legally responsible for the loan.
  • Check your credit report at least once a year; you are entitled to a free report from each bureau.

7Banks and the country

  • Credit fuels growth. Factories, housing, small businesses and farms all run on bank loans. Strong credit growth in 2026 has supported GDP growth of about 7–8% (Topic 03).
  • Bad loans can choke growth. After the 2000s lending boom, banks' gross bad loans (NPAs) rose to about 11% of loans by March 2018. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016), recapitalisation of public sector banks and clean-ups brought them below 3% by 2025.
  • Failures are handled, not ignored. Yes Bank (2020) was rescued by a consortium led by SBI. The PMC cooperative bank collapse (2019) pushed deposit insurance from ₹1 lakh to ₹5 lakh.
  • Financial inclusion. Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar-based KYC and UPI have brought hundreds of millions of people into the formal financial system.

8The global picture

  • 2008: US housing loans, sliced into complex securities, went bad. Lehman Brothers failed and credit froze worldwide, leading to tougher global rules (Basel III).
  • 2023: Silicon Valley Bank failed in about two days, as depositors pulled money via phone apps after losses on bonds held when interest rates rose. In the digital age, bank runs move at internet speed.
  • The dollar system: most trade and cross-border lending is in dollars, cleared through US banks and messaged over SWIFT. India is pushing rupee trade settlement and linking UPI with other countries' fast-payment systems.
  • Central bank digital currencies (like India's e-rupee) and private stablecoins are competing visions of what digital money should look like.

Level 3 · Review for experts

Theories of money, how money is created, monetary aggregates, bank fragility and regulation, India's banking cycle and digital money.

1What money is: competing theories

ViewCore claimAssociated with

Search-theoreticMoney overcomes the double-coincidence problem and limited commitment and record-keeping ("money is memory")Kiyotaki–Wright (1989); Kocherlakota (1998)

2Money creation: the multiplier and its critique

Textbook: M = m·B, m = (1 + c) / (c + r) c = currency/deposits, r = reserves/deposits Endogenous: Loans create deposits; banks then obtain reserves at the policy rate (r is not the binding constraint)
  • Endogenous money (Bank of England, "Money creation in the modern economy", 2014): commercial banks create deposits when they lend. Under interest-rate targeting the central bank supplies reserves on demand, so the multiplier is an accounting ratio, not a causal mechanism.
  • Real constraints on credit creation are profitability, capital requirements, liquidity rules (LCR, NSFR), borrower demand and the policy rate.
  • India's CRR and SLR are still binding balance-sheet costs, so reserve-requirement changes have real but modest effects on credit through the cost of funds. Daily CRR maintenance (99% from October 2026) is a liquidity-management tool, not a multiplier lever.
  • The inputs above are illustrative. Use them to see why a falling currency ratio, as digital payments spread, raises the measured multiplier.

3Aggregates, velocity and the demand for money

  • Velocity. V = PY / M. Indian M3 velocity has trended down for decades as the economy has financially deepened, which is one reason money-supply targeting gave way to multiple indicators (1998) and then inflation targeting (2016).
  • Demand for money. M/P = L(Y, i). Payment technology lowers the demand for transactions money. UPI may have raised the effective velocity of M1 even as precautionary demand for cash persisted.
  • Currency shocks: demonetisation (November 2016) and the withdrawal of the ₹2,000 note (May 2023) were natural experiments in cash demand and deposit inflows. Banks absorbed the resulting liquidity surges through the incremental CRR and reverse-repo operations.

4Why banks are fragile, and how they are regulated

ROE ≈ ROA × (Assets / Equity) Capital ratio = Tier-1 + Tier-2 capital / Risk-weighted assets
  • Maturity transformation creates fragility. Diamond–Dybvig (1983) shows that demand deposits can produce self-fulfilling runs. Deposit insurance and a lender of last resort remove the bad equilibrium but create moral hazard.
  • Basel III in India: minimum total capital of 9% of risk-weighted assets (stricter than the Basel minimum of 8%), plus a 2.5% capital conservation buffer, giving 11.5%. Liquidity rules require an LCR of at least 100% and an NSFR of at least 100%.
  • Prompt Corrective Action triggers on capital, asset quality and leverage. D-SIB surcharges apply to SBI, HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank.
  • Lessons from SVB (2023): unhedged duration risk in "safe" government bonds, concentrated uninsured deposits and digital run speed. These prompted a review of how held-to-maturity losses and deposit run-off assumptions are treated.

5India's banking cycle

  • 2004–2011 boom in infrastructure and corporate lending, followed by the "twin balance-sheet" problem of overleveraged firms and stressed banks.
  • 2015 Asset Quality Review, then gross NPAs peaking at about 11.2% in March 2018; IBC (2016); recapitalisation of public sector banks and their consolidation from 27 to 12 (2017–2020).
  • Repair (2019–2025): NPAs fell below 3%, capital buffers rebuilt, and record profits followed.
  • 2024–2026: credit outran deposits and the credit-deposit ratio rose to about 82%. The RBI argues this is not a funding constraint because investment-deposit ratios fell and capital rose. Risks to watch are unsecured retail loans, microfinance stress and concentration in NBFC lending.

6Digital money and the payments stack

  • UPI architecture: account-to-account transfers over IMPS rails, an interoperable virtual payment address, and third-party apps on top of a public NPCI switch. Zero MDR on most payments made it spread fast, but leaves its long-term financing an open question. A merchant-fee proposal was deferred in October 2026.
  • Market concentration: two apps handle the large majority of UPI volume, which is why NPCI has proposed market-share caps.
  • e₹ (CBDC): wholesale and retail pilots since late 2022. Key design choices are whether it is token- or account-based, whether it pays interest (it does not), offline capability and privacy tiers. The policy tension is between the CBDC and bank deposits (disintermediation) and between the CBDC and UPI (overlapping use).
  • Stablecoins: private dollar-pegged tokens raise concerns about currency substitution and capital flows in emerging markets. The RBI remains cautious.

7International dimension

  • Dollar funding markets: global banks' reliance on dollar wholesale funding made Fed swap lines the true global lender of last resort in 2008 and 2020.
  • Spillovers: the global financial cycle (Topic 02) runs through bank leverage. Cross-border bank lending is highly procyclical.
  • Payment sovereignty: rupee vostro accounts for trade settlement, UPI links with Singapore's PayNow and other systems, and multilateral CBDC projects (BIS mBridge, Nexus) aim to reduce friction and dependence on correspondent-banking chains.

8Review checklist: open questions

  1. With money created endogenously, what is the right role for reserve requirements in India: monetary tool, prudential buffer, or a tax on banks?
  2. Is a rising credit-deposit ratio a funding risk or a sign of efficient intermediation?
  3. Who should pay for UPI's infrastructure, and does zero MDR harm competition?
  4. Should a retail CBDC be interest-bearing? How large a share of deposits could it pull out of banks?
  5. Does deposit insurance of ₹5 lakh strike the right balance between protecting depositors and market discipline?
  6. How should regulators treat interest-rate risk in banks' bond books after SVB?
