Statistical Process Control: Control Charts

Listening to the process: common and special causes of variation, X̄–R and individuals charts, control limits, the run rules that signal trouble, and how to react — or not react — on the shop floor.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: X̄–R chart simulator❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Distinguish common-cause and special-cause variation
  • Calculate centre line and control limits for X̄–R charts
  • Apply the Western Electric / Nelson run rules
  • Know when to use I–MR, p and c charts
  • Explain why control limits are not specification limits

2Two kinds of variation

Common causeMany small, always-present sources: roll-to-roll film variation, ambient humidity, normal machine play. The process is "in control" — predictable. Only changing the system reduces it.
Special (assignable) causeSomething specific and new: a worn doctor blade, a new resin lot, a heater that failed, a new operator. It shows up as an unusual point or pattern. Find it and remove it.
Two mistakes. Reacting to common-cause noise as if it were special (tampering — adjusting the machine after every reading) increases variation. Ignoring a special cause lets defects pile up. A control chart tells you which is which.

3X̄–R chart

Take small subgroups (n = 4 or 5 consecutive pieces) at regular intervals. Plot the subgroup mean X̄ and range R.

Centre lines: X̿ = mean of X̄, R̄ = mean of R X̄ chart limits: X̿ ± A₂·R̄ R chart limits: D₃·R̄ and D₄·R̄ n = 4: A₂ 0.729, D₃ 0, D₄ 2.282 n = 5: A₂ 0.577, D₃ 0, D₄ 2.114
Signal (one of several rule sets)Meaning

4SPC in a converting plant

What to chartExtrusion: film thickness across the web and gauge profile (I–MR or X̄–R from the gauge scanner), dart impact, COF. Printing: colour ΔE for key brand colours per roll (I–MR), viscosity of inks per hour, register error. Lamination: adhesive coat weight (g/m²), bond strength after cure. Pouch making: seal strength per hour (X̄–R, n = 5 pouches), pouch dimensions, leak-test results (p chart). Charts must be at the machine and plotted by the operator, with a written reaction plan — what to do when a rule is broken.

5Worked example

Seal strength, subgroups of 5 pouches20 subgroups: X̿ = 42.0 N/15 mm, R̄ = 4.6 N X̄ limits: 42.0 ± 0.577 × 4.6 = 39.35 to 44.65 R limits: 0 to 2.114 × 4.6 = 9.72 Specification: minimum 30 N/15 mm. The process is far above spec — but subgroup 21 at X̄ = 39.0 is below the LCL: something changed (jaw temperature, film lot). Stop, find the cause, record it.

6Introducing SPC

  1. Pick one critical characteristic with a known problem — not twenty at once.
  2. Check the measurement system first (Day 16); a noisy gauge makes a noisy chart.
  3. Collect 20–25 subgroups under normal running; calculate the limits.
  4. Remove points with known special causes and recalculate.
  5. Put the chart at the machine with a reaction plan; train the operators.
  6. Review signals and actions weekly; recalculate limits only when the process has changed for good.

7Lab: X̄–R chart simulator

A process makes subgroups of five. Press P to run it; inject a mean shift, a trend or extra spread and see how fast the chart notices. The first 20 subgroups set the limits.

8Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Never put specification limits on an X̄ chart. Spec limits apply to individual pieces; control limits describe subgroup means.
  • "In control" does not mean "good" — a stable process can still make scrap. That is capability (Day 15).
  • A chart nobody reacts to is wallpaper. The reaction plan matters more than the chart.
  • Managers: don't ask for a reason for every bad day if the process is in control — you are asking people to explain noise.

9Practice

  1. Take 25 subgroups of one characteristic from your records and build an X̄–R chart.
  2. Check the four run rules. Any signals?
  3. Write a one-page reaction plan for that chart.
