The Hard Thing About Hard Things

Ben Horowitz · first published 2014 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Management books describe how to run a company when things go well; the real test of a leader is the messy, unscripted problems that have no formula.

Summary

Ben Horowitz co-founded Loudcloud, a web-hosting company that went public just as the dot-com bubble burst. He spent the following years fighting to keep it alive, selling the hosting business, rebuilding the firm as the software company Opsware, and finally selling that to Hewlett-Packard. This book turns those near-death experiences into practical advice for founders and managers.

His central point is that the hardest problems in business have no recipe. Setting goals or hiring well is easy to describe; deciding whom to lay off, how to replace a loyal friend who has outgrown the job, or what to do when cash is running out is not. Leaders must face these choices with incomplete information and no guarantee of success.

Horowitz is candid about the emotional side of leadership, which he calls the struggle. Chief executives often feel alone and afraid, and he argues that managing one's own psychology is a core part of the job. Rather than dwelling on everything that could go wrong, a leader should focus on the path forward.

Much of the book offers concrete guidance. When cutting staff, act quickly, explain the reasons honestly, and have managers speak to their own people. Tell the truth about problems, because hidden bad news erodes trust. Hire for the strengths a role needs instead of for a lack of weaknesses, and invest in training, since good managers are made, not found.

He also distinguishes between a peacetime leader, who expands an advantage and encourages broad creativity, and a wartime leader, who must respond to an existential threat with focus and tough decisions. Few people excel at both.

Underlying everything is a priority order: look after the people first, then the products, then the profits. A company that is a good place to work can survive its worst days.

299 words

One-line takeaway: There is no formula for the hardest decisions, so face them honestly, manage your own fear, and take care of your people first.

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What is the central argument behind the book's title?

    Answer B. Horowitz argues that the truly difficult situations leaders face cannot be handled by following a formula.

  2. Which company did Horowitz co-found before rebuilding it as Opsware?

    Answer C. Loudcloud was the hosting company he co-founded; after selling its hosting business it became the software firm Opsware.

  3. What does Horowitz call the painful emotional experience of running a company in crisis?

    Answer A. He uses the term 'the struggle' for the fear, isolation and doubt that leaders feel when things go badly.

  4. When laying people off, which practice does Horowitz recommend?

    Answer D. He advises acting fast once the decision is made and having each manager personally inform their team members, out of respect.

  5. How does Horowitz suggest a leader should handle bad news inside the company?

    Answer A. He argues that being honest about problems lets more people help solve them and preserves credibility.

  6. What hiring principle does the book favour?

    Answer C. Looking only for a lack of weaknesses tends to produce mediocre hires; the role's key strengths matter more.

  7. Why does Horowitz stress training employees?

    Answer B. He sees training as one of the most valuable things a company can do to raise performance and set clear expectations.

  8. A 'wartime' leader, in Horowitz's framing, is one who:

    Answer D. Wartime leadership is about survival under threat, demanding directness and tight focus.

  9. In what order does Horowitz say a company should take care of things?

    Answer C. He insists that looking after people comes first, then products, then profits.

  10. What does Horowitz identify as a key personal skill for a chief executive?

    Answer A. He describes controlling one's own fear and focus as one of the hardest and most important parts of the job.
