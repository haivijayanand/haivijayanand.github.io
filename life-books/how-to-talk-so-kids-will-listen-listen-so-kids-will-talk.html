How to Talk So Kids Will Listen & Listen So Kids Will Talk

Adele Faber and Elaine Mazlish · first published 1980 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Children cooperate more willingly and grow more confident when parents acknowledge their feelings and replace blame, threats and lectures with respectful, descriptive language.

Summary

Adele Faber and Elaine Mazlish were two mothers who attended parenting workshops inspired by the child psychologist Haim Ginott, then went on to run their own groups for parents. Their book collects what they learned into a friendly, workbook-style guide, full of cartoons, exercises and stories from real families.

The starting point is feelings. When a child is upset, the usual adult replies of denying, advising or reasoning tend to make things worse. Instead, the authors suggest listening with full attention, acknowledging with a simple word or sound, naming the feeling, and sometimes granting in fantasy what cannot be given in reality. A child who feels understood calms down faster and is more open to solving the problem.

Next comes cooperation. Rather than nagging or threatening, a parent can describe the problem, give information, say it in a single word, share their own feelings, or even leave a written note. These approaches invite children to take responsibility without feeling attacked.

The book questions punishment, arguing that it often breeds resentment rather than reflection. Alternatives include stating your expectations clearly, expressing strong disapproval without insulting the child, offering choices, showing how to make amends, and working through problems together by listing ideas and agreeing on one.

Later chapters cover encouraging independence by letting children make choices and respecting their struggles, and giving praise that describes what the child actually did rather than vague words like good or great. The final idea is to free children from labels such as lazy or shy, because children tend to live up, or down, to the roles we give them.

The tone is warm and forgiving. Parents will slip back into old habits, and the authors treat that as normal, offering practical skills that can be practised a little at a time.

297 words

One-line takeaway: This week, when your child is upset, resist the urge to fix or correct, and simply name the feeling you see before saying anything else.

  1. What is the book's central message for parents?

    Answer B. The authors show that acknowledging feelings and speaking respectfully makes children more willing to listen and cooperate.

  2. Whose work inspired the parenting workshops the authors attended?

    Answer D. Faber and Mazlish learned their approach in groups led by the child psychologist Haim Ginott.

  3. When a child is upset, which response does the book recommend?

    Answer A. Naming the feeling helps a child feel understood, while denying or advising usually escalates the upset.

  4. What does 'granting a wish in fantasy' mean?

    Answer C. Imagining aloud what the child longs for shows understanding without giving in.

  5. Which is one of the book's skills for engaging cooperation?

    Answer B. Describing what you see lets the child figure out what to do without feeling attacked.

  6. Why do the authors question punishment?

    Answer A. They argue punishment distracts children with anger and revenge instead of helping them make things right.

  7. What kind of praise does the book recommend?

    Answer D. Descriptive praise lets children see their own effort and draw their own positive conclusions.

  8. How does the book suggest encouraging a child's independence?

    Answer C. Letting children choose and acknowledging that a task is hard builds their confidence and self-reliance.

  9. What is the danger of labels like 'lazy' or 'shy'?

    Answer A. The authors show that children often act out the roles adults assign them, so freeing them from labels opens new possibilities.

  10. In the book's problem-solving method, what do parent and child do after hearing each other's feelings?

    Answer D. Joint brainstorming without judging ideas, then choosing one, gives the child a real stake in the solution.
