The Principles of Scientific Management

Frederick Winslow Taylor · first published 1911 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Work can be studied and redesigned scientifically, replacing rule of thumb with measured methods so that both employers and workers prosper.

Summary

Frederick Taylor, an engineer who rose from the shop floor of American steel works, wrote this short book to argue that the greatest waste in industry was not lost materials but lost human effort. He believed that the real aim of management should be maximum prosperity for the employer together with maximum prosperity for each worker, and that these two goals were not in conflict.

Taylor saw three reasons why output stayed low. Workers feared that producing more would cost jobs, so they deliberately held back, a habit he called soldiering. Pay systems often punished extra effort by cutting rates. And almost every trade was run by rule of thumb, with methods passed down informally and never tested.

His remedy was to treat each job as a subject for careful study. Managers should break work into its elements, time and measure them, and find the one best way to do each task, including the right tools, motions and rest periods. They should then select workers suited to the job, train them in the new method, and pay a bonus when the defined daily task was met.

He summed this up in four duties for management: develop a science for each part of the work; choose and train workers scientifically; cooperate closely with workers so the work follows that science; and share the work and responsibility more evenly, with managers taking on the planning that had been left to the men.

Taylor illustrated these ideas with experiments in handling pig iron, shovelling and inspecting bicycle bearings, where output rose sharply alongside higher wages.

He insisted that his system was not a collection of efficiency tricks but a complete change of mindset on both sides. Later critics faulted its narrow view of people, yet it laid the foundation for industrial engineering and modern management.

302 words

One-line takeaway: Study the work, find the best method, train people in it and reward them, and treat management as responsible for planning rather than leaving it to habit.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. According to Taylor, the main object of management should be:

    Answer C. He argued that the interests of employers and workers were shared and could both be served by higher productivity.

  2. What did Taylor mean by 'soldiering'?

    Answer A. Taylor used the term for the systematic holding back of output by workers who feared that working faster would hurt them.

  3. Which of the following is one of Taylor's four principles?

    Answer D. Careful selection and training of workers for the job was one of the four duties he assigned to management.

  4. What did Taylor want to replace with a scientific approach?

    Answer B. He criticised traditional, untested craft methods and wanted each element of work studied and measured.

  5. How were workers rewarded under Taylor's task system?

    Answer B. Taylor linked a clearly defined task with extra pay when that task was completed.

  6. How did Taylor propose dividing responsibility between managers and workers?

    Answer A. He wanted management to assume the planning and method design that workers had carried on their own.

  7. Which experiment is a well-known example in the book?

    Answer C. The pig-iron loading study was Taylor's best-known demonstration of large gains from a studied method.

  8. Why, according to Taylor, did many piece-rate systems fail to raise output?

    Answer D. Rate cutting taught workers that extra effort would be punished, which encouraged them to restrict output.

  9. Which criticism is most often made of Taylor's approach?

    Answer B. Later thinkers argued that it treated people as parts of a machine and overlooked motivation and social needs.

  10. What did Taylor say was essential for scientific management to work?

    Answer A. He stressed that the system was a change of mindset toward cooperation, not just a set of efficiency techniques.
