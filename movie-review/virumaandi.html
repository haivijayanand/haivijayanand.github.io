Summary

Virumaandi opens in Madras Central Prison. Angela Kaathamuthu (Rohini), a human-rights activist writing a doctoral thesis against the death penalty, interviews two men waiting to be hanged: Kothala Thevar (Pasupathy) and Virumaandi (Kamal Haasan). Both come from the same villages near Theni, and both were part of the same bloody feud.

Kothala tells his story first, painting Virumaandi as a reckless, violent man. Then Virumaandi tells his — and almost every event turns upside down. The film shows us both versions in full, and lets the audience realise how easily a court, a police file and a public can be convinced of the wrong truth.

Kamal Haasan wrote, produced, directed and co-edited it, ten years after Kuruthipunal and seventeen after Nayagan. It is his most direct argument against capital punishment.

If two men can tell the same story so differently, how can a state be certain enough to kill one of them?

Two accounts of the same story

This section reveals both versions —

Kothala Thevar’s version

Told by Pasupathy’s Kothala
  • Virumaandi, deported from Singapore, comes home to fertile land in Chinna Kolarupatti and causes trouble.
  • His raid on Nallama Nayakkar’s village leads to 24 deaths.
  • He rapes Kothala’s niece Annalakshmi and drives her to suicide.
  • His testimony sends Kothala’s men to prison.
  • Verdict implied: Virumaandi is a monster who deserves to hang.

Virumaandi’s version

Told by Kamal Haasan’s Virumaandi
  • Kothala engineered the feud to grab Virumaandi’s land.
  • Annalakshmi taught him forgiveness; he went to Nayakkar’s village to apologise — and Kothala’s men slaughtered the sleeping villagers.
  • He lied in court to protect Kothala’s family, gave his land to the victims’ families, and married Annalakshmi secretly.
  • Kothala’s gang forced her into another marriage; she killed herself.
  • His grief-driven attack on Kothala’s house brought the death sentence — on evidence falsified by Inspector Peykkaman.

Plot

The interviews

Angela arrives at Madras Central Prison to interview death-row inmates, guided by the honest Chief Jailer Jayanth (Nassar) and watched by the scheming Deputy Jailer Peykkaman.

Kothala speaks

Kothala Thevar narrates a story of land, a jallikattu bull-taming contest, a love affair and a massacre — with Virumaandi as the villain.

Virumaandi speaks

Virumaandi retells the same events with Kothala as the schemer, and himself as a hot-headed but honest man changed by his love for Annalakshmi (Abhirami).

The prison riot

Angela’s camera records Peykkaman plotting to kill Jayanth and admitting he falsified Virumaandi’s charge sheet. A riot breaks out. Virumaandi protects Angela and the footage; Jayanth’s men help them escape. Peykkaman kills Girish before Virumaandi kills him, and Kothala dies at the hands of other inmates.

The appeal

Angela presents the footage in court and petitions the President for clemency. On national television, Virumaandi and Angela ask for a swift final decision — either execute him, so he can join Annalakshmi, or free him to live in peace. The film ends without telling us which.

Two films, two directors?

Rediff’s critic said the film belongs to Kamal the director, not Kamal the actor — a fair sign of how much of it rests on its writing and structure.

Music

Ilaiyaraaja’s soundtrack is rooted in the folk music of southern Tamil Nadu — temple songs, village chants and dirges, some sung by local folk artists rather than film singers. “Annalakshmi” and “Sandiyare Sandiyare” are set in raga Shuddha Saveri.

Kamal Haasan sings on six songs himself, including the night duet “Onnavida” with Shreya Ghoshal, a lasting favourite. The audio was launched on 8 December 2003.

Technology & craft

Live jallikattu sound

Real bulls were brought onto the set for the bull-taming scenes, and their sound was recorded live using the Nuendo digital audio workstation — unusual at a time when Tamil films usually dubbed everything later.

An early try at HD

Kamal Haasan and Keshav Prakash attempted to shoot in high definition — years before digital cameras became normal in Indian cinema — but dropped it because of technical problems.

Lighting among the sheep

For the night song “Unna Vida”, the team used digital lighting, with the cinematographer hiding among a flock of sheep to light the frames.

Villages built in the city

Political protests forced the shoot out of Madurai. Art director Prabhakar rebuilt Theni villages and a full prison in Chennai; filming began on 7 July 2003 at Campa Cola Grounds.

Structure as technique

The Rashomon structure means many scenes are shown twice, from different sides. The editing — partly by Kamal Haasan himself — has to make each version convincing in turn.

The title fight

Launched in Madurai in April 2003 as Sandiyar, the film was renamed after objections from Pudhiya Tamizhagam leader K. Krishnasamy and a meeting between Kamal Haasan and Chief Minister J. Jayalalithaa.

Emotional arc across the film

Intensity (0–10) at key moments, scored for this review.

Where the emotions come from

Being made the villain. Watching Virumaandi first through Kothala’s eyes, then through his own, makes you feel how easily a man is turned into a monster on paper.
Annalakshmi. Abhirami’s Annalakshmi is the one person who changes Virumaandi, teaching him to ask forgiveness. Her forced marriage and death are the film’s heaviest blows.
The massacre. Virumaandi goes to apologise and finds a village killed in its sleep. The cruellest irony in the film is that his act of peace becomes the evidence against him.
Pasupathy’s Kothala. Charming, folksy and utterly ruthless. His version of events is so convincing that the second half lands like a slap.
A corrupt file. Peykkaman’s falsified charge sheet is a small, ordinary crime — and it is enough to send a man to the gallows. That ordinariness is what frightens.
“Hang me or free me.” The final appeal is not for mercy but for an end to waiting. It turns the death-penalty debate into something personal and unbearable.

What works

  • The two-version structure, rare in Tamil cinema, used to make a real argument rather than a gimmick.
  • Pasupathy and Kamal Haasan, each convincing in the other’s story.
  • Ilaiyaraaja’s folk-rooted songs, especially “Onnavida”.
  • A film about the death penalty that never becomes a lecture.

What doesn’t

  • Critics such as the Deccan Herald objected to the excessive violence.
  • At almost three hours, the prison-riot climax feels stretched.
  • The final appeal on television is more message than drama.

Watch it for: the jallikattu, the switch from Kothala’s story to Virumaandi’s, and Pasupathy. Watch it with: Kuruthipunal — two Kamal Haasan productions that question how the system decides who is guilty.
