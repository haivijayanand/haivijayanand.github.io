Insurance & Risk

How many people sharing a small cost protects each of them from a big loss. Covers how much life and health cover a family needs, why term insurance usually beats "insurance plus savings" plans, what changed in Indian insurance in 2025–26, and the economics of risk. Pick your level.

Level 1 · For school students

Bad things happen to someone, but we don't know to whom. Insurance means everyone shares the cost, so no one is ruined.

1What is risk?

Risk is the chance that something bad happens: falling ill, an accident, a fire, a flood, or a family's earner dying early.

Most of these are rare but very costly. A hospital stay can cost more than a family earns in a year. You cannot know in advance whether it will happen to you, but you can know roughly how often it happens to people like you.

4Kinds of insurance

🏥 HealthPays hospital bills. The government's Ayushman Bharat gives free cover to poorer families and all people aged 70 or more.
👪 LifeIf the earning parent dies, the family gets money to keep living and studying.
🛵 MotorRequired by law for every vehicle, to pay for damage to other people.
🌾 Crop and homeProtects farmers from bad weather, and homes from fire and floods.

5Why doesn't everyone just save instead?

Saving works for small, expected costs, like a new school bag. But a ₹1 lakh bill might come next month, before you have saved enough. Insurance protects you from day one. Saving and insurance work best together: save for planned things, insure against rare, big shocks.

2Calculator: how much life cover?

Needs-based method: the present value of rising expenses (a growing annuity), plus debts and goals, minus what you already have. A "10–15 times annual income" rule of thumb is a rough cross-check. Review it when your income, family or loans change.

3Term vs "insurance + savings" plans

Term insurancePays only on death during the term. There is no maturity payout, so premiums are low: a healthy 30-year-old can get ₹1 crore of cover for roughly ₹10,000–15,000 a year (varies by insurer and health).
Endowment / money-backMixes cover with savings. Cover is usually small (about 10 times the premium) and returns have typically been low, around 4–6% a year. High early surrender penalties.
ULIPsCover plus market investment. Charges and lock-ins apply; compare against term plus a mutual fund (Topic 07).

The common advice is "buy term, invest the rest": keep protection and investment separate, so each does its job. IRDAI's own report links mis-selling of complex products to complaints and early exits. Before buying, ask for the policy's internal rate of return, the surrender value table, and the exclusions.

4Health insurance: what to check

  • Sum insured suited to your city's hospital costs; medical costs have risen faster than general inflation, so review every few years.
  • Room-rent limits and co-payment: a room cap can cut your whole claim proportionately.
  • Waiting periods for pre-existing diseases (now capped at 3 years) and specific illnesses. After 5 years of continuous cover (the moratorium), a claim cannot be refused for non-disclosure except in proven fraud.
  • Network hospitals for cashless treatment, and the insurer's claim record.
  • Employer cover is not enough on its own: it ends when you leave the job. Keep a personal policy too.
  • Disclose honestly: hiding a condition is the most common reason for claim disputes.

6Insurance and the country

  • Protection gap: most Indian families are under-insured for life and health. Health spending from people's own pockets pushes many families into poverty each year (Topic 13).
  • Long-term savings: insurers (LIC is the largest) are big buyers of government bonds and infrastructure debt (Topic 08).
  • Climate risk: floods, cyclones and heatwaves raise crop and property losses. Parametric insurance, which pays automatically when rainfall or temperature crosses a trigger, is being tried in several states.

Level 3 · Review for experts

Expected utility and the demand for insurance, pooling and the law of large numbers, asymmetric information, pricing and solvency, catastrophe risk, and India's protection gap.

1Why risk-averse people buy insurance: calculator

EU = (1−p)·u(W) + p·u(W−L) CE = u⁻¹(EU) max premium π* = W − CE risk premium = π* − pL CRRA: u(w) = w^(1−γ)/(1−γ) (γ ≠ 1), u(w) = ln w (γ = 1)

Because utility is concave, the chord between the two outcomes lies below the curve. The gap between expected wealth and the certainty equivalent is what a risk-averse person will pay above the fair premium. That margin funds insurers' expenses and profit (the loading).

2Pooling and the law of large numbers

Per-member loss with n independent risks: mean = pL, s.d. = L·√(p(1−p)/n) → 0 as n → ∞
  • Pooling diversifies idiosyncratic risk only. Correlated risks (pandemics, floods, crop failure across a region) do not average away, which is why reinsurance, catastrophe bonds and government backstops exist.
  • With p = 1% and L = ₹1 lakh, the standard deviation of per-member cost falls from about ₹9,950 (n = 1) to ₹995 (n = 100) and ₹99 (n = 10,000).

3Asymmetric information

  • Adverse selection (Akerlof, 1970; Rothschild–Stiglitz, 1976): when insurers cannot tell risk types apart, high-risk buyers are over-represented, pushing prices up and low-risk buyers out (a "death spiral"). Remedies include underwriting, waiting periods, group and mandatory cover, and risk-separating menus (deductible choice).
  • Moral hazard (Arrow, 1963; Pauly, 1968): cover changes behaviour, both ex ante (less care) and ex post (more use of care). Remedies are co-payments, deductibles, room-rent limits and no-claim bonuses. India's room-rent proportionate deductions are a blunt version.
  • Supplier-induced demand in hospitals and fraud add a third layer, addressed through package rates (PM-JAY), claims analytics and IRDAI's 2025 fraud-monitoring framework.

4Pricing, profitability and solvency

Combined ratio = (claims incurred + expenses + commission) / net earned premium < 100% → underwriting profit Solvency ratio = available solvency margin / required solvency margin IRDAI minimum: 150%
  • Non-life insurers often run combined ratios above 100% and earn from investment income on float. Health is the largest non-life line in India.
  • Life insurance pricing discounts mortality and expense cash flows. Participating products share surplus through bonuses, and the opacity of their returns is a recurring consumer-protection concern.
  • IRDAI is moving towards risk-based capital and Ind AS 117 / IFRS 17-style reporting, which will change how capital and profits are measured.

5Catastrophe risk and the protection gap

  • India's natural-catastrophe losses are mostly uninsured. Reinsurance capacity (GIC Re and foreign branches) and parametric covers (for example, state and municipal heat and rainfall triggers) are expanding.
  • Crop insurance (PMFBY) shows the trade-offs: high subsidy, delays in yield estimation and claim payment, and state opt-outs. It is moving towards technology-based yield estimation.
  • Behavioural frictions (present bias, probability neglect, distrust after claim rejections) keep take-up low even at fair prices. Default enrolment and bundling (with loans, accounts and employment) raise coverage.

6Review checklist: open questions

  1. Will zero GST and 100% FDI raise penetration, or mainly change ownership and pricing at the top end?
  2. How should India regulate commission-driven mis-selling: expense caps, disclosure of returns, or fee-based advice?
  3. Should a basic health cover be mandatory for the "missing middle" between PM-JAY and employer cover?
  4. What is the right public role in catastrophe and climate risk: pools, parametric covers, or reinsurer of last resort?
  5. How will risk-based capital affect product design and LIC's dominance?
