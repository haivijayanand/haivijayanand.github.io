Nonviolent Communication

Marshall B. Rosenberg · first published 1999 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Much of the hurt between people comes from habits of judging, blaming and demanding, and we can connect far more deeply by honestly sharing what we observe, feel, need and request while listening for the same in others.

Summary

Marshall Rosenberg, a clinical psychologist who studied under Carl Rogers, spent decades teaching a way of speaking and listening that he called Nonviolent Communication. His starting point is that most of us learned language that breeds conflict. We label people, compare them, blame them, and insist that they should do things, often without noticing.

In place of these habits he offers a simple model with four parts. First, describe what actually happened, as a camera might record it, without mixing in judgment. Second, name how you feel, taking care not to dress up a thought or an accusation as a feeling. Third, identify the need that lies underneath that feeling, such as rest, respect, order or connection. Fourth, make a clear, positive and doable request.

Rosenberg stresses that other people's actions may trigger our feelings, but our feelings come from our own needs being met or unmet. Seeing this lets us take responsibility for our reactions without blaming anyone else.

He draws a sharp line between a request and a demand. If saying no would lead to guilt, blame or punishment, it was really a demand. People tend to resist demands, while freely chosen requests invite willing cooperation.

The same four parts guide listening. When someone speaks harshly, we can look past the words to the feelings and needs behind them and reflect those back, offering empathy instead of defending, fixing or advising.

The book also turns the model inward. We can treat our own mistakes with self-compassion rather than inner scolding, explore anger by tracing it back to the unmet needs beneath it, and express appreciation by naming specifically what someone did and how it made our life better.

For families, the approach offers a way to stay honest without attacking, settle everyday disputes, and to raise children who learn to name their feelings and respect the needs of others.

311 words

One-line takeaway: This week, when something at home bothers you, try saying it in four parts: what you saw, how you feel, what you need, and one clear, doable request, then listen for the feelings and needs behind the reply.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What are the four components of Rosenberg's model?

    Answer B. The model asks us to share what we observe, what we feel, what we need, and what we request.

  2. How should an observation be expressed in this approach?

    Answer C. Rosenberg asks us to separate what we actually saw or heard from our judgments about it.

  3. According to the book, what is the root cause of our feelings?

    Answer A. Others' actions may stimulate feelings, but the book holds that feelings arise from our own needs.

  4. What turns a request into a demand?

    Answer D. If refusal is met with blame or punishment, the listener rightly hears the request as a demand.

  5. Which of these is an example of a thought disguised as a feeling?

    Answer B. Saying 'I feel that you...' expresses a judgment about the other person rather than an emotion.

  6. What kind of request does Rosenberg recommend?

    Answer A. He suggests asking for specific actions people can actually take, phrased as what you want.

  7. When someone speaks to us harshly, what does the book suggest we listen for?

    Answer C. Empathic listening means hearing the feelings and needs beneath even critical messages.

  8. Which habit does Rosenberg see as getting in the way of compassionate connection?

    Answer D. He describes judging, comparing and denying responsibility as forms of communication that cut us off from compassion.

  9. How does the book suggest we handle anger?

    Answer B. Rosenberg treats anger as a signal pointing to judgments and unmet needs that can then be expressed.

  10. How does Nonviolent Communication suggest expressing appreciation?

    Answer D. The book encourages specific appreciation that names the action, our feelings, and the needs it met.
