Statistics for Test Data: Mean, Scatter, Confidence Intervals and Linear Regression

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
StatisticsRegressionConfidence intervalsTest data

Test data is noisy. Before concluding that a temperature rose or a vibration trend is real, you need to know how much scatter to expect and how confident you can be in a mean or a slope. A handful of statistical tools, used correctly, separate real changes from noise and prevent both false alarms and missed trends.

Key takeaways

  • Standard deviation s describes scatter of readings; the standard error s/√n describes uncertainty of the mean.
  • 95 % confidence interval of a mean: x̄ ± t0.975,n−1·s/√n (t ≈ 2.0 for large n, larger for small n).
  • Linear regression gives slope and intercept; report the slope with its standard error or confidence interval, not just R².
  • A trend is statistically significant at 95 % if the slope's confidence interval does not include zero.
  • Always plot residuals: curvature, outliers and changing scatter break the assumptions.

3. Cautions

  • Correlation is not causation: speed, load and temperature often change together.
  • Don't extrapolate far beyond the data range.
  • Investigate outliers before removing them; use tests such as Grubbs' only for genuinely normal data.
  • Autocorrelated data (consecutive samples from a slow process) overstates confidence: thin the data or use time-series methods.
