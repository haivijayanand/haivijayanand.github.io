Involute Spline Couplings: Standards, Fits, Load Capacity and Wear

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
SplinesDIN 5480ISO 4156Fretting

A spline transmits torque between a shaft and a hub through many teeth at once. Involute splines centre themselves under load and are easy to make with gear-cutting methods, which is why they dominate in gearboxes and drive shafts. Their usual problem is not breakage but wear: misalignment makes the teeth slide, and sliding under load causes fretting.

Key takeaways

  • Involute splines (DIN 5480, ISO 4156, ANSI B92.1) use 30° pressure angle in most standards; DIN 5480 is based on a reference diameter.
  • Flank-centred fits are self-centring under torque; diameter-centred fits are used where running accuracy matters without load.
  • Size by flank pressure and shaft shear. Only part of the teeth carry load: a load-sharing factor of 0.5–0.75 is common.
  • Misaligned splines slide axially every revolution. Crowning the male teeth, hard surfaces and good lubrication control fretting wear.
  • Oil-flooded splines wear far less than grease-packed or dry ones.

3. Load capacity checks

Flank pressure: p = 2·T / (d · z · h_w · L · K) [MPa, T in N·mm] d = pitch diameter m·z, h_w ≈ 0.9·m (working depth), L = engagement length, K = load-sharing factor (0.5–0.75; 1.0 only for perfectly aligned, accurate splines) Shaft shear: τ = 16·T / (π · d_r³) d_r ≈ root diameter Tooth shear at pitch line (rarely critical): τ_t = 4·T/(d·z·s·L·K), s = tooth thickness
Spline conditionIndicative allowable flank pressure
Fixed, hardened (≥ 50 HRC), aligned100–200 MPa
Fixed, through-hardened60–100 MPa
Flexible / sliding under load, hardened and lubricated20–40 MPa (wear-limited)
Sliding under load, unhardened5–15 MPa

Allowable values vary widely with hardness, lubrication and life. Use them as a first screen and confirm against test experience or a detailed method (e.g. Dudley's spline rating).

4. Misalignment, crowning and fretting

  • With angular misalignment θ, each tooth slides axially by roughly ±(d/2)·θ per revolution. At high speed this is millions of small strokes per hour: classic fretting conditions.
  • Crowning the male teeth (a slight barrel shape along the length) lets the coupling tilt without loading the tooth ends. It reduces edge pressure but shortens the effective contact length, so flank pressure rises.
  • Lubrication: continuous oil flow removes debris and heat and gives the lowest wear. Grease works for low-speed or intermittent duty with regular re-packing. Dry splines need hard, coated surfaces (nitriding, DLC, MoS₂ films).
  • Warning signs: red-brown oxide ("cocoa") at the spline ends, step wear on flanks, rising backlash, and a growing 1× and 2× shaft vibration component.

5. Worked example

DIN 5480 W 30 × 1.25 × 22, engagement L = 25 mm, torque T = 300 N·m, load sharing K = 0.75.

StepResult
Pitch diameter d = m·z27.5 mm
Working depth h_w ≈ 0.9·m1.125 mm
Flank pressure p47 MPa
Root diameter d_r ≈ d_B − 2.2·m27.25 mm
Shaft shear τ = 16T/(π·d_r³)76 MPa

What it shows

A flank pressure near 47 MPa is acceptable for a fixed, hardened spline but too high for a sliding, misaligned one, where 20–40 MPa is typical. For a flexible coupling at this torque, lengthen the engagement, increase the diameter, or ensure oil-flooded lubrication and hard surfaces. The shaft shear stress of 76 MPa is moderate for a hardened alloy steel.
