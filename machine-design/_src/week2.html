    How fatigue works

    In 1842 the Versailles train disaster and, later, failed railway axles led August Wöhler to test axles under rotating bending. He found that steel survives a stress forever if the stress is low enough, but breaks after a number of cycles if it is higher — even far below the yield strength.

    1 · InitiationA microscopic crack starts at a stress raiser: a fillet, a machining mark, an inclusion, corrosion pit.
    2 · PropagationThe crack grows a little each cycle, leaving beach marks (clam-shell lines) on the fracture face.
    3 · Final fractureThe remaining section is too small and breaks suddenly — a rough, fibrous zone. Its size tells you the load: small = low nominal stress.

    Fatigue gives no visible warning: no necking, no bending. That is why it is responsible for the majority of mechanical failures in service — shafts, axles, springs, bolts, gear teeth, welded frames.

    The S–N curve and endurance limit

    Plot fully reversed stress amplitude S against cycles to failure N on log–log axes. For steels the curve bends to a horizontal line at about 10⁶–10⁷ cycles: below that stress the part lasts indefinitely. That stress is the endurance limit. Aluminium and copper alloys have no knee — they always fail eventually, so their "fatigue strength" is quoted at a stated life (often 5 × 10⁸ cycles).

    Rotating-beam endurance limit (steel): Se′ = 0.5 Sut for Sut ≤ 1400 MPa; Se′ = 700 MPa above
    High-cycle line: Sf = a·Nb, a = (f·Sut)² / Se, b = −⅓·log(f·Sut / Se) (f ≈ 0.9 at 10³ cycles)
    Life: N = (σa / a)1/b

    Marin factors: from test bar to real part

    Se′ comes from a small, mirror-polished specimen in a laboratory. Your part is bigger, rougher, may be loaded axially, and must be reliable. Marin's equation corrects it:

    Materials and treatments for fatigue

    • Higher Sut raises Se′ — but the surface factor ka falls, so a rough high-strength part gains little. Polish or grind high-strength steel.
    • Compressive residual stress at the surface is the best friend of fatigue: shot peening, rolled fillets and threads, nitriding and carburising all add it. Gains of 20–50 % are common.
    • Tensile residual stress is the enemy: grinding burns, welding, some platings (chrome, nickel) and decarburised surfaces after heat treatment.
    • Clean steels (vacuum-degassed, low inclusions) for bearings and springs.
    • Corrosion destroys the endurance limit — there is no knee in corrosion fatigue. Protect the surface.

    Cost of fatigue strength

    Grinding a fillet, shot peening a spring or rolling a thread costs little compared with a larger part — and much less than a field failure. In design reviews, look first at the cheap fixes: bigger radius (Day 6), better finish, peening; then at a better steel; last at a bigger section.

    Worked example

    Machined steel bar, Sut = 600 MPa, Ø40, rotating bendingSe′ = 0.5 × 600 = 300 MPa ka = 4.51 × 600^−0.265 = 0.828 kb = 1.24 × 40^−0.107 = 0.836 Se = 300 × 0.828 × 0.836 = 208 MPa (35 % of Sut, not 50 %) f ≈ 0.88 → f·Sut = 530 MPa; a = 530²/208 = 1351 MPa; b = −⅓ log(530/208) = −0.1356 At σa = 250 MPa: N = (250/1351)^(1/−0.1356) ≈ 2.5 × 10⁵ cycles At 1440 rpm that is about 3 hours of running.

    Procedure

    1. Find Sut of the material in its heat-treated condition.
    2. Se′ = 0.5 Sut (or 700 MPa if Sut > 1400).
    3. Apply ka (finish), kb (size), kc (load type), kd (temperature) and ke (reliability) to get Se.
    4. Calculate the alternating stress σa at the critical point, multiplied by Kf.
    5. If σa ≤ Se/n: infinite life. If not, find N from the S–N line and compare with the required life.
    6. If mean stress is present too, go to Day 8.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Never use the textbook Se′ without Marin factors — the corrected value is often 30–40 % of Sut.
    • A rotating shaft with a steady bending load sees fully reversed bending stress every revolution: 1440 rpm is 2 million cycles a day.
    • Count the cycles honestly: a gear tooth is loaded once per revolution, a bearing race many times, a vibration-excited bracket at its natural frequency.
    • Read failed parts. Beach marks point back to the origin; the origin shows you what to fix.

    Mean and alternating stress

    σm = (σmax + σmin) / 2 σa = (σmax − σmin) / 2 R = σmin / σmax
    LoadingσmRExample
    Fully reversed0−1Rotating shaft in bending
    Repeated (0 to max)σa0Gear tooth bending, hoist chain
    Fluctuating> σa0 to 1Preloaded bolt, valve spring, connecting rod

    A tensile mean stress opens cracks and shortens life; a compressive mean stress helps. Tests by Gerber (1874) and Goodman (1899) gave the lines we still use.

    The fatigue diagram

    Plot σa (up) against σm (across). Fully reversed failure is at (0, Se); static failure at (Sut, 0). The failure line joins them:

    Goodman: σa/Se + σm/Sut = 1/n
    Soderberg: σa/Se + σm/Sy = 1/n (very conservative)
    Gerber: nσa/Se + (nσm/Sut)² = 1 (parabola, fits test data)
    ASME elliptic: (nσa/Se)² + (nσm/Sy)² = 1
    Langer (first-cycle yield): σa + σm = Sy/n

    If the load grows with σa/σm fixed (the usual case: the same force just gets bigger), the factor of safety is measured along the line from the origin through the operating point.

    Combined stresses

    For a shaft with alternating bending, steady torsion and so on, make von Mises equivalents of each component and use them in the Goodman equation:

    σa′ = √( (Kf·σa,bend + Kf,ax·σa,ax/0.85)² + 3(Kfs·τa)² )
    σm′ = √( (Kf·σm,bend + Kf,ax·σm,ax)² + 3(Kfs·τm)² )
    1/n = σa′/Se + σm′/Sut

    This is the basis of the DE-Goodman shaft equation on Day 13.

    Which line?

    GoodmanThe usual design choice: simple, slightly conservative, linear — easy to solve for size.
    Gerber / ASME ellipticCloser to test data; use for analysing failures or when weight matters.
    SoderbergGuards against yield as well, but too conservative for most designs. Common in older Indian textbooks and exams.
    Always also check LangerA point can be under the Goodman line and still yield on the first cycle when σm is large.

    Materials and cost

    Mean stress is often a design choice. Pre-setting springs (Day 16), autofrettage of gun barrels and pressure vessels, and shot peening all create a compressive residual mean stress at the surface — fatigue strength for little cost. Bolt preload (Day 10) does the opposite trick: it keeps the bolt's alternating stress small, even though the mean is high.

    Worked example

    Steel with Sut 620, Sy 380, Se 220 MPa; σm = 120, σa = 80 MPaGoodman: 1/n = 80/220 + 120/620 = 0.364 + 0.194 = 0.557 → n = 1.79 Soderberg: 1/n = 80/220 + 120/380 = 0.680 → n = 1.47 Gerber: n = 2.24 Langer: n = 380/(80+120) = 1.90 Design factor of safety = min(Goodman, Langer) = 1.79

    Procedure

    1. Find σmax and σmin at the critical point over a load cycle (include Kf).
    2. Calculate σm and σa (von Mises components for combined loads).
    3. Find Se with Marin factors (Day 7).
    4. Decide the load line: usually proportional (from the origin).
    5. Calculate n by Goodman, and check first-cycle yield with Langer.
    6. If n is too low: lower Kf, improve finish, use a stronger material, or enlarge the section.

    Lab: fatigue diagram

    Move the operating point (σm, σa). All five lines are drawn; the factors of safety along the load line are listed.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Apply Kf to both σa and σm for ductile materials (unless local yielding is shown to relieve the mean).
    • A compressive mean stress does not increase allowable σa beyond Se in design — be conservative there.
    • If loads vary in blocks (start-up, run, overload), use Miner's rule: Σ nᵢ/Nᵢ = 1 at failure (in practice design to 0.3–0.7).

    It is easier to grind a shaft to any size than to make a bore to any size, because bores are made with fixed-size tools (reamers, plug gauges). So most fits use the H hole and change the shaft letter. Shaft-basis (h shaft) is used when one drawn bar carries several parts, such as pins.

    Surface finish

    Roughness Ra is the average height of the surface profile, in µm. Specify it with the ISO 1302 / ISO 21920 symbol.

    Ra, µmProcessUse
    12.5–6.3Rough turning, millingNon-functional faces
    3.2–1.6Fine turning, millingStatic fits, gear blanks, housings
    0.8–0.4GrindingBearing seats, running fits, gear teeth

    A tolerance and the finish must agree: an IT6 diameter with Ra 6.3 µm is nonsense — the peaks alone eat half the tolerance. A rough rule: Ra ≤ ⅛ of the tolerance.

    Tolerance stack-up

    When parts stack along an axis (bearings, spacers, circlips), their tolerances add. The worst case adds them all: guaranteed to assemble, but expensive. The statistical (RSS) method adds them as √Σtᵢ² — realistic for large batches, accepting a tiny rejection rate.

    Worst case: T = Σ tᵢ Statistical: T = √(Σ tᵢ²)

    Cost of tolerance

    Recall Day 3: each step from IT9 to IT6 roughly doubles machining cost, and grinding adds an operation. Use H7/g6, H7/k6 and H7/n6 for the few fits that matter, and the general tolerance (ISO 2768-m) for everything else.

    Property class 8.8: first digit × 100 = Sut (800 MPa); product of the digits × 10 = Sy (640 MPa). Proof strength Sp (≈ 0.9 Sy, 580–600 MPa for 8.8) is the stress the bolt must carry without permanent set. Nuts are graded to match (class 8 nut for 8.8 bolts).

    Why preload

    Tightening the nut stretches the bolt and squeezes the clamped parts (members). When an external load P pulls the joint apart, the bolt stretches a little more and the members relax a little — but because the members are much stiffer than the bolt, most of P just reduces the clamping force; the bolt sees only a fraction C of it.

    C = kb / (kb + km) (typically 0.15–0.3 for steel joints)
    Bolt force Fb = Fi + C·P Member force Fm = Fi − (1 − C)·P
    Separation load P₀ = Fi / (1 − C)
    Load factor (proof) n = (Sp·At − Fi) / (C·P)
    kb ≈ E·At / l km = 0.5774·π·E·d / (2·ln(5(0.5774l + 0.5d)/(0.5774l + 2.5d)))

    Two consequences: (1) a properly preloaded bolt sees only a small alternating load, so it does not fail in fatigue; (2) the joint stays closed, so it does not leak, slip or hammer. Recommended preload: Fi ≈ 0.75 × proof load for reusable joints, 0.9 for permanent.

    Tightening torque

    T = K · Fi · d K ≈ 0.20 dry steel, 0.15–0.18 oiled, 0.12 with MoS₂ paste

    Torque is a poor measure of preload: friction scatter gives ±25 %. Better methods: torque-angle (turn-of-nut), direct tension indicators, hydraulic tensioners, ultrasonic bolt-length measurement — used on critical joints such as cylinder heads, flywheels and test-rig couplings.

    Bolts in shear and locking

    A joint loaded sideways should carry the load by friction from the preload (μ·Fi per surface), with fitted bolts or dowels if slip is not acceptable. Do not rely on a clearance bolt in shear — the joint will slip and the bolt will bend.

    Loosening is caused mostly by transverse slip. Remedies in order of effectiveness: enough preload and long, slender bolts; wedge-locking washer pairs; prevailing-torque nuts (nylon insert, all-metal); thread-locking adhesive; safety wire or tab washers for critical parts. Plain spring washers do little.

    Materials and cost

    8.8 is the general-purpose grade; 10.9 and 12.9 give smaller, lighter joints but are more sensitive to hydrogen embrittlement (careful with electroplating) and to fatigue at the thread. Rolled threads (after heat treatment) are about twice as good in fatigue as cut threads. Stainless A2-70/A4-70 bolts have lower strength (Sy 450 MPa) and gall easily — use anti-seize. A standard bolt costs rupees; a special bolt costs hundreds — design around catalogue sizes and lengths.

    Welds

    Butt weldJoins plates edge to edge. Full penetration: as strong as the plate. Stress = P / (t·l).
    Fillet weldTriangle in a corner. Fails across the throat t = 0.707 h (h = leg). Treated as shear on throat area.
    Plug / slotThrough holes in a lap plate; for extra area.
    Fillet weld, parallel or transverse load: τ = P / (0.707·h·l) (transverse fillets are ≈ 1.4× stronger; designers usually ignore this)
    Allowable (IS 816, E41 electrodes, mild steel): τ ≈ 108 MPa shop welds; take 94 MPa as a working value with site/fatigue margin

    Eccentric weld groups

    A bracket welded to a column with the load off to one side twists the weld group. Treat the welds as lines of unit width:

    Primary shear: τ₁ = P / A (A = 0.707 h × total length)
    Secondary shear: τ₂ = P·e·r / J, J = 0.707 h × Ju
    Two parallel welds of length l, b apart: Ju = l(3b² + l²) / 6
    Resultant at the farthest point: vector sum of τ₁ and τ₂

    For a weld in bending (bracket loaded in its own plane, welds above and below), use the unit section modulus Zu instead: σ = M / (0.707 h·Zu), and combine with direct shear.

    Fatigue of welds

    Welds are full of stress raisers (toe angle, undercut, lack of fusion) and carry tensile residual stresses close to yield. So their fatigue strength hardly depends on the steel's strength. Codes (IS 800, Eurocode 3, BS 7608) give detail categories — S–N curves for each joint shape. A fillet-welded attachment may be good for only 70–80 MPa stress range at 2 × 10⁶ cycles.

    • Put welds in low-stress regions; avoid welds at sharp section changes.
    • Grind weld toes smooth, or peen them (hammer or ultrasonic peening).
    • Stress-relieve after welding when distortion or residual stress matters (machined housings).

    Riveted joints

    Rivets are still used for aircraft skins, bridges, brake linings and where materials cannot be welded. A joint can fail by:

    Tearing of plate between rivets: Pt = (p − d)·t·σt
    Shearing of rivets: Ps = n·(π/4)d²·τ (×2 for double shear)
    Crushing of plate or rivet: Pc = n·d·t·σc
    Efficiency η = least of (Pt, Ps, Pc) / (p·t·σt)
    Unwin's formula for rivet hole: d = 6√t (t in mm)

    Lap joints reach 50–60 % efficiency; double-strap butt joints with several rows reach 80–90 %. A welded butt joint is 100 %.

    Materials and cost

    Low-carbon steels (E250, E350) weld easily; above about 0.25 % C, or alloy steels, need preheat and controlled cooling. Aluminium welding needs TIG/MIG and loses the temper strength near the weld (6061-T6 drops to about half). Welding cost is roughly proportional to weld metal volume — h² × length — so a 10 mm fillet costs four times a 5 mm fillet. Specify the smallest weld that works; do not "add a bit for safety" everywhere.

    Worked example

    Bracket on two vertical fillet weldsP = 40 kN at e = 150 mm; welds l = 120 mm, b = 80 mm apart; h = 8 mm Throat 5.66 mm, A = 2 × 120 × 5.66 = 1357 mm² → τ₁ = 29.5 MPa Ju = 120(3×80² + 120²)/6 = 672 000 mm³; J = 3.80 × 10⁶ mm⁴; r = 72.1 mm τ₂ = 40 000 × 150 × 72.1 / 3.80×10⁶ = 113.8 MPa Resultant at the far corner = 132.5 MPa > 94 → need h = 8 × 132.5/94 = 11.3 → 12 mm

    Procedure (eccentric weld)

    1. Draw the weld group and find its centroid G.
    2. Move the load to G: a direct force P plus a moment P·e.
    3. Calculate primary shear (P/A) and secondary shear (P·e·r/J) at the farthest points.
    4. Add them as vectors; find the largest resultant.
    5. Size the leg h so the resultant ≤ allowable. Round up to a standard leg (3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12 mm).
    6. Check fatigue by the code's detail category if the load varies.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Increasing the spacing b is far more effective than increasing h when the moment dominates.
    • Show weld symbols to ISO 2553 / IS 813 on drawings; do not write "weld all round" without a size.
    • Do not machine a bearing bore before welding is finished and stress-relieved.
    • Rivet and bolt holes in the same joint do not share load well — use one or the other.

    Thread forms

    FormAngleNotes
    Square0°Highest efficiency, hard to make, no wear take-up
    Trapezoidal (Tr, IS 7008 / ISO 2904)30°The usual Indian/ISO choice; split nut can take up wear
    ACME29°Inch-based equivalent of trapezoidal
    Buttress7°/45°Heavy load in one direction (vices, gun breeches)
    Ball screw—Rolling balls; 90 % efficient, needs a brake — CNC machines

    Tr40×7 means nominal Ø40, pitch 7. Lead L = number of starts × pitch: the axial travel per turn.

    Torque and efficiency

    Mean diameter dm = d − p/2 tan λ = L / (π·dm) μ′ = μ / cos α (α = half thread angle)
    Raise: T = (W·dm/2) · (L + π·μ′·dm) / (π·dm − μ′·L) + W·μc·dc/2
    Lower: T = (W·dm/2) · (π·μ′·dm − L) / (π·dm + μ′·L) + W·μc·dc/2
    Efficiency η = W·L / (2π·T_raise)
    Self-locking if π·μ′·dm > L, i.e. λ < φ (friction angle)

    The collar (thrust face between screw and the load cap) often wastes as much torque as the thread itself. A thrust ball bearing there nearly doubles the efficiency.

    The self-locking trade-off. A screw that holds its load without a brake must have λ < φ. It can be shown that its efficiency is then always below 50 %. High efficiency (ball screws) and self-locking cannot go together — add a brake.

    Stresses in the screw and nut

    Direct: σc = W / (π·dc²/4) (dc = core Ø) Torsion: τ = 16T / (π·dc³)
    Combined (Tresca): τmax = √((σc/2)² + τ²)
    Thread shear in nut: τn = W / (π·d·t·z) (z threads engaged, t thread thickness at root ≈ p/2)
    Bearing pressure: pb = W / ((π/4)(d² − dc²)·z) ≤ 10–17 MPa for steel on bronze
    Long screws in compression: check buckling with Euler/Johnson

    Materials and cost

    The screw: C45 or 40Cr4, thread rolled or whirled for production, ground for precision lead screws. The nut: phosphor bronze or cast iron — dissimilar metals to avoid seizure, and the cheaper part wears. Nut length is set by bearing pressure, not strength. A rolled trapezoidal screw costs a fraction of a ball screw; choose ball screws only when efficiency, speed or positional accuracy justify them.

    Worked example

    Screw jack, Tr40×7 single start, W = 50 kN, μ = μc = 0.12, collar Ø60dm = 40 − 3.5 = 36.5 mm; λ = atan(7/(π×36.5)) = 3.49°; μ′ = 0.12/cos15° = 0.124 (φ = 7.08°) Thread torque to raise = 170.4 N·m; collar torque = 50 000 × 0.12 × 30 = 180 N·m Total 350 N·m → efficiency 15.9 % (thread alone 32.7 %) λ 3.49° < φ 7.08° → self-locking. With a 400 mm handle: 350/0.4 = 876 N push — too much for one person; add a ratchet or a thrust bearing.

    Design procedure

    1. Estimate the core diameter from direct compression with a generous factor (σ ≈ 60–80 MPa).
    2. Choose the standard trapezoidal thread above it; find dm, p and λ.
    3. Calculate torques to raise and lower, efficiency, and confirm self-locking if required.
    4. Check combined stress in the screw core and buckling for the unsupported length.
    5. Find the number of nut threads from bearing pressure; check thread shear in nut and screw.
    6. Size the handle (operator effort ≈ 250–400 N) and the collar or thrust bearing.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Multi-start threads raise lead and efficiency — and usually lose self-locking. Check.
    • Vibration can make a "self-locking" screw creep down. Use a brake for anything that holds a load over people.
    • Keep the screw in tension where possible (hang the load) to avoid buckling.
    • Protect lead screws from chips and dust with bellows or wipers; wear on the nut controls life.
