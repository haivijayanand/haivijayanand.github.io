@sub How gravure presses moved from one rotating steel shaft to servo-synchronised electronic line shafts, why it matters for register, and how converters can migrate
@outline From intaglio plate to the shafted press | The mechanical line shaft and its limits | Backlash in the take-off | Evolution to the electronic line shaft | Electronic gearing and register correction | MLS versus ELS compared | Retrofit paths and the Indian context | Trends, engineering considerations and conclusions
@tnotes For a century, every unit of a gravure press was synchronised by one long rotating steel shaft. Today new high-speed presses are shaftless: each cylinder has its own servo drive following a virtual master. This talk explains why that happened, what the mechanical shaft could not do, what the electronic shaft makes possible, and how converters, especially in India, can migrate.

T: From intaglio plate to the shafted press | tab1
N: Gravure descends from intaglio plate printing. Rotogravure on cylinders appeared in the late nineteenth century with Klič's work, and multi-unit presses for packaging grew through the twentieth century, all synchronised by a mechanical line shaft driven by one main motor.

F: The mechanical line shaft: unit take-off | fig1
- One main motor drives a long shaft along the press
- At each unit a bevel gearbox turns the drive through 90°
- Optional register differential; cardan shaft to the cylinder
- Register corrected by compensator rollers changing the web path
N: At each print unit a bevel gearbox takes power off the line shaft, an optional differential allows phase adjustment, and a cardan shaft drives the cylinder. Register is normally corrected not by moving the cylinder but by compensator rollers that lengthen or shorten the web path between units.

B: Limits of the mechanical line shaft
- Torsional wind-up: each unit's load twists the shaft differently
- Backlash and wear in gears, keyways, splines and couplings
- Lightly damped torsional and lateral resonances at certain speeds
- Compensator moves disturb tension: register and tension fight
- Long web paths lengthen makeready and waste
N: The shaft is a long torsion spring. Doctor-blade friction and ink drag twist it by different amounts at each unit, and this changes with speed. Gear and coupling backlash grows with wear. The system has resonances. And because register is corrected by changing the web path, register correction disturbs tension, which is especially troublesome on stretchy films.

B: Backlash in the take-off and its control
- Bevel gears: deliberate clearance + wear; specify ISO 1328/AGMA grade, matched lapped pairs
- Cardan shafts: needle-bearing and spline clearance
- Single U-joint at an angle: speed varies twice per revolution, use matched phasing
- Register controller removes slow drift, not backlash crossings in ramps
- Check backlash at overhauls; watch it with condition monitoring
N: Every element of the take-off adds lost motion. Bevel gears need clearance for thermal growth and oil film, and wear adds to it, so specify high accuracy grades and matched pairs. Cardan shafts add clearance in their bearings and splines, and a single universal joint at an angle produces a twice-per-revolution speed fluctuation unless the joints are correctly phased. The register controller cannot follow backlash crossings during speed ramps, so backlash has to be controlled mechanically and monitored.

B: Strengths that keep the MLS in service
- Low capital cost and simplicity
- Maintainable by mechanical technicians
- No motion network and no electronic obsolescence risk
- All units stay mechanically in step, even in an emergency stop
- Economical where runs are long and speeds moderate
N: The mechanical shaft is not obsolete everywhere. It needs no motion network, has no electronics to become obsolete, keeps all units in step even during an emergency stop, and can be maintained by mechanical technicians. Where runs are long and speeds moderate, it remains the economical choice.

T: Milestones to the electronic line shaft | tab2
N: Several technologies had to mature: AC vector and servo drives, high-resolution encoders, deterministic motion networks such as SERCOS and EtherCAT, and fast register controllers. The first shaftless presses appeared in newspaper and then packaging printing in the 1990s, and the ELS became standard for new high-speed gravure presses in the 2000s.

F: Mechanical versus electronic line shaft | fig2
- MLS: one motor, one shaft; register via compensators
- ELS: one servo per cylinder following a virtual master axis
- Register corrected by phasing the cylinder itself
N: The figure contrasts the two. In the mechanical press one motor and one shaft drive every unit, and register is corrected through the web path. In the electronic press each cylinder has its own servo motor following a virtual master position signal, and register is corrected by advancing or retarding the cylinder itself.

E: Electronic gearing | eq1
W: Each axis i follows the master angle θ_m scaled by the radius ratio R_ref/R_i, plus a phase offset φ_i(t) set by the register controller. Cylinder size changes and register become software parameters.
N: Electronic gearing is one equation. Each cylinder axis follows the virtual master angle, scaled by the ratio of reference to actual cylinder radius, plus a phase offset that the register controller adjusts. Changing cylinder circumference or correcting register is now a change of parameters, not of gears or rollers.

B: Register correction on an ELS press
- Electronic differential: advance or retard the cylinder itself; shortest web path
- Compensator roller retained, cylinders electronically synchronised
- Predictive or feed-forward register: correct elongation before the error appears
- An ELS keeps cylinders in step; only web-side feedback corrects stretch
N: On an electronic press there are three ways to correct register. The usual one moves the cylinder phase directly, with no compensator and the shortest web path. Some presses keep compensators with electronic synchronisation. The newest predict elongation and tension changes and correct before an error appears. But in every case register control is still needed, because the drives only keep cylinders in step; they cannot see the web stretch.

B: What the ELS makes possible
- Stiffer control loops: no shaft, couplings or gears in the drive train
- Register held through speed ramps
- Automatic phasing at makeready from a scanning head
- A unit can leave line speed and rejoin in register
- Recipe recall of phase offsets, tensions and ratios
- Drive diagnostics: torque, following error, temperature
N: Removing the mechanical spring allows much higher servo gains. All axes follow the same command during ramps, so register holds while the press speeds up. Makeready waste drops with automatic phasing. A unit can drop out to clean clogged cells and rejoin in register. Settings are stored as recipes. And every drive becomes a sensor. Note, though, that an ELS still needs register control, because only web-side feedback sees substrate stretch.

T: MLS versus ELS compared | tab3 | rows=0-13
N: The comparison table covers register accuracy, makeready waste, speed, flexibility, web path length, maintenance skills, capital cost and obsolescence risk. The ELS wins on performance and flexibility; the MLS wins on capital cost, simplicity and maintainability by mechanical technicians.

B: Retrofit and migration paths
- Modernise the MLS: AC vector main drive, digital register control, sectional servos at unwind, infeed, outfeed, rewind
- Hybrid: keep the shaft; add servo phase correction at each take-off
- Full ELS conversion: one servo per cylinder (where frame and budget allow)
- New ELS press: 400–450 m/min, frequent changeovers, extensible films
N: For most converters the question is not shaft or no shaft but how far along a staged path to go. Modernising the mechanical press with AC drives and digital register control gives much of the benefit cheaply. A hybrid keeps the shaft but adds servo phase correction at each unit. Full conversion is practical only for good frames. And for high speeds, frequent changeovers and thin films, a new ELS press is the usual route.

B: The Indian flexible-packaging context
- Gravure is the backbone: >10 new gravure presses for every new CI-flexo press
- Local makers (Pelican, Kohli, UFlex, C Trivedi, Mohindra) supply most
- Questions before investing: share of short and repeat runs, substrate extensibility, drives skills, power quality and EMC, spares over a 15–20 year life
N: In India gravure remains the backbone of flexible packaging, with far more gravure than flexo presses installed every year, mostly from Indian makers. Before choosing an architecture, a converter should ask what share of jobs are short or repeat runs, how stretchy the main substrates are, whether drives expertise and service are available, how clean the power supply is, and what spares will cost over the press life.

B: Current trends
- Integrated, predictive register preset from job data at standstill
- Lightweight engraved sleeves changed from the side
- Automated cylinder and ink-pan changeover; robotic handling
- Extended colour gamut: fewer cylinder and ink changes
- The contest now is makeready time, waste and energy versus flexo and digital
N: The line-shaft debate is settled for new high-end presses. The contest now is makeready time, waste and energy, because shrinking run lengths expose gravure's weakest point. Predictive register, sleeves, automated changeover and extended colour gamut all attack that weakness.

B: Engineering considerations
- Drive sizing: inertia matching and acceleration torque per axis
- Network timing: deterministic cycle, synchronised clocks
- Tension zoning designed together with the drives
- Safety: coordinated stop of all axes on fault
- Condition monitoring: MLS gear-mesh orders and torsional resonances; ELS torque and following-error trends
N: Engineering the ELS means sizing each servo for its inertia and acceleration, designing a deterministic network, zoning tension with the drives, and ensuring all axes stop together safely on any fault. For condition monitoring, mechanical presses need vibration analysis of gear-mesh and shaft orders and torsional resonances, while on electronic presses rising torque or following error on an axis is the early warning.

C: Conclusions
- ELS is the default for new high-speed gravure presses
- MLS survives where runs are long, speeds moderate, drives expertise scarce
- Shaft wind-up, backlash and compensators set a floor on register and waste
- ELS makes register a software quantity
FW: Next gains
- Predictive register and sleeve-based quick change
- Robotic cylinder handling; efficient drying
- Data-driven maintenance
- Staged MLS → hybrid → ELS path for growth markets
N: The shaft gave gravure a century of reliable synchronisation, but its wind-up, backlash and reliance on compensators set a floor on register accuracy and waste. The next gains will come from what is built on the electronic shaft. For converters in growth markets such as India, a staged path lets investment follow need.
