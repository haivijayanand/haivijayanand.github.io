Reengineering the Corporation

Michael Hammer and James Champy · first published 1993 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Companies achieve dramatic gains not by tweaking old tasks but by starting over and redesigning whole business processes around the customer.

Summary

Michael Hammer and James Champy argue that most large companies were built for a world that no longer exists. Their structures grew from the industrial idea of splitting work into small, specialised tasks, each handled by a different department. That design worked when markets were stable, but it produces slow handoffs, errors, duplicated checks and layers of managers whose main job is to stitch the fragments back together.

Three forces, they say, have made this model obsolete: customers who now expect more and can choose freely, competition that has become global and intense, and change that has become constant. Incremental improvement cannot close the gap. Instead, firms must reengineer, which the authors define as fundamentally rethinking and radically redesigning business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in cost, quality, service and speed.

The key shift is to stop looking at departments and start looking at processes: the complete chain of activities that turns inputs into something a customer values, such as filling an order or developing a product. The book's cases, including credit approvals at IBM and supplier payments at Ford, show how removing needless steps and handoffs cut cycle times and costs sharply.

Redesigned processes tend to share features. Several jobs are combined into one, workers make decisions themselves, steps happen in a natural order, checks and reconciliations are reduced, and a single case manager can serve as the customer's point of contact.

These changes ripple outward. Departments give way to process teams, narrow tasks become broader jobs, controlled employees become empowered ones, pay shifts towards results, and managers move from supervising to coaching. Information technology is treated as an enabler that lets companies break old rules, not as a way to automate existing waste.

Finally, the authors stress that reengineering must be led from the top, with clear owners for each process, because it is disruptive and often fails when attempted halfway.

313 words

One-line takeaway: Don't automate or patch a broken process; ask why it exists and redesign it from scratch around the outcome the customer needs.

    Answer C. The book highlights more demanding customers, intensified competition and constant change as the forces driving the need for reengineering.

    Answer D. They trace the problem to the industrial-era principle of breaking work into narrow tasks spread across many departments.

    Answer C. The authors see IT as an enabler of radically new processes, warning against simply automating existing waste.

    Answer B. Redesigned processes typically compress jobs and push decision-making to the people doing the work.

    Answer B. They argue small tweaks to a fundamentally flawed process cannot close the large performance gap companies face.

    Answer D. Because reengineering is disruptive and cross-functional, the authors insist it be driven from the top with designated process owners.
