Competitive Strategy

Michael E. Porter · first published 1980 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

A firm's profitability depends on the structure of its industry and on choosing a clear position within it, so strategy starts with analysing competitive forces.

Summary

Michael Porter wrote this book to give managers a practical framework for thinking about competition. His central claim is that every company competes inside an industry whose structure largely decides how much profit is available. Before choosing a strategy, a firm must understand that structure.

Porter identifies five forces that shape it. New entrants threaten to add capacity and cut prices. Buyers with bargaining power push prices down or demand more quality. Powerful suppliers raise costs. Substitute products from other industries place a ceiling on what customers will pay. Finally, rivalry among existing firms can erode margins through price wars and costly marketing. When these forces are strong, as in many commodity businesses, few firms earn attractive returns; when they are weak, profits can be high. The task of strategy is to find a position where the company can defend itself against these forces or shape them in its favour.

He then describes three broad ways to win. Cost leadership means becoming the lowest-cost producer through scale, efficiency and tight control. Differentiation means offering something customers value as unique, such as design, brand or service, and charging a premium for it. Focus means serving a narrow segment better than broadly aimed rivals, using either cost or differentiation. A firm that fails to commit to any of these risks being stuck in the middle, with no real advantage.

Much of the book offers tools for analysing rivals: understanding their goals, assumptions, current strategy and capabilities, so that their likely moves can be predicted. Porter also explains barriers to entry, strategic groups within an industry, and how to read market signals.

Later chapters apply the framework to different settings, including fragmented, emerging, mature, declining and global industries, showing that the right strategy changes as an industry evolves.

The lasting message is that good strategy rests on careful analysis of industry structure and a deliberate choice of position.

315 words

One-line takeaway: Analyse the five forces in your industry, then commit clearly to cost leadership, differentiation or focus rather than drifting in between.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What does Porter say largely determines the profit potential available to firms?

    Answer C. Porter argues that the collective strength of competitive forces in an industry sets how much profit firms can earn.

  2. Which of these is one of Porter's five competitive forces?

    Answer A. Substitutes from other industries limit the prices an industry can charge, making them one of the five forces.

  3. How do powerful buyers affect an industry's profitability?

    Answer D. Strong buyers capture value by bargaining for lower prices or higher quality, squeezing industry margins.

  4. What is the main purpose of barriers to entry from an existing firm's point of view?

    Answer B. High entry barriers such as economies of scale or capital needs keep new rivals out and help preserve profits.

  5. A firm that aims to be the lowest-cost producer in its industry is pursuing which generic strategy?

    Answer C. Cost leadership seeks advantage through scale, efficiency and tight cost control across the industry.

  6. What does a differentiation strategy rely on?

    Answer A. Differentiation earns higher prices through features, brand, design or service that buyers value.

  7. What does Porter mean by being 'stuck in the middle'?

    Answer D. A firm that neither leads on cost nor truly differentiates or focuses tends to earn below-average returns.

  8. How does a focus strategy differ from the other two generic strategies?

    Answer B. Focus applies cost or differentiation advantages to a specific segment, serving it better than broad competitors.

  9. Why does Porter recommend analysing competitors' goals, assumptions, strategy and capabilities?

    Answer B. Understanding these elements lets a firm anticipate rivals' moves and reactions before choosing its own.

  10. What do the book's chapters on fragmented, emerging, mature and declining industries show?

    Answer C. Porter shows that strategic choices must adapt as an industry's structure and stage of evolution change.
