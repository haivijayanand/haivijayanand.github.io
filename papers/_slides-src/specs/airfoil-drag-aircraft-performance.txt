@sub How the drag of a two-dimensional section propagates into range, endurance, climb, turn and hover, from jet transports to quadcopters, in ten worked examples
@outline What makes up section drag | From section drag to vehicle performance | Transports: fuel burn, laminar flow, drag divergence | Light aircraft, sailplanes and small UAVs | Combat aircraft | Helicopters and multicopters | Value of one drag count | Conclusions and research directions
@tnotes Every aircraft designer chooses an airfoil, but how much does its drag actually matter to range, endurance or hover payload? This talk traces section drag through to vehicle performance with one consistent set of simple, calibrated relations and ten worked examples across fixed-wing aircraft and rotorcraft.

E: What makes up section drag | eq1,eq3
W: Friction + pressure (form) + wave drag. The friction coefficient blends laminar and turbulent skin friction at the transition point x_tr; the thickness form factor 1 + 2t/c + 60(t/c)⁴ converts it to profile drag.
N: Section drag has three parts: skin friction, pressure or form drag, and at high Mach number wave drag. The paper uses a simple model that blends laminar and turbulent friction at the transition location and multiplies by a thickness form factor. It is calibrated against published data and is good enough to show trends and sensitivities.

F: Transition location dominates profile drag | fig1
- 12 % section: fully turbulent vs 60 % laminar
- Halving drag is possible at drone and light-aircraft Re
- Laminar benefit shrinks as Re rises and transition moves forward
N: This figure shows section profile drag against transition location for three Reynolds numbers. Moving transition from the leading edge to 60 per cent chord can almost halve drag. That is why laminar-flow sections are so attractive, and also why losing laminar flow to contamination is so costly.

F: The drag bucket | fig2
- Natural-laminar-flow sections: low, narrow bucket
- Conventional turbulent sections: higher but wider
- Low-Re sections: drag set by separation bubbles
- The operating C_l must sit inside the bucket
N: Minimum drag alone is not the full story. A natural-laminar-flow section has a low but narrow bucket, a conventional section a higher but wider one. If the aircraft flies outside the bucket, the laminar section can be worse. So the airfoil must be matched to the range of lift coefficients the vehicle actually uses.

T: Where airfoil drag matters, by vehicle class | tab3 | cols=0,1,2,3
N: Different vehicles sit in very different Reynolds and Mach number ranges, so different section characteristics dominate: minimum drag and drag divergence for transports, bucket width for sailplanes, bubble behaviour for small drones, and drag rise with Mach number and lift for helicopter rotors.

E: The performance relations | eq6,eq7,eq10
W: Breguet range scales with L/D; propeller or electric endurance scales with C_L^{3/2}/C_D. Near the optimum (L/D)max ∝ C_D0^{−1/2} but endurance ∝ C_D0^{−1/4}: a 10 % cut in C_D0 gives about 5 % more L/D but only about half that in endurance.
N: The parabolic polar, Breguet range and the endurance parameter connect section drag to performance. The sensitivity exponents are the key insight: range responds to the square root of zero-lift drag, endurance only to the fourth root. So the same airfoil improvement is worth twice as much in range as in endurance.

T: Worked example A: narrow-body transport at M 0.78 | tab5
N: For a generic single-aisle transport, cutting wing profile drag by 10 per cent, about 7 counts, raises L/D from 16.2 to 16.6 and saves 2.3 per cent of cruise fuel on a 2 500 km sector. A laminar wing would save about 5 per cent. Per thousand flights that is 363 tonnes of fuel and over a thousand tonnes of CO₂.

F: Transonic drag divergence | fig5
- Korn relation: M_DD from airfoil technology factor, thickness, C_L and sweep
- Supercritical sections buy about 0.05–0.07 in Mach number
- Often traded for thicker, lighter wings instead of speed
N: For transports, the drag rise with Mach number sets the cruise boundary. The Korn equation shows how a supercritical section raises drag-divergence Mach number at a given thickness and sweep. In practice the gain is usually spent on a thicker wing, which saves structural weight and fuel volume, rather than on flying faster.

F: Sailplanes and long-endurance aircraft | fig10
- 15 m sailplane: clean laminar wing vs insect/rain contamination
- Contamination raises sink rate across the speed range
- Bucket width and robustness matter as much as minimum drag
N: For sailplanes, performance is the sink polar. Insects and rain trip the laminar boundary layer early, and the polar shifts upward over the whole speed range. This is why modern sailplane sections are designed to be robust to contamination, not just to have the lowest clean drag.

F: Small UAVs: the laminar separation bubble | fig12
- 2.5 kg electric UAV, Re below 3×10⁵
- Bubble-prone section raises battery power required throughout
- Choosing a proper low-Re section is the largest single lever
N: At small-drone Reynolds numbers, laminar separation bubbles dominate. The worked example compares a well-chosen low-Reynolds-number section with a bubble-prone one on a 2.5 kilogram electric UAV. The difference in battery power, and therefore endurance, is larger than anything a refinement of a good section could give.

F: Combat aircraft: wave drag and sustained turn | fig14
- Supersonic wave drag ∝ (t/c)²: thin sections essential
- Sustained turn at M 0.8: reducing induced factor K helps more than profile drag
- Buffet and high-α drag set the manoeuvre boundary
N: For combat aircraft, linear supersonic theory shows thickness wave drag growing with the square of thickness, so supersonic wings must be thin. In a sustained turn at Mach 0.8 the induced drag dominates, so reducing the induced-drag factor pays more than reducing section profile drag.

E: Helicopter hover: power and figure of merit | eq16,eq17
W: Hover power = induced (κ T^{3/2}/√(2ρA)) + profile (σ c̄_d ρA(ΩR)³/8). Profile drag enters the figure of merit directly through σ c̄_d/8.
N: In hover, rotor power is induced power plus profile power. The blade section drag enters directly through the mean drag coefficient times solidity. So for helicopters the airfoil drag affects the most important number of all: how much the aircraft can lift.

T: Worked example H: 5.5 t helicopter hot-and-high | tab6
N: For a generic 5.5 tonne helicopter hovering at 3 000 metres and ISA plus 20, profile power is 13.5 per cent of the total. A 10 per cent better blade polar adds 47 kilograms of hover-capable mass, while 25 per cent worse, typical of leading-edge erosion, costs 127 kilograms, a large share of the useful load.

F: Multicopters: hover endurance of a 2 kg quadcopter | fig19
- Small rotors at low Re: mean c̄_d high and sensitive to section choice
- Figure of merit and endurance fall steadily as c̄_d rises
- One count of rotor c̄_d ≈ 0.7 s of hover endurance
N: For a 2 kilogram quadcopter, the rotor blades work at low Reynolds number, so their mean drag coefficient is high and depends strongly on the section. Hover endurance and figure of merit fall steadily as blade drag rises. Each drag count on the rotor is worth about 0.7 seconds of hover, small per count, but section choices change the drag by dozens of counts.

F: Sensitivity across vehicle classes | fig20
- 10 % less section drag → typically 1–3 % in range, endurance or hover capability
- Largest for rotorcraft and low-Re drones
- In-service contamination and erosion can cost more than design refinements gain
N: This chart summarises the normalised sensitivity of each vehicle's main metric. Ten per cent less airfoil drag typically buys one to three per cent of range, endurance or hover capability. The point for operators is that maintaining the surface matters as much as designing it well.

C: Conclusions
- 10 % less profile drag → 1–3 % in range, endurance or hover capability
- A laminar transport wing could save about 5 % of trip fuel
- Contamination and erosion can cost more than many design refinements gain
- For rotorcraft and high-speed aircraft the drag rise with M and C_l sets the boundary
FW: Research directions
- Robust laminar flow tolerant of contamination and manufacturing
- Riblets, suction and plasma transition control
- Variable geometry and morphing to keep the bucket on the operating point
- Better prediction near C_l,max, low Re, transonic and in rotors
N: The paper's main findings and research directions. Better prediction tools are most valuable exactly where the drag is hardest to predict: near maximum lift, at low Reynolds number, in transonic flow, and in the unsteady environment of a rotor.
