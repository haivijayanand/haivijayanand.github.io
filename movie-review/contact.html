Summary

Contact follows Dr. Ellie Arroway (Jodie Foster), an astronomer who has spent her life listening for radio signals from other worlds — the SETI search that most of her colleagues think is a waste of time. Her father, who first taught her to use a radio, died when she was a child; she has trusted only evidence ever since.

Then, at the Very Large Array in New Mexico, she hears it: a pulsing signal from the star Vega, counting in prime numbers. Hidden inside are plans for a machine. What follows is a story about who gets to travel, who gets the credit, and what happens when a rigorous sceptic experiences something she cannot prove.

Carl Sagan and Ann Druyan began the story as a film treatment in 1979; it took eighteen years and three directors to reach the screen. Sagan died during filming, and the film is dedicated to him.

Most alien films ask what the visitors want. Contact asks what we are willing to believe — and makes its atheist scientist the one who must ask for faith.

Decoding the signal

The film’s middle act is a detective story told through data. It is one of the few Hollywood films to show how a real signal search might work.

Layer 1
Prime numbers. Regular pulses counting 2, 3, 5, 7, 11… No natural source produces primes, so the signal must be intelligent.
Layer 2
Our own broadcast, returned. Hidden under the primes is a video: Hitler opening the 1936 Berlin Olympics — the first TV signal strong enough to leave Earth, reflected back from Vega.
Layer 3
63,000+ pages of data. Encoded drawings that no one can read without a key.
The primer
The billionaire Hadden finds the key by hacking government systems: the pages fold into 3-D, revealing plans for a one-person transport machine.
The Machine
Built by several nations at Cape Canaveral — and, secretly, a second one in Japan.

Plot

Spoilers ahead. Acts 4 and 5 are blurred —

The listener

Young Ellie learns about the stars and radio from her father, who dies when she is still a girl. As an adult SETI astronomer she loses federal funding when the president’s science adviser, David Drumlin, calls her work futile. The eccentric billionaire S. R. Hadden funds her, and she moves to the Very Large Array.

The signal

Four years later Ellie detects the transmission from Vega. Drumlin takes public credit. The government, the military, the media and religious groups crowd in. Hadden decodes the primer, and an international project builds the Machine.

Who will go?

The selection panel favours Ellie, but the religious writer Palmer Joss — once her lover — questions her: she does not believe in God, and most people on Earth do. The panel chooses Drumlin.

The second machine

A religious extremist bombs the Machine during a test, killing Drumlin. The dying Hadden, living on the Mir space station, reveals a second machine in Japan. Ellie goes. Joss admits he voted against her because he was afraid of losing her.

Eighteen hours

Ellie falls through a series of wormholes and arrives on a beach like her childhood drawing of Pensacola. An alien appears in the form of her father and tells her that humanity has taken a first step. To everyone watching, though, the pod simply dropped through the machine in an instant; her recorder shows only static. Before a congressional hearing, Ellie admits she cannot prove anything and asks them to accept her experience on faith. Privately, officials note that her recorder captured eighteen hours of static.

Music

Alan Silvestri, Zemeckis’s regular composer since Back to the Future, wrote a restrained, mostly gentle score that keeps Ellie and her father at its centre. It stays quiet for long stretches, letting radio hiss and the pulses of the signal do the work.

Sound is the film’s real instrument: the first contact is something Ellie hears, headphones on, before anyone sees anything. It earned the film its Oscar nomination for Best Sound.

Technology & science

The opening zoom

A three-minute computer-generated shot pulls back from Earth through the solar system and the galaxy, while radio broadcasts fade to older and older programmes as the signals travel farther — then lands in young Ellie’s eye. It was inspired by the 1977 short film Powers of Ten.

The mirror shot

When young Ellie runs to fetch her father’s medicine, the camera follows her and the shot turns out to be a reflection in the cabinet mirror. It was built from three separate plates combined digitally — a seamless trick audiences still study.

Real telescopes

Filming began at the Very Large Array near Socorro, New Mexico, where the production negotiated control of the giant dishes to move them for the camera. A second unit filmed at the Arecibo dish in Puerto Rico.

Wormholes by Kip Thorne

The journey was shaped by physicist Kip Thorne’s research on traversable wormholes — work he first did after Sagan asked him how the novel’s travel could be made plausible. Weta Digital created the wormhole sequence.

Designing the Machine

Concept artist Steve Burg reused an idea he had drawn for an unused Terminator 2 scene. The pod began as a twelve-sided dodecahedron and became a sphere inside it; the crew studied Kennedy Space Center to design the launch gantry.

A beach you can’t trust

On the alien beach, artists ran some waves backwards and sped up the palm-tree shadows, so the scene feels slightly wrong — leaving the audience unsure whether it is real.

Many effects houses

Sony Pictures Imageworks (led by Ken Ralston on the Machine), Weta Digital, ILM, Warner Digital and Effects Associates all worked on the film, with rendering in Pixar’s RenderMan.

The Clinton footage

Real 1996 footage of President Clinton speaking about a Martian meteorite was digitally reworked to seem to be about the Vega signal. The White House counsel objected publicly, calling it inappropriate.

Science check

The SETI Institute called it the most accurate SETI depiction in any Hollywood film, while noting small errors, such as the VLA’s sensitivity compared with Arecibo.

Contact was a modest success: it nearly doubled its budget, but opened in the shadow of a lighter alien film, Men in Black. Its reputation has grown far more than its box office — Roger Ebert later added it to his “Great Movies” list.

The release also sent Sagan’s novel back onto the New York Times bestseller list for two months in the summer of 1997.

Where the emotions come from

A girl and her radio. Young Ellie calling out “CQ, this is W9GFO” into the night, hoping someone answers, is the whole film in one image — and the root of her lifelong search.
The moment of discovery. Ellie at the VLA, headphones on, eyes widening as the pulses arrive, then racing her car while shouting coordinates into the radio — one of cinema’s great scenes of pure scientific joy.
Credit stolen. Watching Drumlin take the spotlight for her life’s work is a familiar, painful truth for anyone who has worked in a large organisation.
“They should have sent a poet.” Ellie, a scientist, finds herself without words before the beauty of the universe — the film’s most quoted line.
A father, again. The alien chooses her father’s form to make the meeting bearable. For a moment the film becomes about a daughter’s grief, not first contact.
Eighteen hours. The final reveal says nothing outright, yet tells us she was gone. Evidence exists — it is simply hidden. A quiet, perfect ending.

What works

  • Jodie Foster’s Ellie — stubborn, brilliant and lonely, one of film’s best scientist characters.
  • A first-contact story that respects real science and real scientists.
  • An ending that trusts the audience instead of explaining everything.

What doesn’t

  • At about two and a half hours, the politics in the middle drag; Mick LaSalle felt the climax was weaker than the first 90 minutes.
  • The Ellie–Palmer romance feels thin next to the rest.
  • The Clinton footage was a misjudged gimmick.

Watch it for: the opening zoom, the discovery at the VLA and the beach. For engineers, the decoding sequence is a treat — signal processing as suspense.
