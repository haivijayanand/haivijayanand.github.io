The Explosive Child

Ross W. Greene · first published 1998 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Children who melt down easily are not choosing to misbehave; they lack certain skills, and adults help most by solving the underlying problems together with them, calmly and ahead of time.

Summary

Ross Greene, a child psychologist, wrote this book for parents of children who become frustrated quickly and react with outbursts, shouting or shutdowns. Many such families have tried sticker charts, time-outs and stricter rules, only to find that the explosions continue. Greene offers a different way of seeing the child.

His central belief is that children do well if they can. A child who keeps exploding is not lazy, manipulative or after attention. Instead, the child is missing certain skills, such as handling frustration, shifting smoothly from one plan to another, and thinking through problems while upset. When the demands of a moment are greater than the skills the child has, an outburst follows.

Because of this, Greene argues that rewards and punishments often miss the point. They can motivate a child who already has the skills, but they cannot teach flexibility to a child who does not yet have it. More pressure in a heated moment usually makes things worse.

He also points out that outbursts are usually predictable. They tend to happen around the same unsolved problems, such as homework, bedtime, screen time or getting ready in the morning. Parents can list these problems and work on them before the next blow-up, rather than during it.

Greene describes three ways adults can respond to a problem. They can insist on their own solution, they can set the expectation aside for now, or they can solve the problem together with the child. He strongly favours the collaborative way. The adult first listens carefully to understand the child's concern, then shares the adult's own concern, and finally invites the child to find a solution that works for both and is realistic.

Over time, this calm teamwork reduces outbursts, builds the child's thinking skills and repairs trust between parent and child.

300 words

One-line takeaway: This week, pick one daily problem that often leads to a meltdown, and at a calm moment ask your child what is hard about it, share your own concern, and agree on one solution you both can live with.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What is Greene's core belief about children's behaviour?

    Answer B. Greene holds that children do well when they have the skills to meet the demands placed on them.

  2. According to the book, what mainly causes a child's explosive outbursts?

    Answer D. Outbursts happen when demands exceed skills the child has not yet developed.

  3. Why does Greene think rewards and punishments often fail with these children?

    Answer A. Incentives can motivate, but they do not build missing skills like flexibility or problem solving.

  4. What does Greene say about when outbursts tend to occur?

    Answer C. Meltdowns tend to cluster around the same recurring problems, such as homework or bedtime.

  5. When is the best time to work on a problem that triggers meltdowns?

    Answer B. Greene stresses proactive problem solving at calm moments rather than in a crisis.

  6. What is the first step in solving a problem collaboratively with a child?

    Answer C. The process begins with gathering information about what is hard for the child.

  7. After understanding the child's concern, what should the adult do next?

    Answer A. Both sides' concerns must be on the table before looking for a solution.

  8. What makes a good solution in Greene's approach?

    Answer D. A workable solution must be doable and meet the concerns of both child and adult.

  9. Which of the three responses to a problem does Greene most favour?

    Answer C. He sees collaborative problem solving as the approach that reduces outbursts and builds skills.

  10. Besides fewer outbursts, what long-term benefit does the collaborative approach bring?

    Answer A. Working together builds the child's problem-solving skills and restores trust.
