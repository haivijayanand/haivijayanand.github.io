PID Control and Tuning: P, I and D Actions, FOPDT Models, Ziegler–Nichols and Lambda Tuning

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
PIDControlTuningAutomation

PID controllers hold temperatures, pressures, speeds and flows on test rigs and in plants. Most loops are tuned by trial and error and end up either sluggish or oscillating. A short step test that fits a first-order-plus-dead-time model, followed by a tuning rule, gives good settings in minutes and explains why some loops cannot be made fast.

Key takeaways

  • u = Kc·[e + (1/Ti)∫e dt + Td·de/dt]: P reacts to error, I removes offset, D anticipates.
  • A bump test gives a FOPDT model: gain K, time constant τ and dead time θ; difficulty rises with θ/τ.
  • Lambda (IMC) tuning: Kc = τ/(K(λ + θ)), Ti = τ; choose λ ≥ θ for robustness.
  • Ziegler–Nichols closed-loop: Kc = 0.6Ku, Ti = Pu/2, Td = Pu/8; aggressive (quarter-decay) by design.
  • Use derivative on measurement with filtering, and anti-windup on the integral when the output saturates.

1. The three terms

TermEffectToo much causes
Proportional (Kc)Output proportional to errorOscillation; steady offset if used alone
Integral (1/Ti)Accumulates error to remove offsetSlow oscillation, overshoot, windup
Derivative (Td)Reacts to rate of change; dampsNoise amplification, output chatter

2. Process model from a step test

FOPDT: G(s) = K·e^(−θs)/(τs + 1) K = ΔPV/ΔOutput; θ = time before PV starts to move; τ = time from start of movement to 63 % of change

4. Practical points

  • Fix mechanical and sensor problems first (valve stiction, sensor noise, wrong scaling).
  • Anti-windup: stop integrating when the output saturates, or use back-calculation.
  • Bumpless transfer between manual and auto.
  • For dead-time-dominant loops (θ/τ > 1), accept slower tuning or use a Smith predictor or feedforward.
