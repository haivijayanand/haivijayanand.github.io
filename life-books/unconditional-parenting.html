Unconditional Parenting

Alfie Kohn · first published 2005 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Children flourish when they feel loved for who they are rather than for what they do, so parents should work with children instead of controlling them through rewards, praise and punishment.

Summary

Alfie Kohn, an author known for questioning rewards and grades in schools, turns his attention to family life. His central claim is that many common parenting tools, even gentle-looking ones, send children a quiet message: you are loved only when you behave the way we want. He argues that children need the opposite message, that our love does not depend on their performance.

Kohn draws a sharp line between two styles. One is doing things to children, using control to get compliance. The other is working with children, involving them in solving problems. He believes the first may win short-term obedience but often costs trust, self-respect and genuine moral growth.

He is critical of punishment, including time-outs, which he sees as a form of withdrawing love. More surprisingly, he is also wary of rewards and constant praise. In his view, a stream of approval for good behaviour is the flip side of disapproval for bad behaviour, and it can leave children anxious about earning acceptance and less interested in the activity itself.

Kohn points to research suggesting that children who feel their parents' affection is conditional tend to feel worse about themselves and may resent their parents. Unconditional acceptance, he says, is what lets children feel secure enough to grow.

Instead of quick tricks, he offers guiding principles. Parents can ask whether a request is really necessary, put the relationship first, try to see situations through the child's eyes, talk less and ask more questions, and assume the best possible motive behind a child's actions. He encourages giving children a real say in decisions that affect them, appropriate to their age.

The book asks parents to think about long-term goals, such as raising caring, thoughtful people, rather than simply getting a child to obey today.

295 words

One-line takeaway: This week, replace one automatic 'good job' with a simple description of what your child did or a question about how they feel about it, and involve them in solving one recurring household problem together.
