Netscape vs Internet Explorer: the browser wars

The web browser turned the internet from a tool for scientists into a place for everyone. Its history is a cycle: Mosaic, then Netscape, then Internet Explorer, then Firefox and Chrome — each new leader arriving with better technology or a stronger distribution channel.

01The requirement: one window onto all the world's documents

By 1989 the internet connected universities and laboratories, but finding information meant knowing which computer to log into and which commands it used — FTP, Telnet, Gopher, each different. At CERN, Tim Berners-Lee proposed linking documents across computers with hypertext. He defined three things — HTML (the page), HTTP (the request) and URLs (the address) — and in 1990 wrote the first browser-editor, WorldWideWeb, on a NeXT computer.

04Version explorer

Blue chips: Mosaic, Netscape and Mozilla. Amber chips: Internet Explorer, Edge and the modern era.

05United States v. Microsoft

Microsoft bundled Internet Explorer free with Windows and integrated it deeply into Windows 98, while Netscape charged for Navigator. In May 1998 the US Department of Justice and 20 states sued, arguing Microsoft used its Windows monopoly to crush Netscape. In 2000 a trial court ordered Microsoft split in two; in 2001 the appeals court upheld the finding of illegal monopoly maintenance but overturned the break-up. A settlement followed requiring Microsoft to share interfaces and allow PC makers to choose other software. By then Netscape had already lost: AOL bought it in a deal completed in 1999.

Two decades later the same questions returned around search and mobile. In the US government's search case against Google, a court ruled in 2024 that Google held an illegal monopoly in search; in 2025 it ordered remedies but did not require Google to sell Chrome.

07Retirements

  • Mosaic: NCSA ended development in 1997.
  • Netscape: after Netscape 6–9 (built on Mozilla's Gecko), AOL ended support on 1 March 2008. Its code lives on in Firefox and Thunderbird.
  • Internet Explorer: stagnated after IE 6 (2001); Microsoft replaced it with Edge in 2015 and retired the IE desktop application on 15 June 2022. Old intranet sites still open in Edge's "IE mode".
  • Plug-ins: Adobe ended Flash Player on 31 December 2020; Java applets and ActiveX were removed from modern browsers.

08Today

Most browsers now share one engine family: Chromium/Blink (Chrome, Edge, Opera, Brave, Samsung Internet). WebKit (Safari) and Gecko (Firefox) are the only independent alternatives. The browser has become the platform itself — office suites, CAD viewers, maps and even this series run inside it — and in 2025 AI-assisted browsers that can read and act on pages appeared.

09Lessons for engineers

  1. Distribution can beat product. IE won by being already installed; Chrome by being promoted on the world's most visited page.
  2. A monopoly that stops improving invites a challenger. Five years without a new IE gave Firefox its opening.
  3. Open standards outlive companies. HTML, HTTP and URLs survived every browser that implemented them.
  4. Security architecture becomes a selling point. Sandboxed processes and automatic updates are now expected.
