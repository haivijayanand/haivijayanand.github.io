Where the rate comes from

A helical compression spring is a torsion bar wrapped into a helix. Push on the ends and the wire barely bends; almost all of the deflection is the wire twisting about its own axis. Everything below follows from that one idea.

An axial load F acting at radius D/2 puts a torque on the wire, and the twisted length is one circumference per active coil:

T = F·D / 2 L = π·D·n

A bar of torsion constant Kt twists through θ = T·L / (G·Kt), and the coil end travels along the axis by that angle acting through the same radius, δ = θ·D/2. Substituting and collecting:

δ = F·π·D³·n / (4·G·Kt) k = F/δ = 4·G·Kt / (π·D³·n)
Dmean coil diameter, wire centre to wire centre
nactive coils, the ones free to twist
Gshear modulus of the wire
Kttorsion constant of the wire cross-section
kspring rate, force per unit deflection

The torsion constant is the only thing that changes between wire shapes

Round Kt = π·d⁴ / 32 Rectangular Kt = β·b·t³ b = long side, t = short side

Substituting the round case gives back the textbook form, which is a useful check that the general formula is being applied correctly:

k = 4·G·(π·d⁴/32) / (π·D³·n) = G·d⁴ / (8·D³·n)

Two consequences worth holding on to. Wire size enters to the fourth power for round wire and as the short side cubed for rectangular, so a 5% error in measuring the wire is a 20% error in rate: measure the wire more carefully than anything else on the part. And the coil diameter enters as a cube in the denominator, so winding the same wire on a slightly larger mandrel softens the spring quickly.

β and α for rectangular wire

A rectangle has no closed-form torsion solution, so Saint-Venant's series is used, with β for stiffness and α for peak stress. Both depend only on the aspect ratio:

A square wire is about 11% less stiff than round wire of the same cross-sectional area, because the circle is the most efficient torsion section. Rectangular wire is chosen for solid height efficiency and energy storage in a given envelope, never for rate alone.

Counting coils

Total coils N is what you count on the part, end to end. Active coils n is the number free to twist. Coils that are squared or ground flat bear against the seat and contribute almost nothing, so they are subtracted. The end treatment also fixes the solid height and the pitch.

End typeActive nSolid length LsPitch p
Open, plainNd(N+1)(L0 − d)/n
Open, groundN − 1d·NL0/(n+1)

Squared & groundN − 2d·N(L0 − 2d)/n

For rectangular wire, substitute the axial wire width b for d. Real dead coils are rarely exactly two: a ground end that is not quite flat leaves part of a coil working. That is the usual reason a measured rate comes out below the calculated one, and it is what the implied-coil figure on the Measured data tab is for.

Helix angle λ = arctan( p / (π·D) )

Beyond about 12° the coil is steep enough that the wire carries real bending as well as torsion, and the rate formula starts to over-predict stiffness.

Pre-compression

Pre-compression, also called preload or initial compression, means installing the spring at a length L1 shorter than its free length L0. The spring is already pushing before the mechanism does anything:

δ1 = L0 − L1 F1 = k·δ1 preload δ2 = L0 − L2 F2 = k·δ2 load at the working length ΔF = F2 − F1 = k·(L1 − L2) load change over the stroke

Pre-compression does not change the rate. The rate is fixed by wire, coil diameter and active coils. Preload moves where you sit on the line; it does not tilt the line. If you need a different force change over a given stroke, you have to change the spring, not the installed length.

What it is for

  • Taking up backlash and clearance so nothing rattles at rest.
  • Guaranteeing a minimum seating force. Valve seats, seals, brushes, contacts and clutch plates need force at zero stroke, not force after 5 mm of travel.
  • Keeping a follower against a cam through the whole cycle, so the spring never goes slack and the parts never separate and slam back together.
  • Flattening the force variation across a stroke. The force ratio is F2/F1 = δ2/δ1, so a large preload with a small working stroke gives nearly constant force. A soft spring compressed a long way is how you get near-constant force; a stiff spring barely compressed gives a force that swings wildly over the same travel.

What it costs

  • Preload consumes travel. Available deflection is L0 − Ls, and the preload has already spent δ1 of it before the stroke begins.
  • The spring sits stressed the whole time it is installed. Held at high stress, spring steel takes a permanent set and loses free length, which quietly reduces the preload over months. Presetting, also called scragging, at the factory stabilises this by taking the set deliberately during manufacture.
  • Assembly has to apply and hold the preload force while fasteners go in.

Design guides

  • Keep the working deflection below roughly 80% of the deflection to solid, so the spring never reaches coil bind under tolerance stack-up, wear or thermal movement.
  • For cyclic duty, leave a clearance of at least 10 to 15% of the working deflection between the working length and solid.
  • Specify preload as a force with a tolerance, not as a length. The length that produces it depends on the rate, which itself carries a manufacturing tolerance of typically ±10%.

Reading it off a test

If you measure force against the travel of your mechanism rather than against the spring's own deflection from free length, every reading is offset by the preload. Fitting a straight line with an offset recovers both: the slope is the rate and the intercept is F1. An intercept that comes out near zero or negative when you expected a preload usually means the spring is going slack at the top of the stroke, which is worth knowing.

Stress

The wire carries torsional shear from the applied torque plus a direct transverse shear, and the bore of the coil is stressed more than the outside because the wire on the inside of the helix is shorter. Wahl's factor rolls both corrections into one multiplier:

C = D / d spring index Kw = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C τ = Kw · 8·F·D / (π·d³) round wire τ = Kw · T / (α·b·t²) rectangular wire

Peak stress is on the coil bore, at the mid-point of the long side for rectangular wire. That is where fatigue cracks start, which is why shot peening the inside of the coil matters and why a drawn-in scratch there is a rejection.

The index C governs how severe the correction is. At C = 4, Kw is about 1.40; at C = 12 it is about 1.12. Below about 4 the wire is bent so tightly it is hard to coil and the stress concentration is punishing. Above about 12 the spring is floppy, tangles in handling and buckles readily. Four to twelve is the workable band, six to nine the comfortable middle.

The tensile strength of spring wire rises as the wire gets thinner, following

Sut = A / d^m

with A and m from Shigley's Table 10-4. The allowable torsional stress for static duty is taken as a fraction of that: 45% for cold-drawn carbon and music wire, 50% for hardened and tempered wire, 35% for austenitic stainless and non-ferrous alloys. For rectangular wire this tool converts the section to an equal-area diameter before applying the size correction, which is an approximation. Fatigue duty needs a Goodman or Zimmerli analysis on the alternating and mean stresses, which this tool does not do.

Buckling

A compression spring is a column and will bow sideways if it is too slender. The criterion is Shigley's, based on slenderness and seating:

λ = a_end · L0 / D C1 = E / (2(E − G)) C2 = 2π²(E − G) / (2G + E) δ_crit = C1·L0·[ 1 − √(1 − C2/λ²) ]

If λ² ≤ C2 the spring is stable at any deflection, which for steel means a slenderness ratio L0/D under about 5.2 on flat parallel plates. The seating constant a_end is 0.5 for flat parallel plates at both ends, 0.707 with one end pivoted, 1.0 with both pivoted and 2.0 with one end free to tilt, so a spring that is fine between two flat faces can buckle at a quarter of that length if one end sits on a ball joint. Failing this check does not make a spring unusable: run it in a bore or over a rod and accept the friction and wear.

Surge frequency

A spring is a distributed mass on a distributed stiffness, so it has its own longitudinal resonance. Excite it near that frequency and a compression wave runs up and down the coils, producing loads far above the static value while the ends barely move. Between two fixed parallel plates the fundamental is

fn = ½·√(k / m) m = ρ·A_wire·π·D·N

Keep the fundamental at least 15 to 20 times the highest forcing frequency. Surge is what destroys valve springs when an engine reaches a critical speed, and it is why such springs are given a varying pitch, so that no single section of the coil has one clean resonance.

Fitting measured readings

The Measured data tab does an ordinary least-squares fit of load against deflection. Three models are offered: through the origin, a straight line with an offset, and a quadratic for a spring that stiffens as dead coils come into contact.

The two inverted figures are the diagnostic. Both come from rearranging the same rate equation:

Implied G = k_fit · π·D³·n / (4·Kt) Implied n = 4·G·Kt / (π·D³·k_fit)

They cannot both be true. They are two readings of the same discrepancy, expressed once as a material problem and once as a geometry problem, and geometry is the more common culprit. A measured rate below calculation with a good R² usually means fewer active coils than assumed, or a mean diameter larger than nominal, since D enters as a cube. A measured rate above calculation on a low-index spring is often just the missing curvature correction. Scattered points with a poor R² point at the test rather than the spring: friction in the fixture, a load cell not aligned with the spring axis, or ends not seating flat.

What this tool does not cover

  • Fatigue life, Goodman diagrams and shot-peening benefit.
  • Set and creep at elevated temperature. Both G and the allowable stress fall as it gets hotter.
  • Rate change as dead coils close up. Real springs stiffen slightly near solid.
  • Manufacturing tolerance. Commercial rate tolerance is typically ±10% and free length carries its own, so a rate quoted to four digits is arithmetic precision, not real accuracy.
  • Curvature correction to the rate at low index, and bending at high helix angle.
  • Variable-pitch, conical and barrel springs, all of which have a non-constant rate by design.

The same spring in every material

Geometry is held fixed at whatever is set on the Spring tab; only the material changes. Rate follows G alone, so it scales directly with the shear modulus. Load capacity is a separate question: it follows the tensile strength, which rises as wire gets thinner, and the allowable fraction of it, which depends on the alloy class. The two do not move together, so the stiffest choice is rarely the strongest.

Safe load is where peak shear reaches the static allowable. Where that exceeds the load at solid, the spring can be closed fully without taking a set and the row says so. These are static limits; cyclic duty needs a fatigue analysis on the alternating and mean stresses.
