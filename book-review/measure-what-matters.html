Measure What Matters

John Doerr · first published 2018 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Clear objectives paired with a few measurable key results help organisations focus, align and achieve far more than they otherwise would.

Summary

John Doerr, a venture capitalist, explains the goal-setting method known as OKRs, short for Objectives and Key Results. He first learned it as a young engineer at Intel under Andy Grove, and later brought it to Google in its early days. The book mixes his own explanation with case studies from companies and non-profits that adopted the system.

The idea is simple. An objective states what you want to achieve: it should be significant, concrete and inspiring. Key results state how you will know you got there: a small number of specific, time-bound and verifiable measures. If a key result cannot be checked as done or not done, it is not a key result.

Doerr describes four benefits he calls superpowers. The first is focus: by choosing only a few priorities, leaders signal what matters most and what can wait. The second is alignment: when goals are public across the organisation, teams can link their work to company aims and see what colleagues are doing. The third is tracking: progress is reviewed regularly, so plans can be adjusted, kept or dropped during the cycle rather than judged only at the end. The fourth is stretch: ambitious goals push people beyond what seems safe, and falling a little short of a bold target can still mean great progress.

He separates committed goals, which must be fully met, from aspirational ones, where partial success is expected. He also stresses that OKRs work best when many are proposed by teams themselves, not all imposed from the top.

The later chapters argue that annual performance reviews should give way to continuous conversations, feedback and recognition, and that OKRs should be kept separate from pay decisions. Culture, he concludes, decides whether the method thrives.

289 words

One-line takeaway: Pick a few inspiring objectives, define measurable key results for each, share them openly and review progress often.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What does the abbreviation OKR stand for?

    Answer B. OKR stands for Objectives and Key Results, the two parts of every goal in the system.

  2. From whom did Doerr first learn the OKR method?

    Answer D. Doerr encountered OKRs while working at Intel, where Andy Grove had developed and championed the approach.

  3. Which statement best describes a good key result?

    Answer A. Key results must be measurable so that anyone can tell whether they have been achieved.

  4. What is the main role of the objective in an OKR?

    Answer C. The objective names the desired destination; the key results show how progress towards it is measured.

  5. Why does Doerr recommend choosing only a few objectives at a time?

    Answer D. Focus, the first superpower, depends on deciding what is most important and setting aside the rest.

  6. How does making OKRs visible across the organisation help?

    Answer A. Transparency supports alignment and connection, so teams can see how their efforts link to wider aims.

  7. What does the 'tracking' superpower encourage during a goal cycle?

    Answer C. Doerr favours frequent progress reviews so that plans respond to reality before the cycle ends.

  8. How does Doerr distinguish committed OKRs from aspirational OKRs?

    Answer B. Committed goals must be delivered in full, whereas stretch goals are bold enough that falling somewhat short is normal.

  9. What does Doerr suggest about linking OKRs to compensation?

    Answer D. Tying OKRs directly to pay discourages ambitious goals, so he advises keeping the two apart.

  10. What does the book propose in place of relying on annual performance reviews?

    Answer A. Doerr argues for ongoing performance management that complements OKRs throughout the year.
