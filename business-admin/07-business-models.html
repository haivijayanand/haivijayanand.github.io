Business Models

Two firms can sell similar things and make money in completely different ways. A business model is the logic of how a firm creates value for customers and captures some of it as profit. Pick your level.

1The Business Model Canvas

Osterwalder's canvas puts a whole business on one page in nine blocks. Choose an example to fill it in.

Read it right-to-left for value creation (customers → value → channels) and left-to-right for cost (partners → activities → resources). Revenue minus cost at the bottom must be positive, eventually.

2Unit economics: is each customer worth it?

CAC = marketing & sales spend ÷ new customers won LTV = (avg monthly revenue × gross margin) ÷ monthly churn Payback months = CAC ÷ (monthly revenue × gross margin)
CAC vs LTV

Rules of thumb used by investors: LTV/CAC of at least 3, and CAC payback within 12–18 months for small-business software. Below 1, growth destroys money: each new customer costs more than they will ever pay back.

Level 3 · For experts — a review

Platform economics, network effects, and modelling subscription businesses by cohort.

1Platforms and network effects

A platform creates value by enabling interactions between two or more groups (Rochet & Tirole 2003; Parker, Van Alstyne & Choudary 2016). Cross-side network effects (more riders attract more drivers) and same-side effects (more users of a messaging app) make value rise with scale. Pricing follows elasticities and externalities, not cost: the side that brings more value to the other is often subsidised (free search for users, paid ads for advertisers).

2Subscription cohort simulator

Revenue in a subscription business is the sum of cohorts, each decaying with churn and growing with expansion (upsell). Net revenue retention (NRR) > 100% means a cohort's revenue grows even as customers leave.
