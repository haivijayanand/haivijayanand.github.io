The Blessing of a Skinned Knee

Wendy Mogel · first published 2001 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Children grow strong, capable and kind when parents stop trying to protect them from every hardship and instead give them clear limits, real responsibilities and room to stumble.

Summary

Wendy Mogel, a clinical psychologist in Los Angeles, noticed that many of the anxious families she saw were doing everything they could for their children, yet the children were often fragile, demanding or worried. Looking for a better path, she turned to old Jewish teachings on family life and found practical wisdom that suits parents of any background.

Her central point is that small struggles are good for children. A scraped knee, a disappointing grade or a lost game teaches resilience in a way that constant rescue never can. When parents smooth every bump, children miss the chance to learn that they can cope, recover and try again.

Mogel also asks parents to accept the child they actually have. Not every child is gifted or exceptional, and an ordinary report card is not a disaster. Seeing a child's real strengths and limits, rather than an imagined ideal, eases pressure on everyone at home.

She treats chores as a gift rather than a burden. Helping with meals, laundry and caring for younger siblings shows children that they are needed and that a family works together. Along with this comes respect for parents: clear rules and calm authority, not endless negotiation.

The book encourages gratitude and moderation. Children who do not get everything they want learn patience and appreciate what they have. Mealtimes can become calm, thankful moments rather than battles over food, and a regular day of rest with family can slow down a busy week.

Mogel even sees a child's stubborn or mischievous streak as energy that can be guided toward good ends rather than crushed. Her message is warm and freeing: parents can relax, set sensible limits and trust that ordinary days, honest effort and a few skinned knees are what raise self-reliant, grateful and caring adults.

298 words

One-line takeaway: This week, give each child one real household job of their own, and when they hit a small setback, hold back from fixing it and let them work it out with your encouragement.
