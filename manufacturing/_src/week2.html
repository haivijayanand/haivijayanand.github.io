    Why forecast at all?

    Anything with a lead time longer than the customer will wait must be planned to a forecast: raw material with a six-week import lead time, a new extruder, the number of operators for next quarter. Even a make-to-order converter forecasts films, resins and foil, because the customer gives ten days and the supplier needs forty.

    Three truths about forecasts. They are always wrong; they are better for families than for single items; they are better for the near future than the far future. So forecast at the level and horizon the decision needs — tonnes of BOPP film per month, not pouches of SKU 4711 next Thursday.

    Simple methods

    Moving average: F(t+1) = (D(t) + D(t−1) + … + D(t−n+1)) ÷ n Exponential smoothing: F(t+1) = α·D(t) + (1 − α)·F(t) 0 < α < 1

    A longer moving average (larger n) or a smaller α smooths noise but reacts slowly to real change. Typical α is 0.1–0.3. Where there is a trend, use double smoothing (Holt); where there is seasonality, use seasonal indices (Holt–Winters) or year-on-year ratios.

    Measuring error

    Error e = actual − forecast MAD = average of |e| MAPE = average of |e| ÷ actual × 100 % Bias = Σ e Tracking signal = Σ e ÷ MAD (outside ±4 → the method is biased)

    MAD feeds directly into safety stock (Day 10): σ ≈ 1.25 × MAD. A forecast that is always low (positive bias) causes shortages however clever the method.

    Demand in flexible packaging

    Seasonal, lumpy and promotion-drivenPackaging demand follows the customer's sales: edible oil and snacks peak before festivals, ice-cream and beverages in summer, tea in winter. Brand owners change artwork often and run promotions with special packs, so SKU-level demand is lumpy. The usable forecast is at the level of substrate and structure (tonnes of PET, BOPP, foil, PE film per month) plus customer schedules (call-offs) for the next 4–8 weeks. Ask key customers for their rolling forecast every month and track how good it is — some are reliable, some always over-order.

    Worked example

    Exponential smoothing, α = 0.3Month: 1 2 3 4 5 Actual t: 120 132 118 140 151 Forecast: 120 120 123.6 121.9 127.3 → month 6: 0.3×151 + 0.7×127.3 = 134.4 t Errors (months 2–5): 12, −5.6, 18.1, 23.7 → MAD = 14.9 t, bias +48.2 → tracking signal 3.2 Positive and growing: demand is trending up; use Holt's method or raise α.

    A monthly forecasting routine

    1. Clean the history: remove one-off orders, stock-outs, and data errors.
    2. Run a statistical forecast for each family (with trend and season where present).
    3. Collect customers' forecasts and sales team's knowledge of new products, losses and promotions.
    4. Agree one consensus number in a short meeting; write down why it differs from the statistics.
    5. Track MAPE and bias by family and by customer every month.
    6. Feed the agreed forecast to S&OP and material planning (Day 8).

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Forecast demand, not sales. Shipments during a stock-out under-state what customers wanted.
    • Do not tune α every month to fit the last point — you will chase noise.
    • Measure forecast accuracy at the lead-time horizon you buy at, not last week's number.
    • Managers: a forecast without an error figure is half a number.

    Bill of materials and MRP

    The bill of materials (BOM) lists what goes into one unit, level by level, with the quantity per unit and the scrap allowance. MRP takes each MPS quantity and explodes it through the BOM:

    Gross requirement = parent quantity × quantity per parent × (1 + scrap allowance) Net requirement = gross − on-hand − scheduled receipts (+ safety stock) Planned order = net, rounded up to the lot size (minimum order, pack size) Release date = need date − lead time

    BOM of a laminated pouch

    Three-layer PET / ink / adhesive / Al foil / adhesive / PE stand-up pouchLevel 0: 1 000 stand-up pouches. Level 1: slit laminate reel (kg) + zipper (m) + cartons. Level 2: laminate = printed PET (12 µm) + Al foil (9 µm) + PE film (80 µm) + adhesive (2 × ~2.5 g/m²). Level 3: printed PET = plain PET + inks (by colour) + solvent; PE film = LDPE/LLDPE resins + masterbatch + slip additives (made in-house on the blown-film line). Each level has its own scrap allowance: make-ready waste in printing, edge trim in slitting, set-up waste in pouch making. The lead times differ widely: foil and PET may be imported (6–10 weeks), inks are local (1 week), the PE film is made in-house (3 days).

    Priority rules

    RuleRun nextStrength
    FIFOFirst in, first outFair, simple; poor on everything else
    SPTShortest processing timeMinimises average flow time and WIP
    EDDEarliest due dateMinimises maximum lateness
    Critical ratioSmallest (time to due) ÷ (work remaining)Dynamic; good for multi-step jobs
    Johnson's rule (two machines in series, e.g. print → laminate). List all jobs with their times on both machines. Pick the smallest time anywhere. If it is on machine 1, put that job as early as possible; if on machine 2, as late as possible. Remove the job and repeat. The result minimises makespan.

    Sequence-dependent changeovers

    Colour, width, substrate and adhesive sequencingIn printing, changeover time depends on what ran before: going from a light job to a dark one in the same station needs only a quick wash; dark to light needs a thorough wash-up. Shared colours (the same white or the same brand red) can stay in the station. Planners group jobs by number of colours, shared inks, cylinder circumference, web width and substrate. In lamination, group by adhesive system (solvent-less vs solvent-based, food vs non-food) and by web width. In extrusion, sequence by MFI and colour of masterbatch: natural → white → coloured → black. In pouch making, group by pouch size and zipper type. A good sequence can cut changeover time by a third with no investment.

    Machining has the same problem with fixture and tool families; food plants with allergen sequences (allergen-free first, washdown after).

    Worked example

    Johnson's rule: print then laminateJob: A B C D E Print h: 5 2 8 3 6 Lam h: 4 6 3 7 2 Smallest 2: B on print → first; E on lam → last. Order: B _ _ _ E Next 3: D on print → second; C on lam → fourth. Order: B D _ C E Remaining A → third. Order: B D A C E Makespan = 26 h (FIFO order A B C D E gives 27 h; with more jobs the gap grows).

    How to build a weekly schedule

    1. List released jobs with due dates, routes, run times and material status (no material, no schedule).
    2. Schedule the bottleneck first (drum), in a changeover-friendly sequence that still meets due dates.
    3. Back-schedule upstream steps from the bottleneck dates; forward-schedule downstream steps.
    4. Check finite capacity — no machine loaded beyond its hours.
    5. Publish a frozen 2–3 day sequence to the floor; replan the rest daily.
    6. Track OTIF and the reasons for misses every week.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • No rule is best for everything. SPT for flow, EDD for due dates, sequence groups for changeovers — combine them.
    • Do not schedule jobs whose material, cylinders or approvals are not ready — they fill the plan and block the floor.
    • Expediting is a symptom. If more than 10 % of jobs are "urgent", the planning system is broken, not the people.
    • Managers: protect the schedule. Every jump-the-queue order costs a changeover and delays others.

    Why hold stock, and what it costs

    ReasonsCover lead time; absorb variation in demand and supply; allow economic batches; buy ahead of price rises; decouple processes.
    Holding cost (20–30 % of value per year)Interest on money, storage space, handling, insurance, obsolescence (artwork changes!), damage and ageing (films, adhesives with shelf life).
    Ordering / set-up costPurchase paperwork, freight, inspection — or for made items, the changeover cost.
    Inventory turns = annual cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory value Days of inventory = 365 ÷ turns

    How much to order: EOQ

    EOQ = √(2·D·S ÷ H) D annual demand, S cost per order, H holding cost per unit per year Total cost = (D ÷ Q)·S + (Q ÷ 2)·H

    The total-cost curve is flat near the EOQ: ordering 30 % more or less than EOQ raises cost only a few percent. So round EOQ to a convenient pack or truck load. The real power of the formula is what it says about set-up: cut S (Day 11) and the economic batch falls with its square root.

    Inventory in flexible packaging

    Raw, WIP and finished — and the special problemsRaw material: films (PET, BOPP, CPP, Al foil, paper) by grade, thickness and width; resins and masterbatches; inks (with Pantone shades) and solvents; adhesives with shelf life.     Setting inventory policy

    1. Do an ABC analysis by annual value: A ≈ 20 % of items, 80 % of value — control tightly.
    2. Add XYZ by demand variability: X steady, Y variable, Z lumpy. AX items can run lean; CZ items may be made only to order.
    3. For A and B items set EOQ (or pack size) and ROP with a chosen service level.
    4. For C items use simple two-bin or periodic review with generous stock — they are cheap.
    5. Measure turns and slow / obsolete stock monthly; clear what has not moved in 180 days.
    6. Review supplier lead times twice a year; they drift.

    The SMED method (Shigeo Shingo)

    Changeover time = from the last good unit of the old job to the first good unit of the new job at full speed and quality. Include the "ramp-up" — it is often the longest part.
    1. Observe and record — video the whole changeover; list every element with its time.
    2. Separate — mark each element internal (only possible with the machine stopped) or external (could be done while running). Move external elements outside the stop: fetch tools, cylinders, inks and material before the stop.
    3. Convert internal to external — preheat, premix, preset, prealign off-line.
    4. Streamline all elements — quick clamps instead of bolts, standard heights and centring, no adjustments (fixed stops), parallel work by two people, checklists and changeover carts.
    5. Standardise — write the new standard work, train, and measure every changeover.

    Why it matters

    Capacity lost to changeovers = number of changeovers × changeover time Economic batch ∝ √(changeover cost) (Day 10) At the same capacity, halving changeover time lets you halve batch size → halve cycle stock and WIP lead time

    SMED is the bridge between cost and flexibility: either you gain capacity (same batches, more running time) or you gain flexibility (smaller batches, same running time) — or a bit of both.

    SMED in converting

    Gravure, flexo, laminator, slitter, pouch machineGravure press: cylinders, doctor blades and inks staged on a trolley by the next station before the stop (external); ink viscosity and shade pre-adjusted in the ink kitchen; quick-lock cylinder chucks; two operators working both sides; pre-register marks and auto-register. Flexo: sleeves mounted and proofed off-line on a mounting machine; anilox and chambers changed as cassettes. Laminator: adhesive mix ready; web splice at speed. Slitter: knife positions preset from the job card or recipe; shafts preloaded with cores. Pouch machine: sealing jaws and cutting dies preheated on a stand; recipe-stored temperatures and lengths. Typical results: 9-colour gravure changeovers from 90 min to 40 min; pouch machine size change from 60 to 20 min.

    The method was born in car press shops (die changes from hours to under ten minutes) and works the same in injection moulding, CNC machining (tool presetting, zero-point clamping) and food filling (CIP and format parts).

    Worked example

    Pouch machine size changeBefore: 62 min, 5 changeovers/day on 3 shifts → 310 min/day lost (23 % of 1 350 min) Elements external but done during the stop: fetch dies 8 min, find tools 5 min, get film 6 min → move out Convert: preheat seal jaws on a stand (12 min internal → 0) Streamline: quick clamps (10 → 4 min), recipe for settings (8 → 2 min) After: 23 min → 115 min/day lost → 195 min/day gained = 3.25 h/day: running time 1 040 → 1 235 min, ≈ 19 % more output, no capex.

    Little's Law

    WIP = throughput × lead time → lead time = WIP ÷ throughput

    This holds for any stable system — a factory, a department, a queue at a bank. Its message is blunt: if throughput is fixed by the bottleneck, the only way to cut lead time is to cut WIP. Releasing more orders into the floor to "get things moving" does the opposite.

    TriggerA plan says make itDownstream consumption says replace it
    Good forMake-to-order, long lead times, lumpy demandRepetitive items with steady demand

    RiskFloods the floorStarves if demand jumps
    Number of Kanbans N = d̄ × L × (1 + α) ÷ C d̄ demand rate, L replenishment lead time, α safety factor, C container quantity
    CONWIP (constant work in progress): a hybrid for varied jobs. A fixed number of "cards" circulates through the whole route; a new job is released only when a finished one leaves. Jobs are chosen by the schedule, but WIP never rises above the cap.

    Shop-floor control in converting

    Push for jobs, pull for common itemsCustomer-specific printed jobs are pushed by the schedule (they are make-to-order), but WIP is capped CONWIP-style: no more than, say, two days of work released in front of the press. Common items run on pull: plain PE film for lamination kept in a supermarket of standard widths, replenished by Kanban to the extrusion line; standard inks and adhesives on two-bin Kanban; cores and cartons on vendor-managed Kanban. A job tracking board (physical or MES screen, Day 27) shows every job's stage: printed → curing (with cure-release time) → slit → pouched → QC → dispatch.

    Running a shift

    1. A board at each area: plan vs actual by hour, downtime reasons, quality issues, safety cross.
    2. A 10-minute start-of-shift meeting: safety, yesterday's misses, today's plan, problems needing help.
    3. Hourly: supervisor marks actual vs plan; any gap gets a reason, not just a number.
    4. Escalate problems by rule — within 15 minutes to the supervisor, within an hour to the manager.
    5. Release new jobs only when WIP is below the cap.
    6. End of shift: handover note — job status, issues, settings that worked.

    Set throughput and WIP to see lead time; then size a Kanban loop. The animation shows cards circulating between the supermarket and the supplying process — press P.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Releasing work early does not finish it early. It just waits longer on the floor.
    • Reduce Kanban cards one at a time; each removal exposes a problem to fix.
    • Pull needs stable demand and short, reliable changeovers (Day 11). Fix those first.
    • Managers: ask "how many days of WIP?" every week. It is the earliest warning of a late-delivery month.

    2. Pick one common item and size a Kanban loop for it.
