LabVIEW and data acquisition

A test rig produces signals — temperature, pressure, torque, speed, vibration — and an engineer must capture them, check them live, and store them for analysis. Before the PC this meant chart recorders and meters read by hand. Data-acquisition software, led by National Instruments' graphical LabVIEW, turned the PC into an instrument; today it runs everything from school labs to aircraft test facilities.

01The requirement: make the computer an instrument

In the 1960s Hewlett-Packard created a bus to connect instruments to computers — HP-IB, standardised in 1975 as IEEE 488 or GPIB. In 1976 James Truchard, Jeff Kodosky and Bill Nowlin, engineers at the University of Texas, founded National Instruments to make GPIB interface boards. Their customers then faced the hard part: writing programs, in BASIC or C, to control instruments, collect readings and display them. Most test engineers were not programmers.

02LabVIEW: programs drawn as wiring diagrams

Jeff Kodosky wanted engineers to program the way they draw block diagrams. In LabVIEW (Laboratory Virtual Instrument Engineering Workbench) every program is a virtual instrument (VI) with a front panel (knobs, switches, graphs) and a block diagram where functions are joined by wires. Execution follows dataflow: a block runs as soon as all its inputs have data, so parallel loops come naturally. The Macintosh's graphics made it possible; LabVIEW 1.0 appeared in October 1986.

1986 — LabVIEW 1.0 (Mac). Interpreted; slow but revolutionary.
1990 — LabVIEW 2. A compiler made VIs run at near-C speed.
1992 — Windows and Sun. LabVIEW 3 reached PCs and workstations. NI also offered LabWindows/CVI for engineers who preferred C.
1998 — TestStand. A sequencer for production test, calling LabVIEW, C or Python steps.
2000s — Real-time and FPGA. LabVIEW Real-Time and FPGA modules programmed CompactRIO controllers (2004) for embedded control and monitoring.
2017–2021 — NXG. A rewritten "LabVIEW NXG" was tried and then discontinued; NI returned to developing classic LabVIEW.
2020 — Community Edition. Free for non-commercial use.
2023 → — Emerson. Emerson completed its purchase of NI in October 2023. LabVIEW now releases quarterly; the NI Nigel AI assistant arrived in 2025, and LabVIEW 2026 Q3 (July 2026) added prompt-based generation of VIs.

03Try it: a LabVIEW-style monitoring VI

A typical rig-monitoring VI: acquire a vibration signal, filter it, compute RMS and peak, compare with a limit, and log. Change the controls on the "front panel" and watch values flow along the wires of the "block diagram" each time the loop runs. Raise the bearing defect, then switch the filter off: the impacts are high-frequency, so a low-pass filter hides them — a common monitoring mistake.

05Try it: bits, range and resolution

A DAQ card converts voltage to numbers with an analogue-to-digital converter (ADC). The smallest step is range ÷ 2bits. A small signal on a large range wastes resolution — which is why choosing the input range matters.

07Data files and long-term access

  • TDMS (NI) and DXD (DEWESoft) are binary formats with channel metadata; both have free readers and Python libraries (npTDMS, DEWESoft's data reader library).
  • HDF5 and CSV are vendor-neutral; export key results to them for archiving.
  • Record the configuration — sensor sensitivities, ranges, filters, sample rates — with the data; a file without it cannot be trusted years later.
  • Check licence terms before upgrading: test rigs are often kept on a validated software version for years.

09Lessons for engineers

  1. Choose sample rate and range before the test. Neither can be fixed afterwards.
  2. Separate acquisition from analysis. Log raw data reliably; analyse in a different program or loop.
  3. Document the chain. Sensor → conditioner → DAQ → software settings, with calibration dates.
  4. Graphical or text, structure matters. A messy block diagram is as hard to maintain as messy code.
