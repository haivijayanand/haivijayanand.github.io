Inflation

Why the same ₹100 buys less every year — what inflation is, how it is measured, what it does to you, to India and to the world, and how it is kept in check. Pick your level.

Level 1 · For school students

No jargon. If you can buy a samosa, you can understand inflation.

1What is inflation?

Inflation means prices of most things slowly go up over time. So the same money buys less than before.

Ask your grandparents what a cinema ticket or a cup of tea cost when they were young. They will tell you a few rupees — or even a few paise! Today the same things cost many times more. The tea is the same tea. What changed is the value of the rupee: each rupee buys a little less every year.

A simple exampleLast year a samosa cost ₹10. This year it costs ₹11. The samosa went up by ₹1 out of ₹10 — that is 10% inflation for samosas.
It is about "most things"If only mangoes become costly, that is just mangoes. Inflation is when the general level of prices — food, clothes, bus fare, school fees — goes up together.
The oppositeWhen prices of most things fall for a long time, it is called deflation. It sounds nice, but it can make businesses shut and people lose jobs.

3Why do prices go up?

🛒 Too many buyers, too few thingsImagine 50 children want ice cream, but the shop has only 20. The shopkeeper can raise the price. When a whole country has more money to spend than there are goods, prices rise. Economists call this demand-pull inflation.
⛽ Making things costs moreIf petrol, diesel or electricity become costly, lorries charge more to carry vegetables, and factories pay more to run machines. They pass this on to us. This is cost-push inflation.
🌧️ Nature and bad luckToo little rain, or floods, can spoil crops. Fewer onions in the market → onion prices jump. In October 2026, onion prices in India were more than double what they were a year earlier.
🖨️ Printing too much moneyIf a government simply prints lots of new notes, there is more money but the same amount of goods — so each note is worth less. This is why money printing is controlled very carefully.

4Who feels it?

👨‍👩‍👧 Your familyParents' salaries may go up once a year, but prices go up all year. If prices rise faster than the salary, the family has to cut back.
👵 Grandparents on pensionSomeone getting a fixed amount every month finds it buys less each year — unless the pension also rises with prices.
🐷 Your piggy bank₹500 kept in a piggy bank for five years is still ₹500 — but it buys fewer things. Money kept in a bank earns interest, which helps it keep up.
🇮🇳 The whole countryPoor families spend most of their money on food, so when food prices jump, they are hit the hardest. That is why villages felt more inflation than cities in October 2026.

5Is all inflation bad?

A little is normal and healthy. When prices rise slowly — say 2 to 4 percent a year — businesses are happy to produce more, and people plan without worry. India's Reserve Bank aims for about 4%.

Too much is very harmful. When prices rise very fast, it is called hyperinflation:

  • Germany, 1923 — prices doubled every few days. People carried money in wheelbarrows, and children used bundles of notes as building blocks.
  • Zimbabwe, 2008–09 — the country printed a 100-trillion-dollar note, and even that could not buy much.

Falling prices are not great either. If everyone expects things to be cheaper next month, they stop buying today; shops sell less, factories cut jobs. So the goal is low and steady, not zero.

6Who keeps it in check?

In India, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is like a referee for prices. Its job is to keep inflation near 4% (anywhere from 2% to 6% is allowed).

Prices rising too fast?RBI raises the interest rate. Loans become costlier, people and companies borrow and spend a little less, and prices cool down.
Prices too slow, jobs scarce?RBI lowers the interest rate. Loans get cheaper, people buy more, factories get busier.
The government helps tooIt can release stored rice and wheat, allow imports of onions or pulses, or cut taxes on fuel to bring prices down.

8Check yourself

Level 2 · For working people

What inflation does to your salary, savings, loans and long-term goals — and why the country and the world move with it. Educational, not financial advice.

1Definition, and how India measures it

Inflation is the sustained rise in the general price level, measured as the percentage change in a price index — usually year-on-year (this month versus the same month last year).

Two traps when reading the headline number

  • Base effect. Year-on-year inflation compares with last year. If prices were unusually low a year ago, this year's figure looks high even if prices barely moved this month. October 2026's 6.09% partly reflects very low prices in October 2025 (when inflation was a record low after GST cuts).
  • Falling inflation ≠ falling prices. If inflation drops from 6% to 4%, prices are still rising — just more slowly. Prices do not come back down unless there is deflation.

2Your salary in real terms

What matters is not your salary but what it buys. If your pay rises 8% and inflation is 6%, your real pay rose about 2%. If you got a 5% hike in a 6% year, you took a pay cut in real terms without anyone saying so.

Real growth ≈ Nominal growth − Inflation (exact: (1+n)/(1+π) − 1)
  • Government employees and pensioners get DA, revised twice a year from CPI-IW, which protects much of their basic pay from inflation.
  • Private-sector employees depend on the annual increment. Negotiating it is, in effect, negotiating against inflation.
  • Self-employed and small businesses must raise their own prices — often with a delay, so their margins get squeezed first.

Calculator — what will things cost, and what will your money be worth?

3Savings and investments — the real return

A fixed deposit at 7% sounds safe. But after tax and inflation, the money may actually be losing buying power. The test for any saving is: does it beat inflation after tax?

Cash and savings accountsUsually earn less than inflation. Good for emergencies and spending money, poor for long-term wealth.
Fixed-income (FD, bonds, small savings)Fixed in rupees. Real return depends on the rate you locked in versus the inflation that actually happens. Unexpected inflation hurts.
Real assets and equityShares, property and gold have historically tended to keep up with inflation over long periods — but with ups and downs along the way, and no guarantee.
Inflation-linked optionsSome instruments pay a return linked to inflation; pensions with DA work the same way. Ask a qualified adviser what fits you.

4Loans and EMIs

Inflation helps borrowers — on paperA fixed EMI of ₹30,000 feels lighter after ten years of pay rises, because inflation shrinks the real value of what you owe.
…until interest rates riseWhen inflation is high, RBI raises its repo rate. Most new floating-rate home loans in India are linked to an external benchmark (usually repo), so your rate rises — the bank either raises the EMI or lengthens the tenure.
Lenders and savers loseUnexpected inflation quietly moves wealth from people who lent at a fixed rate (depositors, bond holders) to people who borrowed at one.

5Long goals: children's education, retirement

Rule of 70: divide 70 by the inflation rate to get the number of years in which prices double. At 6%, prices double in about 12 years; at 10%, in about 7.

  • Education and healthcare costs in India have often risen faster than headline CPI, so plan with a higher rate for these goals.
  • Retirement can last 25–30 years. At 6% inflation, the monthly expense you need at 60 is roughly a third of what you will need at 80. A retirement plan without an inflation adjustment is incomplete.

6What inflation does to the country

The poor pay mostFood and fuel take a bigger share of a poor family's budget, and they have little savings in assets that rise with prices. Inflation works like a tax that falls hardest on those least able to pay — the "inflation tax".
Interest rates and growthTo fight inflation RBI raises rates, which slows borrowing, housing, car sales and business investment. Controlling prices can cost some growth and jobs in the short run.
Government budgetInflation raises tax collections in rupees, but also raises the cost of food and fertiliser subsidies, salaries and DA. Moderate inflation also shrinks the real burden of past government debt.
The rupee and importsIndia imports more than 85% of its crude oil. Costlier oil or a weaker rupee feeds straight into fuel, transport and then everything else — "imported inflation".
Exports and competitivenessIf India's prices rise faster than its trading partners', its goods become less competitive unless the rupee weakens to compensate.
Trust and planningStable prices let farmers, firms and families plan. Persistent high inflation leads people to buy gold and property instead of investing in productive businesses.

7The global picture

  • Shared shocks. Oil, gas, food grains and fertiliser are priced globally. A war, a pandemic or a shipping blockage raises prices everywhere at once. The IMF expects world inflation to rise from 4.1% in 2025 to 4.7% in 2026 because of the energy shock from the war in the Middle East.
  • 2021–23, the big surge. After COVID, reopening demand met broken supply chains, and then the Russia–Ukraine war spiked energy and food prices. US inflation peaked at 9.1% (June 2022), the UK's at 11.1% (October 2022) — the highest in about 40 years.
  • The US Federal Reserve matters to India. When the Fed raises rates to fight US inflation, money flows out of emerging markets to the US, the dollar strengthens, the rupee weakens, and India's import bill rises.
  • Uneven pain. Emerging economies usually face higher inflation than advanced ones (IMF: about 5.7% versus 3.1% for 2026), and poorer importing countries face food insecurity when grain prices spike.

8Sensible habits in an inflationary world

  • Think in real terms: compare every raise, rent hike and return against inflation.
  • Keep an emergency fund in cash, but avoid leaving long-term money idle.
  • Plan big goals using a realistic inflation rate (and a higher one for education and health).
  • When rates are rising, check whether a floating-rate loan is lengthening its tenure quietly.
  • Spread savings across different kinds of assets rather than betting on one.

9Check yourself

Level 3 · Review for experts

A compact refresher: measurement, theory, welfare costs, policy frameworks, India-specific issues and the global transmission of inflation.

1Definition and measurement

Inflation is a sustained increase in the aggregate price level, equivalently a sustained fall in the purchasing power of money: πt = (Pt − Pt−12) / Pt−12 for monthly year-on-year data. A one-off level shift (e.g., a tax change) is not inflation in this strict sense, though it shows up in twelve months of y-o-y prints.

Index construction

Laspeyres: P_L = Σ p_t·q_0 / Σ p_0·q_0 (fixed base-period basket → upward substitution bias) Paasche: P_P = Σ p_t·q_t / Σ p_0·q_t (current basket → downward bias) Fisher: P_F = √(P_L · P_P) (superlative; approximates a true cost-of-living index)

Measurement biases

Substitution (item and outlet), quality change, new goods and new outlets all tend to make fixed-basket indices overstate cost-of-living inflation. The 1996 US Boskin Commission put the total upward bias at about 1.1 percentage points a year — one reason central banks target a positive rate rather than zero. Hedonic adjustment, chain-linking and scanner data have narrowed but not closed the gap.

2Theories — what drives the price level

Quantity theory (long run)

M·V = P·Y → π ≈ g_M + g_V − g_Y

Over long horizons and across countries, money growth and inflation move together ("always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon" — Friedman). In the short run velocity is unstable, which is why money-growth targeting was largely abandoned by the 1990s.

Phillips curve and expectations

Expectations-augmented: π_t = π^e_t − β(u_t − u*) + ν_t New Keynesian (NKPC): π_t = β·E_t[π_{t+1}] + κ·x_t + ε_t (x = output gap / real marginal cost)

Friedman and Phelps (1967–68) argued no lasting trade-off exists between inflation and unemployment; the 1970s stagflation proved it. Expectations are the key state variable — hence the stress on anchoring. The post-1990s flattening of the curve (small κ) made the 2021–22 surge harder to forecast; evidence suggests the curve is non-linear and steepens when labour markets are very tight.

Demand-pull, cost-push and second-round effects

Supply shocks (oil, food, exchange rate) shift the relative price of a subset of goods. They become persistent inflation only if they feed into wages and expectations — the second-round effects that policy aims to stop. The standard advice is to "look through" first-round supply shocks, provided expectations remain anchored. That condition is the hard part.

Fiscal theory of the price level

B_{t−1} / P_t = E_t Σ (discounted real primary surpluses)

If fiscal surpluses do not adjust to back government debt, the price level adjusts instead. This framework (Leeper, Sims, Woodford, Cochrane) gives a fiscal reading of the 2021 surge, after large transfers financed by debt.

3Welfare costs of inflation

Anticipated inflationUnanticipated inflation
  • Shoe-leather costs — economising on money balances (Bailey's deadweight triangle under the money-demand curve)
  • Menu costs — price changes are costly, so prices are staggered
  • Relative-price dispersion — staggered pricing misallocates resources (the core welfare cost in New Keynesian models)
  • Tax distortions — nominal capital gains and interest taxed; unindexed slabs create "bracket creep"
  • Redistribution from nominal creditors to debtors, from fixed-income earners to asset holders
  • Uncertainty — higher inflation tends to be more variable, which raises risk premia and shortens contract horizons
  • Inflation tax / seigniorage on money holders, regressive because the poor hold more cash
  • Hyperinflation (Cagan: above 50% per month) — the money stops working as a unit of account

Why not target zero? The zero lower bound on nominal rates (room to cut in recessions), downward nominal wage rigidity (the "grease" argument) and the measurement bias above. Together these underpin the common 2% target in advanced economies; emerging markets typically choose higher targets to allow for catch-up growth and Balassa–Samuelson effects.

4Policy frameworks and rules

India's flexible inflation targeting (FIT)

  • The RBI Act was amended in 2016. The target, set by the government in consultation with RBI, is 4% CPI-Combined inflation with a 2–6% tolerance band, decided by a six-member Monetary Policy Committee (three from RBI, three external members; the Governor has a casting vote).
  • Failure is defined as average inflation outside the band for three consecutive quarters. RBI must then explain to the government in writing why, what it will do, and when it expects to return to target. This first happened in 2022.
  • October 2026's 6.09% is the first print above 6% in the new CPI series. One month alone does not trigger the failure clause, but it raises the stakes for the December 2026 MPC meeting.

Taylor rule — illustrative calculator

i = r* + π + a_π(π − π*) + a_y·(output gap) (Taylor 1993: a_π = a_y = 0.5)

Illustrative only: r* and the output gap are unobservable and highly uncertain for India, and RBI does not follow a mechanical rule. Use it to see the Taylor principle at work: the nominal rate must move more than one-for-one with inflation (1 + aπ > 1) so the real rate rises when inflation rises.

Transmission and credibility

  • Channels: interest rate, credit, exchange rate, asset prices and expectations. Lags are long and variable — several quarters before the full effect on inflation shows up.
  • Transmission improved after new floating-rate loans were linked to external benchmarks (from October 2019), but deposit-rate pass-through remains slower.
  • Credibility lowers the sacrifice ratio: anchored expectations let a central bank disinflate with less output loss.

5India-specific issues

  • Food dominance. High food weight and weather-driven vegetable cycles (the TOP volatility of tomato, onion and potato) make headline CPI volatile. The 2023–24 Economic Survey suggested exploring a target that excludes food; the counter-argument is that food inflation feeds household expectations and wages, so ignoring it risks de-anchoring. The 2024-base series, with a lower food weight, changes this debate.
  • Rural–urban gap. In October 2026, rural inflation was 6.63% and urban 5.41%. Food's larger share of the rural basket drives the gap.
  • Administered prices. Fuel taxes, MSP, electricity tariffs and the public distribution system blur the line between monetary and fiscal control of the price level.
  • Imported inflation. Crude oil, edible oils and fertiliser, plus rupee depreciation. The exchange-rate pass-through to CPI is modest but meaningful in fuel-heavy shocks.
  • Fiscal–monetary interaction. Combined general-government debt is high; the FIT framework is partly a commitment against fiscal dominance.
  • Base effects. The October 2025 trough (0.25%, after GST rationalisation) mechanically inflates late-2026 year-on-year prints, so month-on-month seasonally adjusted momentum is the better guide.

6Global transmission

  • Common factor. A large share of national inflation variance is explained by a global factor (commodities, the dollar, global slack). Globalisation reduced the sensitivity of domestic inflation to domestic slack.
  • The dollar cycle. Fed tightening → stronger dollar → capital outflows from emerging markets → depreciation → imported inflation, often forcing emerging-market central banks to tighten during a slowdown.
  • 2021–23. The "transitory" call failed because supply shocks met strong fiscal-backed demand and very tight labour markets. The Fed, ECB and BoE all hiked late but hard; disinflation came with less unemployment than the sacrifice-ratio literature predicted (a "soft-landing" debate still open).
  • 2026. The IMF's October 2026 WEO projects global inflation of 4.7% in 2026, easing to 4.1% in 2027. It describes the global disinflation as paused by the war-driven energy supply shock, with emerging economies (about 5.7%) above advanced ones (about 3.1%).
  • Structural forces to watch. Geoeconomic fragmentation and reshoring (cost-raising), the energy transition and climate shocks ("climateflation" and "greenflation"), ageing workforces, and AI-driven productivity (potentially disinflationary).

7Review checklist — open questions

  1. Should emerging markets with high food weights target headline or core inflation — and does the new CPI basket change the answer for India?
  2. How flat is the Phillips curve, and is it non-linear? What does that imply for the cost of the "last mile" of disinflation?
  3. How large and how stable is r* after the pandemic, and does it differ for India versus advanced economies?
  4. When does a supply shock become a demand problem? What are the best real-time measures of de-anchoring (surveys, breakevens, wage settlements)?
  5. How much of the 2021–22 surge was fiscal (FTPL) and how much was supply-driven?
  6. Should central banks respond to climate-related price shocks, or look through them?
