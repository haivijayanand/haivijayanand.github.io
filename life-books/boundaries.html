Boundaries

Henry Cloud and John Townsend · first published 1992 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Healthy relationships need clear personal boundaries: knowing what you are and are not responsible for lets you love others generously without being controlled, drained or resentful.

Summary

Henry Cloud and John Townsend, two Christian psychologists, wrote this book for people who find it hard to say no. They compare a boundary to a property line. It shows where you end and someone else begins, what belongs to you, and what does not. Without clear lines, people end up carrying burdens that are not theirs while neglecting the ones that are.

A key idea is the difference between being responsible to someone and being responsible for them. We owe our family and friends care, honesty and help in real need. But each adult is responsible for their own feelings, choices, attitudes and behaviour. Rescuing someone from every consequence of their actions does not help them grow.

The authors describe common boundary problems. Some people say yes to things they should refuse because they fear conflict or disapproval. Some cannot say yes to good things, such as accepting help. Others push past other people's limits, and some simply fail to notice what others need.

They also set out principles for how boundaries work. Actions have natural consequences, and we should let people experience them. We can only change ourselves, not others. We must respect other people's limits if we want ours respected. And a boundary is only real when it is backed by action.

Later chapters apply these ideas to parents, friends, spouses, children, work and even our own habits. In marriage, boundaries protect closeness rather than destroying it, because love freely given means more than love that is demanded. With children, parents can teach limits through clear expectations and calm consequences.

The authors warn that setting boundaries brings resistance, both from others who liked the old arrangement and from our own guilt. Their advice is to start small, find supportive people, and stay kind but firm.

298 words

One-line takeaway: This week, notice one request you usually agree to out of guilt, and practise a calm, kind no, offering a clear alternative if you wish, then let the other person handle their own reaction.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What image do the authors use to explain a personal boundary?

    Answer B. They compare a boundary to a property line that shows what belongs to you and what does not.

  2. What is the key difference the book draws in relationships?

    Answer A. We owe others care and help, but each adult is responsible for their own choices and feelings.

  3. According to the book, what usually happens when you rescue someone from every consequence?

    Answer D. Shielding people from natural consequences removes the very lessons that help them change.

  4. Which of these is described as a common boundary problem?

    Answer C. Many people agree to things they should decline because they fear disapproval or conflict.

  5. Why do the authors say some people struggle to receive help?

    Answer A. One boundary problem is refusing good things, such as help or support, from others.

  6. Who does the book say you can truly change?

    Answer D. A core principle is that we have power over our own actions, not over other people.

  7. What makes a boundary real, according to the book?

    Answer C. A limit only works when it is followed by consistent action, not just words.

  8. How do the authors view boundaries within marriage?

    Answer A. Boundaries keep love a free choice, which strengthens rather than weakens intimacy.

  9. What should you expect when you first start setting boundaries?

    Answer B. The authors warn that others may push back and our own guilt may make us want to give in.

  10. How do the authors suggest parents teach limits to children?

    Answer B. Children learn limits best when expectations are clear and consequences are calm and consistent.
