@sub Replacing job-specific spot inks with one fixed CMYKOGV set: history, how builds work, benefits for gravure, colour reduction, alternatives, limits and a roadmap
@outline From three-colour process to ECG | What Extended Color Gamut is | Why gravure suits ECG | Advantages for a gravure converter | Using ECG to reduce colours: a worked job | Alternative schemes compared | Limitations | Roadmap, trends and conclusion
@tnotes A typical gravure packaging job uses CMYK plus several spot colours, each with its own cylinder and ink washup. Extended Color Gamut printing replaces the spots with a fixed set of seven process inks. This talk explains how it works, why gravure is a natural fit, what it saves, where it falls short, and how a converter can adopt it step by step.

T: A century of colour schemes | tab1
N: The history runs from three-colour process at the end of the nineteenth century, through four-colour CMYK, Pantone spot colours in the 1960s, to earlier fixed-palette attempts, Hexachrome in 1994 and Opaltone, which failed mainly because their inks and profiles were proprietary. ECG is the first with open inks, international standards and mainstream software.

B: What Extended Color Gamut is
- Fixed set of seven process inks: C, M, Y, K + Orange, Green, Violet
- Spot colours (Pantone, brand L*a*b*, CxF) become builds from the 7-colour profile
- Orange replaces M+Y in reds; Green replaces C+Y; Violet replaces C+M
- Complementary pairs never overprint: at most 3 chromatic inks + K per colour
- CMYK reaches ≈50–60 % of Pantone; CMYKOGV ≈90 %
N: ECG adds orange, green and violet to CMYK and keeps the same seven inks on press for every job. A separation engine finds the simplest build for each spot colour from the press's seven-colour profile. Orange takes over from magenta plus yellow, green from cyan plus yellow, violet from cyan plus magenta. Builds of two or three inks are less sensitive to register and dot gain than four-ink CMYK builds, and the palette reaches about 90 per cent of the Pantone library.

B: Gamut: what ECG can and cannot reach
- CMYK alone: roughly half to 60 % of Pantone within tight tolerance
- Well-run CMYKOGV: about 90 % (substrate, ink and press dependent)
- Outside the gamut: bright fluorescents, light pastels, true metallics, opaque white
- Two- and three-ink builds are more robust to register and dot gain than four-ink CMYK builds
N: The gamut figures are approximate industry numbers. CMYK alone matches about half of the Pantone library, ECG about nine in ten. What stays outside are very bright fluorescent tones, pastels that need a light ink, true metallics and opaque white. Those are the reasons for keeping one or two open stations.

T: Standards and tools | tab2
N: ECG is supported by standards: FOGRA55 characterisation data for seven-colour printing, ISO 20654 for spot-colour tone value, ISO/TS 21328 for multi-colour data, and ISO 12647 process control. Mainstream separation and colour-management software supports it.

B: Why gravure suits ECG
- Cylinders engraved by a closed digital process: highly repeatable tone
- Very little cylinder wear over a run
- Every spot colour costs a cylinder and an ink-station washup
- Weak points to control: register and ink stability (viscosity, solvent balance)
N: Gravure is a natural fit. Cylinders are engraved digitally and barely wear, so tone reproduction is very repeatable, which is what a fixed seven-colour profile needs. And in gravure each spot colour is expensive: a cylinder to engrave, store and handle, and an ink washup at every job change. The weak points are register and ink stability, which must be held tightly.

T: Advantages of ECG in gravure | tab3
N: The benefits come mostly across a sequence of jobs, not within one. Fewer ink changes mean shorter makeready and less solvent and ink waste. Jobs with the same seven inks can be ganged. Ink inventory shrinks to seven process inks. Colour becomes data-driven and repeatable between presses and sites.

B: Converting a job to ECG
- List every colour: images, spots, whites, varnishes, metallics
- Keep non-convertible items as spots: opaque white, lacquer, true metallics, fluorescents
- Convert remaining spots to builds; check ΔE00 against tolerance (≤2–3)
- Prefer two-ink builds, three at most, K only for depth
- Watch large flat areas: halftone builds can mottle
N: Converting a job follows simple rules. Separate what cannot be built from process inks, opaque white, metallics, fluorescents and any colour the brand owner insists on as a solid. Convert the rest, checking predicted colour difference against the brand tolerance. Keep builds simple, and be careful with large flat areas where a halftone build may look mottled.

T: Worked example: a 9-colour job becomes 7 | tab4
N: In the worked example, a snack pack printed with CMYK plus several spot colours and white is converted. The brand reds, greens and blues become two- or three-ink builds from the extended set, leaving only white as an open station. The job drops colours, and more importantly, its seven inks are the same as the next job's.

B: Reduced-ink variants (ISO/TS 21328)
- CMYK + O: warm categories (snacks, biscuits, confectionery)
- CMYK + OV: reds plus blue or purple brand colours
- CMYK + OG: close to Hexachrome; produce, herbal and beverage packs
- Keeps stations free for whites and coatings on 8-colour presses
N: Not every job needs all three extra inks. A five- or six-colour subset suits many product categories and keeps stations free for white and coatings on an eight-colour press, at the cost of a smaller gamut.

B: Why earlier fixed palettes failed
- Hexachrome (CMYKOG, 1994): proprietary inks and licences; discontinued 2008
- Opaltone (CMYK + RGB): seven colours, but closed system; now inactive
- Lacked open standards, generic profiles and mainstream software
- ECG differs: open inks, FOGRA55 and ISO standards, broad software support
N: Fixed palettes are not new. Pantone's Hexachrome and the Opaltone system both extended the gamut well, but each tied converters to proprietary inks, licences and profiles, and neither had international standards behind it. ECG is the first fixed palette that is open, standardised and supported by mainstream software, which is why it has a better chance.

T: Disadvantages and limitations | tab6 | cols=0,1,2
N: ECG is not free. Register must be held tightly because colours are built from overprints. Ink viscosity and cylinder engraving must be stable. Some colours, fluorescents and very saturated brand colours, stay outside the gamut. Large solids can mottle. Brand owners must accept data-based approval instead of drawdowns. And the first profiling and training costs time.

F: An adoption roadmap for gravure converters | fig1
- Phase 1: assess press stability, register, ink control
- Phase 2: fingerprint and profile one press
- Phase 3: pilot jobs with willing brand owners
- Phase 4: roll out, gang jobs, extend to other presses
N: The paper proposes a four-phase roadmap with three go/no-go gates. First make sure the press can hold register and ink stably. Then fingerprint and profile it. Then run pilot jobs with brand owners willing to approve by data. Only then roll out, gang jobs and extend the profile to other presses.

B: Current trends (2024–2026)
- ECG sold as a system: press, register, inks, cylinders and workflow (BOBST oneECG)
- Metallic effects simulated with the fixed set over metallised film (2025)
- Ink makers offering ECG sets and colour services
- FOGRA55 and ISO/TS 21328 replace proprietary profiles
- Same seven inks across gravure, flexo and digital
N: ECG in gravure has moved from trials to production in Europe. Press makers now sell it as a complete system. A 2025 development simulates gold, silver and bronze from process inks over metallised film. And with common standards, the same seven-colour data can drive gravure, flexo and digital presses.

2: Hybrid: the realistic target
L: Run with ECG
- Images and most brand colours
- Two- and three-ink builds
- Same seven inks every job
R: Keep open stations for
- Opaque white
- True metallics and lacquers
- One critical brand solid, if required
N: For most converters the realistic target is a hybrid: seven ECG stations for images and most brand colours, plus one or two open stations for white, metallics, lacquer or a brand solid the owner will not convert. On a nine- or ten-colour press that is very achievable.

C: Conclusion
- First fixed-palette scheme with open inks, standards and mainstream software
- Value in gravure: no ink changes across a sequence of jobs
- Enables ganging, shorter runs, less solvent and ink waste
- Not a universal replacement for spot colours
FW: Requirements
- Tight register, ink and cylinder control
- Brand-owner acceptance of data-based approval
- ECG + 1–2 open stations as the target
N: ECG has a better chance than Hexachrome or Opaltone because it is open and standardised. In gravure its value lies less in the station count of a single job than in eliminating ink changes across a sequence of jobs, which enables ganging, shorter runs and lower waste.
