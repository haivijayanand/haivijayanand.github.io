Random vibration · acceleration spectral density

Reading g²/Hz

A PSD in g²/Hz tells you how the mean-square acceleration of a random signal is spread over frequency. Each part of the unit carries meaning, and every lab below lets you change one thing and watch what moves.

g²

Mean-square acceleration. Squaring turns amplitude into power. Uncorrelated random components add in g², never in g, so power is the quantity you can add up across frequency.

/Hz

Per hertz of bandwidth. Dividing by the analysis bandwidth makes the number independent of the frequency resolution you chose. It is a density, like kg/m³.

∫ → √ = Grms

Area gives the total. Integrate g²/Hz over Hz and you get g². Take the square root and you get the overall RMS acceleration, Grms.

1 · The physical meaning

A filter, a square, and an average

The cleanest way to picture a PSD value is as a measurement you could make with hardware. Pass the acceleration signal through an ideal band-pass filter 1 Hz wide, centred at frequency f. Square the filter output and average it over a long time. The result, in g², is the PSD value at f in g²/Hz. Repeat for every frequency and you have the spectrum.

If the filter were 4 Hz wide, you would collect roughly four times the power, so you divide by 4 Hz to get back to a per-hertz number. That division is why a PSD stays the same when you change analyser resolution, while a plain FFT amplitude spectrum of noise does not.

Gaa(f) = limΔf→0 ⟨ aΔf²(t) ⟩ / Δf [g²/Hz] Grms = √( ∫ Gaa(f) df )

Worked example: a flat 0.04 g²/Hz from 20 Hz to 2000 Hz has an area of 0.04 × 1980 = 79.2 g². The overall level is √79.2 = 8.90 Grms. Doubling the PSD to 0.08 g²/Hz (+3 dB) raises Grms only by √2, to 12.6 Grms.

2 · Lab · FFT amplitude versus PSD

Why random vibration is quoted as a density

Try this: change the frequency resolution Δf. The noise floor of the amplitude spectrum moves by √2 per step, but the PSD floor stays at the level you set. The sine does the opposite: its amplitude stays at A, while its PSD peak grows as Δf shrinks. A PSD is the right scale for broadband random signals, and the wrong scale for reading a sine amplitude.

What the readouts show

  • Parseval check. The Grms from the area under the PSD matches the RMS computed straight from the time record. The PSD redistributes the signal's mean square over frequency without losing any of it.
  • Sine in a PSD. A sine of peak amplitude A carries A²/2 g² of mean square, all in one frequency. The PSD spreads that over the analysis bandwidth, so its peak reads (A²/2) ÷ (ENBW·Δf), where ENBW is 1.5 bins for a Hann window. Halve Δf and the spike doubles.
  • Noise in an amplitude spectrum. Each bin collects noise power in proportion to its width, so the amplitude floor scales with √Δf. A reader who does not know Δf cannot interpret it.
  • Record length. A PSD needs T = K ÷ Δf seconds of data. Finer resolution or more averages both cost time.
3 · Estimation quality

Resolution, averaging and confidence

A single FFT of a random signal gives a PSD estimate that scatters wildly around the true level; each bin follows a chi-square distribution with only 2 degrees of freedom. Averaging K independent segments raises this to 2K degrees of freedom and shrinks the normalised random error to about 1/√K. Set K to 1 in the lab above and watch the PSD become grass; set it to 64 and it tightens to about ±1.2 dB.

εr ≈ 1/√(Be·T) ≈ 1/√K DOF = 2K T = K / Δf Be = ENBW·Δf (Hann: 1.5·Δf)

Resolution trades against this. Around a lightly damped resonance the true PSD peak has a half-power bandwidth of fn/Q. If Δf is wider than about a quarter of that, the estimate smears the peak and under-reads it. That is bias error, and averaging cannot remove it. Choose Δf from the sharpest peak you care about, then choose K from the record length you can afford.

Slopes, segments and the exact integral

Between breakpoints (f1, P1) and (f2, P2) the profile is P(f) = P1(f/f1)b. The exponent and the slope are two views of the same line: +3 dB/oct means b = 1 (PSD doubles every octave), −6 dB/oct means b = −2.

b = ln(P2/P1) / ln(f2/f1) slope = 10·log10(P2/P1) / log2(f2/f1) dB/oct
Area = P1f1/(b+1) · [ (f2/f1)b+1 − 1 ] (b = −1: Area = P1f1·ln(f2/f1))

Do not integrate a log-log profile with straight trapezoids in linear space. On steep segments the error is several percent in Grms, and the power-law formula is exact.

The level slider shows a rule worth memorising: a change of x dB in PSD changes Grms by x/2 dB in amplitude terms. −6 dB halves Grms; +3 dB multiplies it by 1.41.

5 · From acceleration to motion

Velocity and displacement from the same PSD

Each integration in time divides the spectrum by (2πf)² in PSD terms. The acceleration PSD first converts to SI units (1 g²/Hz = 96.17 (m/s²)²/Hz), then:

Gvv(f) = Gaa(f)·g0² / (2πf)² Gdd(f) = Gaa(f)·g0² / (2πf)4 g0 = 9.80665 m/s²

The 1/f4 weighting means displacement is dominated by the lowest frequencies in the profile, even when they carry little of the g². Lower the first breakpoint in the table and watch the displacement readout jump while Grms barely moves. This is the figure to check against shaker stroke: the 3σ peak-to-peak displacement (6 × Drms) must fit inside the armature's usable travel with margin.

6 · Lab · Single-degree-of-freedom response

Miles' equation

A component on a resonance amplifies the base PSD by its transmissibility squared, |H(f)|². When the input PSD is roughly flat across the resonance and damping is light, the response integral collapses to Miles' equation. This lab drives an SDOF system with the profile from Lab 4 and compares Miles' answer with the exact numerical integral.

Grms,resp ≈ √( π/2 · fn · Q · P(fn) ) |H|² = [1 + (2ζr)²] / [(1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²], r = f/fn, ζ = 1/(2Q)

When to trust Miles

  • The input PSD should be close to flat over roughly fn/2 to 2fn. Put fn on a steep slope or near a breakpoint and the gap to the exact value widens.
  • Q should be moderate to high (about 5 and above). At low Q the response spreads over a wide band and the flat-input assumption breaks down.
  • Miles gives the RMS of absolute acceleration of the mass. For stress and fatigue, the relative displacement Zrms ≈ Grms,resp·g0/(2πfn)² is usually more useful.
  • Response peaks are Gaussian too: 3σ = 3 × Grms,resp is the usual design peak, exceeded about 0.27 % of the time.

PSD is a power quantity, so dB = 10·log10(ratio).

Unit conversions

  • 1 g²/Hz = 96.17 (m/s²)²/Hz
  • One-sided G(f) = 2 × two-sided S(f), for f > 0. Test specifications and controllers use one-sided.
  • PSD in dB re 1 g²/Hz: 0.01 g²/Hz = −20 dB
  • ASD in g/√Hz is the square root of the PSD. 0.04 g²/Hz = 0.2 g/√Hz.

Gaussian peaks

  • ±1σ contains 68.3 % of samples
  • ±2σ contains 95.4 %
  • ±3σ contains 99.73 %
  • Many shaker controllers let you clip the drive at a set sigma limit, often 3σ. Instantaneous peaks of 4 to 5 Grms occur in unclipped long records.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a sine amplitude off a PSD without knowing Δf and the window.
  • Comparing Grms from two PSDs over different frequency ranges.
  • Summing Grms of bands directly. Add g² (areas), then take the root.
  • Using the amplitude-window correction on a PSD. PSD normalisation uses Σw², which accounts for the window's noise bandwidth.
  • Too coarse a Δf around a sharp resonance: bias that averaging cannot fix.
