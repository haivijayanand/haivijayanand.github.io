Shaft Deflection, Rigidity and Critical Speed

A shaft can be strong enough and still fail its job by bending too much, twisting too much, or whirling at its critical speed. Learn to calculate deflections and slopes, angle of twist, and the critical speed by Rayleigh's and Dunkerley's methods.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: Dunkerley and Rayleigh❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Find shaft deflection and slope by superposition of standard cases
  • Know the limits on deflection, slope and twist for gears and bearings
  • Calculate the torsional rigidity and angle of twist
  • Estimate the first critical (whirling) speed with Rayleigh and Dunkerley
  • Design to run safely below or above the critical speed

2Lateral rigidity

Simply supported, central load W: δmax = W·L³ / (48·E·I) slope at bearings θ = W·L² / (16·E·I)
Load W at a from left (b = L − a), deflection at x ≤ a: y = W·b·x·(L² − b² − x²) / (6·E·I·L)
Several loads: add the deflections (superposition)
LimitTypical value
Deflection at a gear0.01 × module (spur), or 0.0002–0.0005 × span
Slope at a spur gear≤ 0.0005 rad
Slope at a deep groove ball bearing≤ 0.001–0.003 rad
Slope at a cylindrical or tapered roller bearing≤ 0.0005–0.001 rad
Machine-tool spindlesMuch tighter — set by accuracy

3Torsional rigidity

θ = T·L / (G·J) J = π·d⁴/32, G ≈ 79–81 GPa for steel
Common limit: 0.25° per metre for line shafts and machine-tool drives; 1°/m for general shafts

Torsional stiffness also sets the torsional natural frequency of a drive train (motor, coupling, gearbox, load): f = (1/2π)·√(k/I) for two inertias joined by stiffness k. Gearbox test rigs and engine drives must keep this away from firing and gear-mesh frequencies.

4Critical speed (whirling)

A rotor is never perfectly balanced. As speed rises, the out-of-balance force bends the shaft, which increases the eccentricity, which increases the force. At the critical speed — the natural frequency of lateral vibration — the deflection becomes large, limited only by damping. Above it the rotor "inverts" and runs more smoothly again (super-critical running, as in turbines).

Single disc: ωc = √(g / δst) → Nc ≈ 946 / √δst(mm) rpm
Rayleigh (upper estimate): ωc = √( g·Σmᵢyᵢ / Σmᵢyᵢ² ) (yᵢ = static deflections under all loads)
Dunkerley (lower estimate): 1/ωc² = 1/ωs² + Σ 1/ωᵢ² (ωᵢ for each mass alone, ωs for the shaft alone)
Uniform shaft, simply supported: ωs = (π/L)²·√(E·I / (ρ·A))
Keep away from the critical speed. Run below about 0.7 Nc, or above about 1.3–1.4 Nc and pass through quickly. Rotating machinery standards (API, ISO) require a separation margin of 15–20 % or more.

5Materials and cost

All steels have the same E, so stiffness costs diameter: δ ∝ 1/d⁴. A 19 % larger diameter halves the deflection. Shorter spans help even more (δ ∝ L³). Moving bearings closer to the gear is usually the cheapest fix of all.

6Worked example

Ø50 steel shaft, 900 mm span, discs 30 kg at 270 mm and 20 kg at 630 mm, running 1450 rpmI = π×0.05⁴/64 = 3.07×10⁻⁷ m⁴, E = 207 GPa Static deflections under the discs (both loads acting): 77.4 µm and 74.7 µm Shaft alone: ωs → 7469 rpm Rayleigh (combined with the shaft by Dunkerley): 3112 rpm Dunkerley: 3009 rpm Running at 1450 rpm = 48 % of the critical speed — sub-critical with a good margin.

7Procedure

  1. Find the static deflection under each mass by superposition (include the shaft's weight for long shafts).
  2. Calculate the critical speed by Rayleigh (and Dunkerley as a lower bound).
  3. Compare with the running speed range; keep the separation margin.
  4. Check slopes at the bearings and gears against their limits.
  5. If too flexible: shorten spans, enlarge the diameter, add a bearing, or move heavy discs toward the supports.
  6. For important rotors, confirm with FEA or a transfer-matrix program, including bearing stiffness.

8Lab: Dunkerley and Rayleigh

Move the discs, change the span and diameter. The curve is the static deflected shape; the critical speeds and the running margin update live.

9Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Bearings and housings are not rigid. Real critical speeds are 10–30 % lower than rigid-support calculations.
  • Overhung masses (a pulley or fan outside the bearings) lower the critical speed sharply.
  • Variable-speed drives sweep through many frequencies — check the whole speed range, not just the rated speed.
  • Vibration measurements with a run-up (Bode plot) find the real critical speed of an installed machine.

10Practice

  1. In the lab, find the shaft diameter needed to keep 3000 rpm below 0.7 Nc.
  2. Move both discs to the centre. Why does the critical speed fall?
  3. A 2 m line shaft of Ø60 carries 4 kN·m. Find the angle of twist. Does it meet 0.25°/m?
