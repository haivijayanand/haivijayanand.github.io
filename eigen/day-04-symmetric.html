Symmetric Matrices: Principal Stresses and Axes

Symmetric matrices are the best-behaved matrices there are: real eigenvalues, perpendicular eigenvectors, always diagonalisable. Nature hands them to engineers everywhere — stress, strain, inertia, stiffness, covariance — and their eigenvectors are the "principal axes" in which the physics becomes simple.

1The spectral theorem

Spectral theorem. If A is real and symmetric (A = Aᵀ) then
  1. all its eigenvalues are real;
  2. eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are perpendicular;
  3. it always has a full set of n perpendicular unit eigenvectors, so
    A = Q Λ Qᵀ, QᵀQ = I
    where the columns of Q are the unit eigenvectors and Λ is diagonal.

Q is a rotation (possibly with a reflection), so the theorem says: every symmetric matrix is a pure stretch along some set of perpendicular axes. No shear, no twisting — just turn to the right axes, stretch by λ₁, λ₂, …, turn back. That is why the image of a circle under a symmetric matrix is an ellipse whose axes are the eigenvectors (you saw it on Day 1).

Proof of the first two statements

Real eigenvalues. Let Av = λv with v possibly complex, and let v̄ be its conjugate. Then v̄ᵀAv = λ v̄ᵀv = λ|v|². Taking the conjugate transpose of the scalar v̄ᵀAv and using A = Aᵀ real gives the same scalar, so it is real; |v|² is real and positive; hence λ is real.

Perpendicular eigenvectors. Let Av₁ = λ₁v₁, Av₂ = λ₂v₂. Then λ₁ v₂ᵀv₁ = v₂ᵀAv₁ = (Av₂)ᵀv₁ = λ₂ v₂ᵀv₁. If λ₁ ≠ λ₂ then v₂ᵀv₁ = 0. The third statement (a complete set even with repeated λ) is proved by induction: take one unit eigenvector, the subspace perpendicular to it is mapped into itself by A, and repeat there.

2Principal stresses — the eigenproblem of solid mechanics

The state of stress at a point is the symmetric matrix of normal and shear stresses. In 2-D:

S = [σx τxy ; τxy σy] traction on a face with unit normal n: t = S n

Ask: is there a face on which the force is purely normal — no shear at all? That means S n = σ n. It is exactly the eigenproblem. The eigenvalues are the principal stresses σ₁ ≥ σ₂ (largest and smallest normal stress at that point) and the eigenvectors are the principal directions. Because S is symmetric they are always real and always 90° apart.

σ₁,₂ = (σx + σy)/2 ± √( ((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy² ) tan 2θp = 2τxy / (σx − σy)

That formula is just the 2 × 2 eigenvalue formula trace/2 ± √(trace²/4 − det). Mohr's circle is its picture: centre at the mean stress, radius equal to the maximum shear, and the two points where it crosses the σ axis are the eigenvalues.

Example 1 — a point on a bracket: σx = 80 MPa, σy = −20 MPa, τxy = 40 MPamean = 30, R = √(50² + 40²) = 64.0 MPa σ₁ = 30 + 64.0 = 94.0 MPa, σ₂ = 30 − 64.0 = −34.0 MPa θp = ½·atan(80/100) = 19.3° (σ₁ direction) max in-plane shear = R = 64.0 MPa on faces at 45° to the principal directions von Mises = √(94.0² − 94.0·(−34.0) + 34.0²) = 114.9 MPa
Example 2 — a gearbox shaft in bending + torsion: σ = 60 MPa, τ = 40 MPaS = [60 40; 40 0] → σ₁,₂ = 30 ± √(30² + 40²) = 30 ± 50 = 80 and −20 MPa von Mises = √(80² + 80·20 + 20²) = √8400 = 91.7 MPa = √(σ² + 3τ²) ✓ A brittle shaft cracks perpendicular to σ₁ — on a helix at 45° to the axis in pure torsion, which is exactly how a chalk stick or a cast-iron shaft breaks when twisted.
Example 3 — a full 3-D stress stateS = [50 30 0 ; 30 −20 0 ; 0 0 40] MPa z is already principal (no shear on z faces): σ = 40 The x–y block [50 30; 30 −20] gives 15 ± √(35² + 30²) = 15 ± 46.1 Principal stresses: σ₁ = 61.1, σ₂ = 40.0, σ₃ = −31.1 MPa Absolute max shear = (σ₁ − σ₃)/2 = 46.1 MPa (Tresca)

3Principal axes of sections and bodies

The second moment of area about an axis in direction a is a quadratic form I(a) = aᵀ J a with the symmetric matrix J = [Ix −Ixy; −Ixy Iy]. Its eigenvectors are the principal axes; about them the product of inertia is zero and bending in one plane does not cause deflection in the other.

Example 4 — equal angle 100 × 100 × 10 mm (sharp corners)Centroid 28.7 mm from the back of each leg Ix = Iy = 180.0 cm⁴, Ixy = −106.6 cm⁴ J = [180.0 106.6 ; 106.6 180.0] cm⁴ Eigenvalues: Iu = 180.0 + 106.6 = 286.6 cm⁴ (about the axis of symmetry, at 45°) Iv = 180.0 − 106.6 = 73.4 cm⁴ (the weak axis, perpendicular to it) An angle used as a strut buckles about v–v, with Iv only 41 % of Ix — which is why steel tables list I_v and r_v separately.

In 3-D the same applies to the 3 × 3 inertia tensor of a rigid body: its eigenvectors are the principal axes of rotation and its eigenvalues the principal moments of inertia. A body spinning about a principal axis spins steadily; about any other axis it wobbles and loads its bearings — the reason rotors are dynamically balanced so that their spin axis coincides with a principal axis. Spinning is stable about the axes of largest and smallest moment and unstable about the middle one (try it with a book or a phone tossed in the air).

4Quadratic forms and positive definiteness

Energy, variance and cost functions are often quadratic: E(x) = ½ xᵀ K x. In eigen-coordinates y = Qᵀx it becomes a plain sum of squares:

xᵀ A x = λ₁y₁² + λ₂y₂² + … + λₙyₙ²
  • All λ > 0 → positive definite: the energy is positive for any deformation. A properly supported structure's stiffness matrix is like this.
  • A zero λ → a mechanism or rigid-body motion (an unsupported structure, a free shaft): deformation without energy. FE solvers report "singular stiffness matrix".
  • Mixed signs → a saddle: the shape of an unstable equilibrium, or a minimum in some directions and a maximum in others.
Example 5 — the ellipse 5x² + 4xy + 2y² = 1A = [5 2; 2 2] (the 4xy is split as 2 + 2) λ = 6 along (2, 1)/√5, λ = 1 along (1, −2)/√5 In eigen-coordinates: 6u² + 1·w² = 1 → semi-axes 1/√6 = 0.408 and 1/√1 = 1 The long axis lies along the SMALL eigenvalue's eigenvector.

The same picture explains why gradient descent is slow on "narrow valleys": the contours of a quadratic cost are ellipses whose axis ratio is √(λmax/λmin), the square root of the condition number.
