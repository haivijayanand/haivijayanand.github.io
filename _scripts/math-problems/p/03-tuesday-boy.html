1. The problem

A stranger tells you: "I have two children. At least one is a boy born on a Tuesday." What is the probability that both children are boys?

The Tuesday sounds irrelevant. Surely the answer is the same as without it? Assume boys and girls are equally likely, the seven weekdays are equally likely, and the two children are independent.

2. The answer — three versions

"At least one is a boy"
1/3
"At least one is a boy born on a Tuesday"
13/27
≈ 48.1 %
"The elder child is a boy"
1/2

The more specific the description of the boy, the closer it is to pointing at one particular child — and the closer the answer moves from 1/3 towards 1/2.

3. Counting the cases

Each child is one of 14 equally likely types (boy or girl × 7 days), so a two-child family is one of 14 × 14 = 196 equally likely pairs. Count the pairs that contain a Tuesday boy:

  • Elder is a Tuesday boy: 14 pairs (younger anything).
  • Younger is a Tuesday boy: 14 pairs.
  • Both are Tuesday boys: counted twice, so subtract 1. Total 27 pairs.

Pairs with two boys among these: elder Tuesday boy with any boy (7) + younger Tuesday boy with any boy (7) − 1 double count = 13. So P = 13/27.

The general formula

If the boy has a trait with probability p (born on a Tuesday: p = 1/7; born on 1 January: p = 1/365):

P(two boys | at least one boy with the trait) = (2 − p) / (4 − p)

p = 1 gives the plain "at least one boy" answer 1/3; p → 0 gives 1/2.

6. Fine print

  • The answer depends on how you learned the fact. If the parent picked one child at random and told you its sex and weekday, the answer is 1/2 whatever the day. 13/27 assumes the statement is a filter: "families having at least one Tuesday boy".
  • The puzzle was made famous by Gary Foshee at a 2010 puzzle-lovers' gathering; it sparked long arguments for exactly this reason.
