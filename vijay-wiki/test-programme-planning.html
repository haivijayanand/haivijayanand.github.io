Planning Long-Duration Test Programmes: Test Hours, Shifts, Availability and Reliability Demonstration

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
TestingPlanningReliabilityEndurance

A specification may call for 1,000 hours of endurance running, but the calendar time depends on shifts, how many of the running hours are actually logged, unplanned stoppages and planned inspections. Honest test-hour accounting gives schedules that hold. Reliability statistics then say what a given amount of failure-free running actually demonstrates.

Key takeaways

  • Separate calendar hours, available hours, running hours and logged (valid) test hours; only valid hours count towards the requirement.
  • Logging efficiency = logged ÷ running hours; availability = running ÷ planned hours. Automation of start-up, checks and logging raises both.
  • Calendar days ≈ required hours ÷ (valid hours per day × availability) + planned inspection days.
  • Moving from one to two shifts roughly halves calendar time; the third shift adds less if night running is limited or poorly logged.
  • Zero-failure demonstration: T = −ln(1 − C)·θ. Showing 500 h MTBF at 90 % confidence needs about 1,150 h without failure.

3. Reliability demonstration

Zero failures, exponential life: T = −ln(1 − C) · θ With r failures: θ_lower = 2T / χ²(C; 2r + 2)
Confidence CTest time as multiple of target MTBF (zero failures)
60 %0.92
80 %1.61
90 %2.30
95 %3.00

Example: demonstrating θ = 500 h at 90 % confidence needs 1,151 h of failure-free testing (shared across units if several are tested).

4. Practical tips

  • Write the test-hour definition (what counts as valid) into the test plan before starting.
  • Automate start-up sequences, condition checks and logging; review data daily.
  • Plan inspections at fixed test-hour intervals and keep spares for the parts most likely to need replacement.
  • Track a daily chart of planned versus achieved valid hours; react when the gap grows.
