Level 1 · For school students

A price is like a message. It tells sellers what to grow and buyers how much to buy.

1What is a market?

A market is any place, real or online, where buyers and sellers meet to exchange things. The price is decided by both together.

Think of the vegetable mandi near your home: farmers and traders bring onions, tomatoes and potatoes; families come to buy. Nobody "sets" the price alone. If there are too many onions, sellers lower the price to sell them; if onions run short, buyers compete and the price climbs.

2Demand and supply in simple words

📉 Demand: what buyers wantWhen onions are cheap, families buy more (extra onion pakodas!). When they are costly, families buy fewer and use them carefully. Lower price → more bought.
📈 Supply: what sellers offerWhen onion prices are high, farmers and traders bring more to market (some take onions out of storage). When prices are low, they bring less. Higher price → more offered.
🤝 Equilibrium: where they meetAt one special price, the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers bring. That is the market price.

4Why do some prices jump so much?

  • Onions and tomatoes spoil quickly and depend on the weather. One bad rain can cut supply, and people still need them for cooking, so prices jump a lot.
  • Salt is cheap to store and always available, and nobody eats more salt just because it is cheaper, so its price barely moves.
  • Festivals raise demand for sweets, clothes and flowers, so their prices rise around Diwali and Pongal.
  • Cheaper mobile data made Indians use far more of it: lower price, more demand.

5When the government steps in

🌾 MSP (Minimum Support Price)The government promises to buy crops like wheat and rice at a fixed minimum price, so farmers are not ruined if market prices crash. For 2027-28, wheat's MSP is ₹2,610 per quintal (100 kg).
🏪 Ration shopsFamilies with ration cards get rice and wheat free or very cheap from fair-price shops.
🧅 Buffer stocksThe government stores onions and pulses when they are cheap and sells them when prices shoot up, as it did in 2026 at ₹35/kg.
🚫 Export limitsWhen prices at home rise too high, the government may stop or tax exports so more stays in India, but this hurts farmers who earn from selling abroad.

Level 2 · For working people

Reading price changes in your daily life and work: shifts versus movements, elasticity, the 2026 onion story, MSP, price controls, and competition.

1Shifts vs movements

  • A movement along a curve is caused by the item's own price: onions get cheaper, so you buy more.
  • A shift of demand comes from income, tastes, prices of related goods and expectations. Higher salaries raise demand for cars; costlier petrol raises demand for EVs (substitutes) and cuts demand for big cars (complements of fuel).
  • A shift of supply comes from weather, input costs (diesel, fertiliser, wages), technology, taxes and the number of sellers. A drought shifts supply left; a new cold-storage chain shifts it right.
  • Expectations matter: if traders expect higher onion prices next month, they hold stock today, which tightens supply now. That is why stock limits exist.

2Elasticity: how strongly people react

Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

Calculator: the farmer's paradox (a bumper crop can mean a lower income)

Because food demand is inelastic, a 20% bigger onion crop can push prices down by far more than 20%, cutting farmers' total income. This is why onion farmers in Nashik sometimes dump crops in good years, and why storage and exports matter.

3The 2026 onion story

  1. Summer: the rabi onion crop was stored. The government bought about 1.2 lakh tonnes for its buffer, after raising its procurement price five times to attract sellers.
  2. Monsoon: heavy, uneven rain damaged crops and spoiled stored onions just as stocks were running down. Supply shifted sharply left.
  3. August–October: retail prices crossed ₹60/kg. The government released buffer stocks, sold onions at ₹35/kg in cities and ran "Kanda Express" trains, but wholesale prices at Lasalgaon kept rising for a while.
  4. The CPI effect: onion inflation of 116% year-on-year in October helped push food inflation to 8.94% and headline CPI above the RBI's 6% limit (Topic 01).
  5. Late October onwards: fresh kharif arrivals began to ease prices. Supply shifted right again.

4MSP: how it works, and the debate

  • The CACP recommends MSPs for 22 mandated crops (plus a fair price for sugarcane). Since 2018-19, MSP is set at least 1.5 times the all-India weighted average cost of production.
  • 2027-28 rabi MSPs: wheat ₹2,610 (+₹25), barley ₹2,286 (+₹136), gram ₹5,958 (+₹83), lentil +₹390, mustard +₹413, safflower +₹675. Larger increases for pulses and oilseeds aim to reduce India's import dependence.
  • MSP only matters where there is procurement: wheat and rice are bought on a large scale (mainly in Punjab, Haryana and Madhya Pradesh). Most other crops are sold below MSP in many markets.
  • The debate: supporters say MSP is income security and protection against price crashes. Critics point to overflowing FCI godowns, a bias towards water-intensive rice and wheat, and a fiscal cost. Proposals include a legal guarantee for MSP, price-deficiency payments, and income support such as PM-KISAN.

5Price controls: ceilings and floors

Price ceiling (maximum price)Set below the market price, it creates a shortage: queues, black markets and lower quality. Examples: rent control (old Mumbai tenancies), drug price caps (NPPA), past caps on LPG and kerosene.
Price floor (minimum price)Set above the market price, it creates a surplus that someone must buy or store. Examples: MSP with procurement (FCI stocks), minimum wages.
Better tools?Targeted cash transfers, buffer stocks, better storage and transport, and open trade usually stabilise prices with fewer side effects than fixed price caps.

6When a few sellers set prices

  • Perfect competition (many small sellers, like a vegetable mandi) has prices close to cost.
  • Monopoly or oligopoly (few sellers: telecom, cement, airlines on some routes) lets firms charge above cost. Tariff hikes by a few telecom companies within weeks of each other are a classic oligopoly pattern.
  • The Competition Commission of India (CCI) fights cartels and abuse of dominance. It has fined cement, tyre and tech companies.
  • Dynamic pricing (cab surge fares, flight and hotel prices, quick-commerce delivery fees) adjusts prices minute by minute to demand. It is efficient in principle but raises fairness and transparency concerns, and is now subject to government guidelines for ride-hailing.

7The global picture

  • Global food prices are set by supply and demand across countries. India's own export bans (wheat in 2022, rice and onions in 2023–24) moved world prices, a reminder of India's weight in the market.
  • Oil: the 2026 West Asia conflict was a textbook supply shock: a small cut in supply against inelastic demand meant a big price jump (Topics 01 and 05).
  • Cobweb cycles in onions, pork and coffee are global: high prices one year lead to over-planting, then a glut and low prices the next.

Level 3 · Review for experts

Equilibrium and welfare, elasticity measures, controls and deadweight loss, agricultural price dynamics, market power, information problems and Indian price policy.

1Equilibrium and welfare

Linear model: Q_d = a − b·P Q_s = c + d·P → P* = (a − c)/(b + d), Q* = a − b·P* Consumer surplus = area under demand above price; producer surplus = area above supply below price
  • The first welfare theorem: competitive equilibrium is Pareto-efficient, given no externalities, complete information and no market power. Most of applied microeconomics studies where these conditions fail.
  • Marshallian surplus is an approximation to compensating or equivalent variation. It is accurate when income effects are small, which is less true for staples that take a large budget share among the poor.

Calculator: equilibrium, a ceiling or floor, and deadweight loss

2Elasticities

Own-price ε = (∂Q/∂P)(P/Q) Income η = (∂Q/∂Y)(Y/Q) Cross ε_xy = (∂Q_x/∂P_y)(P_y/Q_x) Revenue: dR/dP = Q(1 + ε) → revenue rises with price iff |ε| < 1
  • Engel's law: the food share falls as income rises (η < 1 for food). India's food weight in CPI has fallen from about 46% in the 2012 series to nearly 40% in the 2024 series (Topic 01).
  • Short-run vs long-run: demand and supply are more elastic over time (Le Chatelier). Fuel demand barely responds in a month but shifts with vehicle choices over years.
  • Estimation: naive OLS of quantity on price mixes supply and demand. You need instruments that shift one curve only, such as rainfall for agricultural supply.

3Agricultural price dynamics

  • The cobweb model (Kaldor, Ezekiel 1938): planting decisions respond to last season's price, Q_s,t = c + d·P_{t−1}. Prices converge if d/b < 1 and diverge otherwise. Onion acreage cycles in India fit this pattern partially.
  • Storage arbitrage (Deaton–Laroque, 1992): competitive storage truncates price falls, making price distributions skewed with occasional spikes when stocks run out. This explains why the late-monsoon onion squeeze recurs before the kharif crop arrives.
  • Perishability and thin markets: high post-harvest losses, limited cold storage and fragmented APMC mandis widen the farm-to-retail price spread. Farmers often get only a third or so of the consumer rupee for perishables, according to RBI studies.
  • Rational expectations (Muth, 1961) were originally formulated to critique cobweb models: if farmers anticipate cycles, the cycles dampen.

4Market power

Monopoly markup (Lerner): (P − MC)/P = 1/|ε| Cournot with n firms: (P − MC)/P = HHI / |ε| (HHI as a share, 0–1)
  • Monopoly deadweight loss is the Harberger triangle (Topic 06); rent-seeking costs (Tullock, Posner) may exceed it.
  • Oligopoly models: Cournot (quantities), Bertrand (prices, which gives the competitive result with homogeneous goods), and tacit collusion in repeated games. These are used in CCI cartel cases (cement, tyres) and in merger review.
  • Platforms: two-sided markets, network effects and algorithmic pricing raise new questions about collusion and self-preferencing. The Digital Competition Bill debate concerns exactly these.

5Information, frictions and pricing in practice

  • Akerlof's lemons (1970): asymmetric information can unravel markets such as used cars, insurance (a later topic) and credit. Warranties, certification and reputation are the remedies.
  • Search costs sustain price dispersion even for identical goods (Stigler, Diamond paradox). Digital price comparison lowers dispersion but enables personalised pricing.
  • Sticky prices: menu costs and fairness norms (Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler) explain why sellers hesitate to raise prices in shortages, rationing through queues or "out of stock" instead.
  • Dynamic pricing improves allocation when supply is fixed (seats, cabs) but faces fairness backlash. Regulators now cap surge multiples for ride-hailing.

6Indian price policy

  • MSP and procurement: open-ended procurement of wheat and rice creates a floor. Its effects include stock accumulation above buffer norms, fiscal cost through the food subsidy (Topic 08), crop-mix distortion towards water-intensive crops in north-west India, and price-depressing stock sales (OMSS).
  • Price Stabilisation Fund: onion and pulse buffers aim to smooth prices but suffer from procurement timing and losses in storage. In 2026, the Central Warehousing Corporation was brought in as the storage agency.
  • Trade tools: export bans, minimum export prices and duties stabilise domestic prices at the cost of farmers' incomes and India's reliability as a trading partner. Import-duty cuts on edible oils and pulses are the mirror image.
  • Essential Commodities Act stock limits target hoarding but can deter the private storage that smooths prices over time.

7Review checklist: open questions

  1. Should MSP become a legal entitlement, a price-deficiency payment, or be replaced by income support?
  2. How much of food-price volatility is due to supply shocks, and how much to policy-induced uncertainty (sudden bans)?
  3. Can private storage and futures markets for onions (currently restricted) do better than public buffers?
  4. What is the right competition-policy response to algorithmic and dynamic pricing?
  5. With climate change making weather shocks more frequent, how should India's food-price stabilisation framework adapt?
  6. How should the RBI treat food-price spikes: look through them, or respond to protect expectations (Topic 01)?
