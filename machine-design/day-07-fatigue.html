Fatigue: S–N Curve and Endurance Limit

Most machine parts that break in service break by fatigue — a crack that grows a little with every load cycle until the part snaps at a stress well below its strength. Learn the S–N curve, the endurance limit and the Marin factors that turn a laboratory number into one you can design with.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: Marin factors and S–N curve❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Describe the three stages of a fatigue failure and read a fatigue fracture surface
  • Explain the S–N curve and the endurance limit
  • Estimate Se′ from Sut and correct it with the Marin factors ka … ke
  • Estimate the life of a part loaded above the endurance limit
  • Know the materials and treatments that improve fatigue strength

2How fatigue works

In 1842 the Versailles train disaster and, later, failed railway axles led August Wöhler to test axles under rotating bending. He found that steel survives a stress forever if the stress is low enough, but breaks after a number of cycles if it is higher — even far below the yield strength.

1 · InitiationA microscopic crack starts at a stress raiser: a fillet, a machining mark, an inclusion, corrosion pit.
2 · PropagationThe crack grows a little each cycle, leaving beach marks (clam-shell lines) on the fracture face.
3 · Final fractureThe remaining section is too small and breaks suddenly — a rough, fibrous zone. Its size tells you the load: small = low nominal stress.

Fatigue gives no visible warning: no necking, no bending. That is why it is responsible for the majority of mechanical failures in service — shafts, axles, springs, bolts, gear teeth, welded frames.

3The S–N curve and endurance limit

Plot fully reversed stress amplitude S against cycles to failure N on log–log axes. For steels the curve bends to a horizontal line at about 10⁶–10⁷ cycles: below that stress the part lasts indefinitely. That stress is the endurance limit. Aluminium and copper alloys have no knee — they always fail eventually, so their "fatigue strength" is quoted at a stated life (often 5 × 10⁸ cycles).

Rotating-beam endurance limit (steel): Se′ = 0.5 Sut for Sut ≤ 1400 MPa; Se′ = 700 MPa above
High-cycle line: Sf = a·Nb, a = (f·Sut)² / Se, b = −⅓·log(f·Sut / Se) (f ≈ 0.9 at 10³ cycles)
Life: N = (σa / a)1/b

4Marin factors: from test bar to real part

Se′ comes from a small, mirror-polished specimen in a laboratory. Your part is bigger, rougher, may be loaded axially, and must be reliable. Marin's equation corrects it:

Se = ka · kb · kc · kd · ke · Se′

The stress concentration (Kf from Day 6) is normally applied to the stress, not to Se — keep it in one place only.

5Materials and treatments for fatigue

  • Higher Sut raises Se′ — but the surface factor ka falls, so a rough high-strength part gains little. Polish or grind high-strength steel.
  • Compressive residual stress at the surface is the best friend of fatigue: shot peening, rolled fillets and threads, nitriding and carburising all add it. Gains of 20–50 % are common.
  • Tensile residual stress is the enemy: grinding burns, welding, some platings (chrome, nickel) and decarburised surfaces after heat treatment.
  • Clean steels (vacuum-degassed, low inclusions) for bearings and springs.
  • Corrosion destroys the endurance limit — there is no knee in corrosion fatigue. Protect the surface.

6Cost of fatigue strength

Grinding a fillet, shot peening a spring or rolling a thread costs little compared with a larger part — and much less than a field failure. In design reviews, look first at the cheap fixes: bigger radius (Day 6), better finish, peening; then at a better steel; last at a bigger section.

7Worked example

Machined steel bar, Sut = 600 MPa, Ø40, rotating bendingSe′ = 0.5 × 600 = 300 MPa ka = 4.51 × 600^−0.265 = 0.828 kb = 1.24 × 40^−0.107 = 0.836 Se = 300 × 0.828 × 0.836 = 208 MPa (35 % of Sut, not 50 %) f ≈ 0.88 → f·Sut = 530 MPa; a = 530²/208 = 1351 MPa; b = −⅓ log(530/208) = −0.1356 At σa = 250 MPa: N = (250/1351)^(1/−0.1356) ≈ 2.5 × 10⁵ cycles At 1440 rpm that is about 3 hours of running.

8Procedure

  1. Find Sut of the material in its heat-treated condition.
  2. Se′ = 0.5 Sut (or 700 MPa if Sut > 1400).
  3. Apply ka (finish), kb (size), kc (load type), kd (temperature) and ke (reliability) to get Se.
  4. Calculate the alternating stress σa at the critical point, multiplied by Kf.
  5. If σa ≤ Se/n: infinite life. If not, find N from the S–N line and compare with the required life.
  6. If mean stress is present too, go to Day 8.

9Lab: Marin factors and the S–N curve

Change the strength, finish, size, load type and reliability, and watch the knee of the S–N curve move. Move σa above and below Se to see the life.

10Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Never use the textbook Se′ without Marin factors — the corrected value is often 30–40 % of Sut.
  • A rotating shaft with a steady bending load sees fully reversed bending stress every revolution: 1440 rpm is 2 million cycles a day.
  • Count the cycles honestly: a gear tooth is loaded once per revolution, a bearing race many times, a vibration-excited bracket at its natural frequency.
  • Read failed parts. Beach marks point back to the origin; the origin shows you what to fix.

11Practice

  1. Find Se for a hot-rolled 40Cr4 bar (Sut 900), Ø60, axial load, 99 % reliability.
  2. Repeat for the same bar ground. How much does finish change Se?
  3. A machined C45 shaft (Sut 620) runs at 960 rpm with σa = 280 MPa. How many hours will it last?
