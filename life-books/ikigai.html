Ikigai

Héctor García and Francesc Miralles · first published 2016 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

A long, happy life grows from having a reason to get up each morning, kept alive by gentle daily activity, close friendships, moderate eating and work you lose yourself in.

Summary

Ikigai is a Japanese word that roughly means a reason for being, the thing that makes the morning worth waking up for. The authors set out to learn why some people live long and contented lives, and they travelled to Ogimi, a village in northern Okinawa known for its many healthy elders, to listen to how those people spend their days.

What they found was not a secret formula but a set of ordinary habits. The elders keep busy and do not really retire; they tend gardens, help neighbours and keep small duties that give each day shape. They move often but gently, through walking, farm work and simple morning exercises, rather than through punishing workouts. They eat a varied diet rich in vegetables and stop before they feel completely full, a habit the Okinawans call hara hachi bu.

Friendship matters just as much. Many villagers belong to a moai, a close group that meets regularly, shares news and supports members through hard times. Feeling needed and connected appears to protect both mood and health.

The book also draws on Western ideas. It describes flow, the deep absorption we feel when a task is challenging enough to hold our attention, and suggests building more of it into work and hobbies by removing distractions and doing one thing at a time. It borrows from logotherapy, which holds that finding meaning helps people endure suffering, and from Morita therapy, which encourages accepting feelings while still taking useful action.

Finally, the authors talk about resilience: staying calm when plans fall apart, enjoying the present moment and accepting that nothing is perfect or permanent. Their closing advice is simple: stay active, slow down, eat lightly, keep good friends, smile, spend time in nature, give thanks, live in the present and follow your ikigai.

299 words

One-line takeaway: This week, pick one evening to walk, cook or garden together as a family without screens, and at dinner ask everyone to share one small thing they are looking forward to tomorrow.

  2. Which Okinawan village did the authors visit to study long-lived people?

    Answer B. They interviewed elders in Ogimi, a village in northern Okinawa known for longevity.

  3. What is hara hachi bu?

    Answer C. It is the Okinawan habit of finishing a meal before feeling completely full.

  4. What is a moai in Okinawan life?

    Answer D. A moai is a long-standing circle of friends who meet and help each other.

  5. How do the elders of Ogimi tend to approach retirement?

    Answer A. The book notes they stay active and never fully retire from meaningful tasks.

  6. What kind of exercise do the authors find most common among the long-lived elders?

    Answer B. The elders move often but gently, through walking, farming and simple routines.

  7. According to the book, what helps us enter a state of flow?

    Answer D. Flow comes from absorbing, suitably challenging work done with full attention.

  8. What central idea does the book take from logotherapy?

    Answer C. Logotherapy holds that a sense of meaning gives people strength through suffering.

  9. What does Morita therapy, as described in the book, encourage?

    Answer A. Morita therapy teaches people to accept emotions and get on with meaningful action.

  10. Which of these is among the book's closing rules for living with ikigai?

    Answer D. The closing advice includes living in the moment and showing gratitude.
