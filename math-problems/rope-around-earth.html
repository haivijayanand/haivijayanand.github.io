A Rope Around the Earth
Home · Interesting Math Problems · Problem 12 · Geometry & measurement

1. The problem

A rope is wrapped tightly around the Earth's equator, about 40,000 km long. You add just 1 metre to its length.

  • Part A. The longer rope is lifted evenly all round the Earth. How high above the ground is it? Could an ant crawl under it? A cat?
  • Part B. Instead, the rope is pulled up at one point like a tent pole, staying on the ground elsewhere. How high is that point?

2. The answer

Part A — lifted evenly
15.9 cm
A cat can walk under it. And the answer is the same for a football, the Earth or the Sun.
Part B — pulled up at one point
≈ 121.5 m
Taller than a 35-storey building, from one extra metre of rope.

3. Working it out

Part A

Circumference C = 2πR. Adding ΔL to the rope makes the new radius R + h with 2π(R + h) = 2πR + ΔL, so

h = ΔL / 2π = 1 m / 6.283 = 0.159 m

The radius R cancels out completely. Every extra metre of belt adds 16 cm of gap, whatever it is wrapped around.

Part B

The rope leaves the ground at two tangent points, each an angle θ from the peak (measured at the Earth's centre). Each straight piece has length R tan θ and replaces an arc of length Rθ, so the extra length is

ΔL = 2R(tan θ − θ) ≈ 2R θ³/3      height h = R(1/cos θ − 1) ≈ R θ²/2

With R = 6,371 km and ΔL = 1 m: θ³ ≈ 3/(2 × 6,371,000), θ ≈ 0.00617 rad, so h ≈ 6,371,000 × 0.00617²/2 ≈ 121.5 m. Because h grows like R1/3, a bigger planet gives a taller tent.

6. Fine print

  • Part A is an old puzzle (William Whiston, 1702). It surprises because we compare 1 m with 40,000 km, when the gap really depends only on the 1 m.
  • Part B is the same geometry as sag in a long rail or pipeline: a tiny thermal expansion of a long, restrained length makes a large buckle — the reason for expansion joints.
