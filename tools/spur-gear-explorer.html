Spur Gear Fundamentals

The simplest and most common cylindrical gear — straight teeth cut parallel to the rotation axis, transmitting power between parallel shafts.

Spur gears are the baseline of mechanical power transmission. Their teeth are cut straight across the face, generating only radial (tangential + radial) loads — no axial thrust, which simplifies shaft and bearing design. The involute tooth profile, universal since the 18th century, ensures constant velocity ratio under load and is tolerant of small centre-distance errors.

Operating characteristics are dominated by two competing stress regimes: bending stress at the tooth root (Lewis/AGMA) and Hertzian contact stress at the flank (surface fatigue). Gear design is the art of balancing these with material selection, tooth geometry, and lubrication.

⚡

High Efficiency

98–99.5% efficiency per mesh under full load with adequate lubrication. Best of any gear type.

↔️

No Axial Thrust

Straight teeth produce only radial load on bearings. Axial bearings not required — unlike helical.

🏭

Easy to Manufacture

Hobbing, shaping, and grinding are well-established. Tight tolerances achievable at moderate cost.

🔊

Higher Noise

Sudden full-width engagement creates impact loading at each tooth-in. Noisier than helical at high speed.

📏

Parallel Shafts Only

Transmits between coplanar parallel axes only. Bevel or worm gears needed for other orientations.

🛡️

Proven Reliability

AGMA and ISO standards mature. Extensive field data for life prediction and rating calculations.

AGMA vs ISO: The two dominant international standards for gear design rating — AGMA 2101 (US) and ISO 6336 (global) — yield comparable but not identical results. ISO 6336 is the default in European and defence aerospace applications.

Gear Arrangement Types

Spur gears appear in several arrangement families, each with distinct kinematic properties and application niches.

External Mesh

External Spur Gear Pair

Both gears have outward-facing teeth. Rotation reverses direction. Most common arrangement. Efficiency 98.5–99.5%.

Internal Mesh

Internal (Ring) Gear

Pinion meshes inside an annular ring gear. Same rotation direction. Higher contact ratio, more compact radial envelope.

Rack & Pinion

Rack and Pinion

Rack is a gear of infinite radius. Converts rotation to linear translation. Used in steering systems, actuators, CNC axes.

Planetary

Epicyclic / Planetary

Sun, planet and ring gears with a carrier. High ratio in compact envelope. Used in gearboxes, hub drives and aircraft accessory gearboxes.

Gear Train

Simple Gear Train

Series of external meshes. Each stage inverts rotation. Overall ratio = product of individual ratios. Used for large ratio spreads.

Compound Train

Compound Gear Train

Two gears on one shaft multiply ratio per stage. Aircraft accessory gearboxes work this way — hydraulic, fuel and electrical drives taken off one input shaft cluster.

Tooth Profile Families

Same module — different flank geometry. The contour family determines the curvature at the contact point, directly governing contact (Hertzian) and bending stress.

Stress Analysis

Two failure modes dominate: bending fatigue at the root (Lewis/AGMA) and surface contact fatigue at the flank (Hertz/AGMA). Both must be satisfied.

1 · Tangential Load

W_t = (2 · T) / d₁ ← Tangential force at pitch circle (N) T = transmitted torque (N·mm) d₁ = pitch circle diameter of pinion (mm)

2 · Bending Stress (AGMA 2101)

σ_b = W_t / (b · m) · K_A · K_v · K_s · K_H / Y_J W_t = tangential load (N) b = face width (mm) m = module (mm) Y_J = geometry (Lewis form) factor ← profile and z dependent K_A = application / overload factor (1.0–2.5) K_v = dynamic factor (1.0–2.0, speed dependent) K_s = size factor (1.0–1.4) K_H = load distribution factor (1.0–2.0, misalignment dependent)
Y_J is profile-family-dependent. For an involute gear at 20° with z=20 teeth: Y_J ≈ 0.32. At 25°: Y_J ≈ 0.38. Novikov gears use a different form factor approach because bending is the dominant failure mode.

3 · Contact (Hertzian) Stress (AGMA 2101)

σ_H = Z_E · √(W_t · K_A · K_v · K_s · K_H / (b · d₁ · Z_I)) Z_E = elastic coefficient = 191 MPa^0.5 (steel/steel) Z_I = pitting resistance geometry factor d₁ = pinion pitch diameter (mm)

Failure Modes

Gear failures are classified by location (flank vs root) and mechanism (fatigue, wear, plastic deformation). Correct diagnosis drives the right remedy.

Surface Fatigue

Pitting

Sub-surface fatigue cracks propagate under cyclic Hertzian stress, forming craters on the flank. Initial pitting may arrest; destructive pitting progresses. Root cause: σ_H exceeds S_c life limit. Remedy: increase module, reduce load, improve lubrication.

Surface Fatigue

Spalling

Large-scale pitting with deep cratering below the case-core interface. Common in carburised gears with insufficient case depth. Remedy: increase case depth, review thermal treatment, check stress.

Bending Fatigue

Tooth Root Fracture

Fatigue crack initiating at the root fillet, propagating across the tooth section. Catastrophic if tooth separates. Root cause: σ_b > S_t life limit, stress concentration at fillet. Remedy: trochoidal fillet, surface finishing, reduce overloads.

Adhesive Wear

Scuffing / Scoring

Instantaneous welding and tearing of flank asperities under boundary lubrication — high sliding velocity at addendum/dedendum. Leaves directional scratch marks. Root cause: oil film collapse (flash temperature). Remedy: EP lubricant, reduce tip relief, lower speed.

Abrasive Wear

Abrasion

Progressive removal of flank material by hard particles in the lubricant. Increases backlash and profile error. Root cause: contaminated oil or inadequate filtration. Remedy: fine filtration (≤10 μm), oil flush, sealed housing.

Plastic Flow

Rippling / Ridging

Cold flow of flank surface under high contact stress + sliding. Surface texture develops transverse ridges. Root cause: σ_H exceeds yield in surface layer. Remedy: higher surface hardness, reduce load, improve profile geometry.

Misalignment

Edge Loading

Load concentration at one face edge due to shaft angular misalignment or gear crowning errors. Accelerates pitting and root fracture at edge. Remedy: reduce K_Hβ by alignment, add crowning, verify bearing preload.

Overload

Sudden Fracture

Single-cycle fracture from gross overload exceeding ultimate tooth strength. Failure surface shows shear lip and no beach marks. Root cause: shock load, seizure, or jam. Remedy: shock couplings, torque limiters, overload clutches.

Inspection & Maintenance

Condition-based maintenance strategy for spur gear trains — inspection intervals, parameters, acceptance criteria, and corrective actions.

Scheduled Maintenance Programme

Every 8 h / per shift
Operational Monitoring

Log noise level, temperature, vibration (1× and GMF amplitude), and oil pressure. Any step change ≥ 3 dB or 20% vibration rise triggers alert.

Every 250 h
Oil Analysis

Ferrographic / spectrometric oil analysis for Fe, Cr, Ni, Cu wear metals. Compare to baseline. Particle count ISO 4406. Replace oil if viscosity out of spec ±10%.

Every 500 h
Visual Inspection (borescope or access cover)

Inspect tooth flanks for pitting initiation, scoring marks, and edge wear. Check for fretting on gear bore / shaft interface. Record and photograph any findings for trend analysis.

Every 1000 h
Dimensional Check & Backlash Measurement

Measure backlash with dial gauge (max allowable typically 0.05–0.15 mm for m=4). Check tooth profile error and pitch error with gear measuring machine or DTI method.

Every 2500 h
Full Strip Inspection

Remove gears, inspect with dye penetrant (PT) for root cracks. Measure tooth thickness with micrometer or gear tooth vernier. Check bearing condition. Verify shaft alignment with dial gauge and blue bluing contact test.

On Condition / Life Limit
Gear Replacement

Replace at mandatory life limit (AGMA L₁₀ / ISO B₁₀ rated life), or on condition: pitting coverage > 10% tooth area, root crack detected (zero tolerance), backlash > 1.5× new tolerance, or vibration exceeds alarm level after investigation.
