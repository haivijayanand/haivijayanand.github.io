Summary

Inception imagines a technology that lets people share a dream. Dom Cobb (Leonardo DiCaprio) is the best at using it to steal secrets from sleeping minds. A powerful businessman, Saito, offers him a way back to the children he cannot legally return home to — if Cobb can do the opposite of stealing: plant an idea so deeply in a rival heir’s mind that the man believes it is his own.

To do it, Cobb’s team must build a dream inside a dream inside a dream, where time runs slower at every level. But Cobb carries something into every dream: a projection of his dead wife Mal, who keeps breaking in to sabotage the job.

Nolan carried the idea for nearly a decade, first as a horror story about “dream stealers”, before reshaping it into a heist film with a broken family at its centre.

The puzzle box is the hook, but the film’s real subject is a man who must forgive himself before he can go home.

Plot

Spoilers ahead. Acts 4 and 5 are blurred —

The offer

Cobb and his partner Arthur are extractors — thieves who enter targets’ dreams. Saito, impressed by Cobb’s skill with layered dreams, hires him for “inception”: to plant in Robert Fischer’s mind the idea of breaking up his dying father Maurice’s business empire. In return, Saito will clear Cobb’s criminal record so he can go home to his children.

The team

Cobb recruits Eames, a forger who can impersonate people inside dreams; Yusuf, a chemist; and Ariadne, an architecture student who designs the dream mazes. The plan: three dream levels, each running slower than the one above, and a “kick” timed to wake everyone together.

Things go wrong

Fischer’s mind has been trained to defend itself, and armed projections wound Saito. Yusuf’s heavy sedative means dying in the dream will not wake them — it will drop them into Limbo, the raw subconscious. And Mal keeps appearing to wreck the mission.

Cobb’s confession

Cobb admits that he and Mal once lived for years in Limbo, and that he planted in her the idea that her world was not real. Back awake, she could not let go of that idea and took her own life — the guilt he now projects into every dream. On each level the team improvises kicks: Yusuf’s van falls from a bridge, Arthur rigs an elevator in a weightless hotel, Eames blows the snow fortress.

The top

Mal kills Fischer; Cobb and Ariadne follow him into Limbo, where Ariadne shoots Mal’s projection and Cobb finally lets her go. Fischer reaches his father’s bedside and finds the planted idea — his father telling him to build something of his own. Cobb finds an aged Saito in Limbo, and they wake on the plane. At home in Los Angeles, Cobb spins his top, which topples only in reality — but walks off to his children before it falls. The film cuts to black with it still spinning.

The dream levels

The film is built like a set of nested boxes. Each deeper level runs slower than the one above it, so a few minutes of a falling van above become a long fight below.

Reality
A long-haul flight to Los Angeles. The team sedate Fischer and go under together.
Level 1
A rainy city. Yusuf’s dream. Fischer’s armed projections attack; the van’s fall from the bridge is the kick.
Level 2
A hotel. Arthur’s dream. As the van tumbles above, gravity disappears here — the famous rotating-hallway fight.
Level 3
A snow fortress. Eames’s dream. A ski assault on the vault where Fischer meets his father.
Limbo
The raw subconscious — a crumbling city Cobb and Mal built over years. Time almost stops; people can lose themselves for a lifetime.

Music

Hans Zimmer wrote the score while the film was being shot, aiming for something electronic and dense that carried Cobb’s nostalgia and sadness. Johnny Marr, guitarist of The Smiths, added guitar lines in a style that recalls Ennio Morricone’s westerns.

The cleverest musical idea is Édith Piaf’s “Non, je ne regrette rien” (“No, I regret nothing”). Inside the story it is the warning signal played to dreamers before a kick; Zimmer then worked pieces of the song into the score itself. The title is also a quiet comment on Cobb, a man made entirely of regret.

Technology & craft

Inception looks like a CGI film but is mostly built in the real world. Nolan prefers effects captured in camera, using computer graphics to extend or bend reality rather than replace it. It has about 500 visual-effects shots — fewer than his own Batman Begins.

The rotating hallway

A real 100-foot corridor was hung on eight giant rings and turned a full 360° by two motors, so Joseph Gordon-Levitt could fight across walls and ceiling for real. He trained for weeks to do it.

A tilted hotel

In a converted airship hangar at Cardington, England, the hotel bar set was built tilted at 30 degrees.

A train on a truck

The freight train that ploughs through a city street was a replica locomotive body built on a tractor-trailer chassis, moulded in fibreglass from real train parts.

The van

A Ford Econoline van was fired off the bridge by cannon and filmed in slow motion. For the underwater scenes, actors held their breath for minutes at a time, supported by scuba tanks between takes.

Film formats

Shot mainly on anamorphic 35 mm, with key scenes on 65 mm and aerial shots in VistaVision. No IMAX cameras and no 3D. Slow-motion shots used a Photo-Sonics camera at up to 1,000 frames per second.

Miniatures

Paul Franklin’s team built a miniature of the mountain fortress and blew it up for real. Limbo’s collapsing city was designed like a giant iceberg of buildings with water running through it.

Real Paris

Ariadne’s Paris scenes were shot on location at the Musée Galliera, a street bistro and the Bir-Hakeim bridge, with the dream distortions layered on top.

Six countries

Filming ran from June to November 2009: Tokyo for the helicopter opening, Tangier doubling for Mombasa, London’s UCL for the Paris college, Los Angeles for the car chase and bridge, and Alberta’s Fortress Mountain ski resort for the snow assault.

Influences

Critics trace ideas from Borges, The Matrix, Blade Runner, Solaris and especially the anime Paprika.

An original, non-franchise story with a complicated structure was a gamble for a summer release. It paid off: roughly five times its production budget, and the fourth-highest grosser of 2010.

For comparison with The Matrix: Inception cost about two and a half times the original Matrix and earned nearly twice as much — two films, a decade apart, that each made “what is real?” a mainstream blockbuster question.

Where the emotions come from

A man who can’t go home. Cobb sees his children only in memory, always from behind, faces hidden. That small, repeated image carries more feeling than any explosion.
Mal. Marion Cotillard plays a ghost made of guilt — loving, frightening and hurt all at once. Cobb’s confession about what he did to her turns the heist into a tragedy.
The wonder of building. Ariadne’s first dream, when Paris folds over itself, is pure delight — the film’s reminder that imagination is a gift before it becomes a weapon.
A son and a father. Fischer’s scene at his father’s bedside is invented by thieves, yet Cillian Murphy makes it real. The lie heals him.
Letting go. In Limbo, Cobb finally tells Mal he cannot keep her. It is the true climax — not the mission succeeding, but a husband accepting a loss.
The top. The last shot asks the audience whether Cobb is still dreaming. Nolan has said the point is that Cobb has stopped looking — he no longer cares, because his children are in front of him.

What works

  • An original idea, explained clearly enough for a mass audience without being dumbed down.
  • Four dream levels cross-cut so cleanly you never lose track — a feat of editing.
  • Real sets and stunts that still look better than most CGI made since.
  • Zimmer’s score, which turned the trailer “braam” into a decade-long trend.

What doesn’t

  • A great deal of the dialogue is explanation of rules; A. O. Scott found its picture of the mind too literal.
  • David Denby felt it was too in love with its own machinery.
  • Apart from Cobb and Mal, the team are sketched more as roles than people.

Watch it for: the hallway fight, the final twenty minutes of cross-cut kicks, and the last shot. Watch it with: The Matrix — two different answers to the same question.
