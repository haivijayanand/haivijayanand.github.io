1Why do people buy things?

People buy something when it gives them value: it meets a need (food, clothes, shelter) or a want (a game, a nicer pen) and is worth more to them than its price.

Ravi is thirsty after cricket. A cold bottle of water costs ₹20, but to him, right now, it feels worth ₹50. He happily pays ₹20. The shopkeeper paid ₹12 for the bottle. Everyone wins: Ravi gets ₹30 more value than he paid, and the shop earns ₹8.

5Words to remember

1Value, price and cost: the three numbers

Customer's gain = Value to customer − Price Business's profit = Price − Cost

The value proposition is the promise of why a customer should choose you: cheaper, faster, better quality, more convenient, more trusted, or a better experience. A good proposition is specific: "fresh idli batter delivered by 7 am in Avadi" beats "quality food products".

Functional valueIt does the job: a pump that lifts water, software that files GST.
Economic valueIt saves or earns money: an LED that cuts the power bill.
Emotional valuePride, safety, belonging: a branded watch, a trusted hospital.
ConvenienceTime saved: 10-minute grocery delivery.

2Revenue is not profit; profit is not cash

TermMeaningLemonade example
Revenue (sales, turnover)Price × quantity sold45 × ₹20 = ₹900
Gross profitRevenue − direct cost of goods sold₹900 − ₹400 (lemons, sugar, cups) = ₹500
Operating profitGross profit − running expenses₹500 − ₹100 (stall rent) = ₹400

Cash flowMoney actually received − money actually paidIf the society secretary pays ₹200 next week, cash today is only ₹700

Many profitable small businesses close because they run out of cash, not because they make losses. Chapters 14 and 29 come back to this.

3Markup vs margin — the costly confusion

A trader buys at ₹100 and sells at ₹125. The markup is 25% (on cost). The margin is 20% (on price). If the owner tells staff "we need 25% margin" but they add 25% markup, every sale falls 5 points short.

Markup = (Price − Cost) / Cost Margin = (Price − Cost) / Price Margin = Markup / (1 + Markup) Markup = Margin / (1 − Margin)

4Price, volume and profit

Raise the price and each sale earns more, but fewer people buy. This shop sells snacks; at ₹10 it sells 1,000 packets a week, and every ₹1 rise loses about 60 buyers. Cost is ₹6 per packet plus ₹1,500 a week of fixed costs.

1The value stick

Brandenburger and Stuart (1996) and later Oberholzer-Gee (2021) frame strategy with a single stick: willingness to pay (WTP) at the top, price, cost, and willingness to sell (WTS) of suppliers and employees at the bottom. Total value created = WTP − WTS. Price and cost only divide it.

Value stick

Added value of a player = total value created with the player − total value without. A firm can capture no more than its added value in competitive bargaining: the core insight of biform games. Raising WTP (quality, complements, network effects) or lowering WTS (better jobs, supplier productivity) grows the stick; price moves only shift it between players.

2Accounting profit vs economic profit

Economic profit = NOPAT − (WACC × Invested capital) = (ROIC − WACC) × Invested capital

Accounting profit ignores the cost of equity. A firm earning 9% on capital when investors could earn 13% at similar risk is profitable in its books yet destroys value. Stern Stewart branded this as EVA; with adjustments (capitalising R&D, operating leases) it approximates economic rent. Chapter 46 derives WACC.

3Profit maximisation and elasticity

With linear demand Q = a − bP and constant marginal cost c, profit π = (P − c)(a − bP) − F peaks at P* = (a/b + c)/2, where MR = MC. In elasticity form, the Lerner condition gives

(P − MC)/P = 1/|ε| or P = MC × |ε| / (|ε| − 1)

A firm never prices on the inelastic part of its demand curve (|ε| < 1): raising price there increases revenue and cuts cost. The working-level slider above is this model with a = 1,000 + 10b and b = buyers lost per ₹1. Real pricing adds competitor reaction, reference prices, and segmentation (Chapter 11).
