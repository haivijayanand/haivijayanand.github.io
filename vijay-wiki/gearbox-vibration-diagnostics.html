Gearbox Vibration Diagnostics: Mesh Frequencies, Sidebands, Hunting Tooth and TSA

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
GearsVibrationDiagnosticsTSA

A gearbox produces vibration at the gear mesh frequency even when healthy, because tooth stiffness changes as teeth enter and leave mesh. Faults show up as changes around that frequency: sidebands at shaft speeds when a gear is eccentric or has a damaged tooth, rising harmonics with wear, and impulses once per revolution for a cracked or broken tooth.

Key takeaways

  • Gear mesh frequency GMF = teeth × shaft frequency, the same for both gears in mesh.
  • Sidebands at ± input and ± output shaft frequencies around GMF point to the gear that is modulating the mesh.
  • Hunting tooth frequency fHT = GMF·NA/(z₁z₂), where NA = greatest common divisor of the tooth counts.
  • Tooth counts with no common factor spread wear evenly; ratios like 25:50 make the same teeth meet every revolution.
  • Time-synchronous averaging (TSA) isolates one shaft's gear signal; residual and difference signals plus metrics (FM0, FM4, NA4) flag local damage.

1. Characteristic frequencies

GMF = z1·f1 = z2·f2 Sidebands: GMF ± k·f1 and GMF ± k·f2 Assembly phase factor N_A = gcd(z1, z2) Hunting tooth frequency f_HT = GMF·N_A/(z1·z2) Assembly phase frequency = GMF/N_A

Sidebands at output speedOutput gear problem
Once-per-rev impulse in TSA of one shaftCracked, chipped or broken tooth
Peak at hunting tooth frequency, often lowFaulty tooth pair meeting (manufacturing or damage on both)

3. Time-synchronous averaging

With a once-per-revolution tachometer, the signal is resampled to a fixed number of samples per revolution and averaged over many revolutions. Components not synchronous with that shaft average away. Removing the regular mesh harmonics leaves the residual; removing also the first-order sidebands leaves the difference signal. Kurtosis of the difference signal (FM4) rises sharply with a single damaged tooth.
