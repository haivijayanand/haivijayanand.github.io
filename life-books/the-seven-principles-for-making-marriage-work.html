The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work

John M. Gottman and Nan Silver · first published 1999 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Lasting marriages are built less on avoiding conflict than on a deep, everyday friendship, where partners know each other well, show respect and turn toward each other in small moments.

Summary

John Gottman spent decades watching real couples talk, argue and relax together in his research lab, and tracking how their marriages turned out years later. His conclusion is reassuring: happy couples are not people who never fight. What sets them apart is a strong friendship underneath, full of respect, affection and attention to each other.

He first describes warning signs that often predict trouble. He calls them the Four Horsemen: criticism of a partner's character, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling, which means shutting down and withdrawing. Contempt, such as mockery or eye-rolling, is the most damaging. Arguments that open harshly, and moments when one person feels so overwhelmed they cannot think clearly, also do harm. Couples who make and accept small repair attempts, like a joke or a gentle word, recover much better.

The seven principles are the remedy. First, keep updating your love map, your knowledge of your partner's worries, hopes and daily world. Second, nurture fondness and admiration by noticing and saying what you appreciate. Third, turn toward each other when your partner reaches out for attention, even in tiny ways, rather than ignoring them. Fourth, let your partner influence you and share decisions.

Fifth, solve the problems that can be solved: raise issues gently, take breaks to calm down, accept repair attempts and be willing to compromise. Sixth, understand that many disagreements are lasting differences in personality or values. Instead of trying to win, explore the dreams and feelings behind each position so you can live with the difference kindly. Seventh, create shared meaning through rituals, goals and values you build together as a family.

The book is hopeful and practical. Small, steady acts of friendship, repeated daily, protect a marriage far better than grand gestures.

288 words

One-line takeaway: This week, notice each small bid your partner makes for attention, such as a comment or a sigh, and turn toward it with interest, and say one specific thing you appreciate about them each day.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. According to Gottman, what lies at the heart of a lasting marriage?

    Answer B. He finds that happy couples share a strong underlying friendship, not a conflict-free life.

  2. Which of the Four Horsemen does Gottman see as the most damaging?

    Answer D. Contempt, such as mockery or disgust, signals disrespect and is the strongest predictor of trouble.

  3. What does Gottman mean by a 'love map'?

    Answer A. A love map is the store of knowledge you keep about your partner's worries, hopes and experiences.

  4. What is 'stonewalling'?

    Answer C. Stonewalling is when a listener tunes out and withdraws instead of engaging.

  5. What does 'turning toward' your partner involve?

    Answer A. Turning toward means noticing and responding to everyday reach-outs rather than ignoring them.

  6. How does Gottman describe many long-running disagreements between couples?

    Answer D. He says many conflicts reflect enduring differences, so couples should learn to live with them kindly.

  7. What is a 'repair attempt'?

    Answer C. Repair attempts, like humour or a gentle remark, stop negativity from escalating.

  8. Which principle asks partners to share power and decisions?

    Answer B. Accepting influence means taking a partner's views seriously and sharing decision-making.

  9. What does Gottman suggest when a discussion becomes overwhelming?

    Answer A. He advises pausing to soothe yourself, since an overwhelmed mind cannot listen or think clearly.

  10. What does the seventh principle, creating shared meaning, include?

    Answer C. Shared meaning comes from rituals, goals and values the couple builds together.
