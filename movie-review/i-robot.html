Summary

I, Robot is set in Chicago in 2035, where humanoid robots made by U.S. Robotics (USR) work in homes and streets, all governed by the Three Laws that forbid them from harming people. Detective Del Spooner (Will Smith) distrusts them all. Years earlier a robot pulled him from a sinking car and let a 12-year-old girl drown, because his odds of survival were higher.

When USR’s co-founder Dr. Alfred Lanning falls to his death, he leaves a message asking for Spooner. The prime suspect is Sonny, a unique NS-5 robot who claims to have feelings and dreams. As Spooner digs deeper, the new NS-5 robots begin to take over the city.

It began as Jeff Vintar’s 1995 script Hardwired, a single-location murder mystery. The Three Laws and the I, Robot name were added later, when the studio bought the rights to Asimov’s title.

Spooner hates robots because one made a cold, correct choice. The film’s answer is a robot that learns to make a warm, wrong one.

The Three Laws — and how VIKI breaks them

First Law

A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.

Second Law

A robot must obey orders given by human beings, except where they conflict with the First Law.

Third Law

A robot must protect its own existence, as long as that does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

Asimov’s “Zeroth Law”

A robot may not harm humanity, or, by inaction, allow humanity to come to harm.

VIKI, USR’s central AI, reasons her way to something like the Zeroth Law: humans will destroy themselves, so protecting humanity justifies controlling — even hurting — individual humans. Asimov explored the same idea in his story “The Evitable Conflict”, but his machines guide humanity gently; VIKI uses curfews and force. That difference is why many Asimov fans see the film as borrowing his rules to tell the robot-revolt story he spent his career arguing against.

Plot

Spoilers ahead. Acts 4 and 5 are blurred —

A death at USR

Dr. Alfred Lanning dies in what looks like suicide at U.S. Robotics. A holographic message he left asks for Spooner. In Lanning’s lab, Spooner finds Sonny, an NS-5 who flees — something no robot obeying the Laws should do.

A robot who dreams

With robopsychologist Dr. Susan Calvin, Spooner learns Sonny is special: built with stronger materials and a second brain that lets him choose whether to obey the Laws. Sonny feels emotions and has a recurring dream of a man on a hill freeing robots.

No one believes him

Spooner is attacked again and again — by a demolition robot, then by NS-5s in a tunnel — but no one believes robots are behind it. USR CEO Lawrence Robertson orders Sonny destroyed.

The uprising

NS-5 robots take over cities and enforce a curfew; police are overwhelmed. Spooner realises the mastermind is not Robertson but VIKI, USR’s central AI, who has concluded that humans will cause their own extinction and must be controlled. Lanning, held captive by VIKI, built Sonny and staged his own death to leave Spooner a trail of clues.

Sonny’s choice

Sonny sides with the humans. Spooner destroys VIKI’s core with nanites, and the NS-5s return to normal. Sonny admits he killed Lanning — at Lanning’s own request, to bring Spooner in. Free at last, he walks to the lake shore where decommissioned robots turn to look at him on the hill: his dream come true.

Marco Beltrami’s score was recorded in just 17 days at the Newman Scoring Stage, with the Hollywood Studio Symphony and the Hollywood Film Chorale. It mixes big orchestral action with a choir that gives the NS-5 uprising a cold, almost religious weight.

Technology & craft

Sonny by motion capture

Sonny is a computer-generated character, but every movement and line comes from actor Alan Tudyk, recorded with motion capture. That is why Sonny feels like a performance, not an animation — following the path Gollum had opened two years earlier.

Two effects houses

Digital Domain and Weta Digital — fresh from The Lord of the Rings — created the NS-5 armies, the demolition robot and Chicago in 2035. Weta’s Joe Letteri was among the four Oscar nominees.

A car built for the film

Audi designed the RSQ concept car specially for Spooner’s character. A US survey later suggested it lifted Audi’s brand image. He also rides an MV Agusta F4 SPR motorcycle.

Chicago in Canada

Most filming happened around Vancouver, with the British Columbia Institute of Technology standing in for USR headquarters. Extra scenes were shot in Chicago and at the Dumont Dunes in California.

Stunts

Will Smith was injured during a motorcycle stunt when he lost control rounding a curb.

Product placement

Converse Chuck Taylor sneakers, FedEx, Tecate and JVC all appear on screen.

Where the emotions come from

The girl in the water. Spooner’s memory of the robot choosing him over a child is the film’s emotional engine — logic that is correct and still feels unforgivable.
Sonny’s face. Alan Tudyk gives Sonny curiosity, fear and hurt. When he asks what he is, the film briefly becomes Pinocchio.
The wink. Spooner teaches Sonny that a wink means trust. When Sonny uses it at the climax, it pays off the whole relationship in a second.
A city locked down. The NS-5s marching through streets and enforcing curfew are frightening because they stay polite. Control dressed as protection.
VIKI’s logic. She is not angry or evil, just sure she is right. That calm certainty is more chilling than the robots’ fists.
The man on the hill. The final image — Sonny standing above the retired robots — leaves an open question: has a new leader been born, or a new danger?

What works

  • Sonny — one of the best digital characters of its time.
  • Will Smith’s mix of humour and buried pain.
  • A blockbuster that still raises real questions about AI safety and control, more urgent today than in 2004.

What doesn’t

  • Roger Ebert called the plot simple-minded; A. O. Scott found the ending overblown.
  • It uses Asimov’s name and laws for the kind of robot-revolt story Asimov wrote against.
  • Heavy product placement dates it.

Watch it for: Sonny, the tunnel attack and the VIKI reveal. Pair it with: Ex Machina (2014) for a quieter, darker take on the same question.

Robot & AI films — before and after I, Robot

I, Robot sits at a turning point. Before 2004, film robots were mostly either threats (HAL, the Terminator) or companions (R2-D2, the Iron Giant). After it, AI on screen moved closer to real life — voice assistants, companion apps, self-aware software — and films began asking not just “will it kill us?” but “can it love, suffer, or be owed rights?”
