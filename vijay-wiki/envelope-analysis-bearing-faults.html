Envelope Analysis for Rolling Bearing Faults

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
EnvelopeDemodulationBearingsCondition monitoring

An early bearing defect produces tiny impacts that hardly change the overall vibration level. Each impact rings a high-frequency resonance of the bearing and housing, and the ringing repeats at the defect frequency. Envelope analysis isolates that resonance band, extracts the repetition rate, and shows the defect frequency clearly, often months before the fault is visible in a normal spectrum.

Key takeaways

  • Impacts excite structural resonances (typically 1–20 kHz); the defect frequency appears as the repetition rate of the ringing.
  • Steps: band-pass around a resonance → rectify or Hilbert transform → low-pass → FFT of the envelope.
  • Choose the band where impacts dominate: spectral kurtosis or a kurtogram finds it objectively.
  • Look for BPFO, BPFI (with shaft-speed sidebands), 2×BSF (with FTF sidebands) and their harmonics in the envelope spectrum.
  • Commercial variants include spike energy (gSE), PeakVue, HFD and shock pulse methods; the principle is the same.

1. Why ordinary spectra miss early defects

A small spall generates impacts with little energy at the defect frequency itself. Most of their energy appears as bursts of ringing at a resonance, smeared across many spectral lines and buried under gear mesh, unbalance and noise. Demodulation recovers the timing of the bursts.

2. The method

SettingGuidance
Sample rateAt least 2.56 × upper band edge (e.g. 51.2 kHz for a 20 kHz band)
Band-passCentred on an excited resonance; width ≥ 2 × (highest defect frequency + sidebands), typically 1–5 kHz wide
Envelope spectrum FmaxCover at least 3–4 harmonics of BPFI plus sidebands (often 10–20 × shaft speed)
ResolutionFine enough to separate shaft-speed sidebands: Δf ≤ shaft speed / 10
Averages4–8; use the same speed and load each time for trending

3. Reading the envelope spectrum

PatternLikely fault
BPFO and harmonics, no sidebandsOuter race defect (stationary in load zone)
BPFI and harmonics with ±1× shaft speed sidebandsInner race defect (rotating with shaft)
2×BSF with ±FTF sidebandsRolling element defect
FTF and harmonicsCage problem
1× shaft speed and harmonics onlyRubbing, looseness, or no bearing fault
Raised envelope noise floor, no clear linesLubrication problem, generalised roughness

4. Choosing the band: kurtosis and kurtogram

Impulsive signals have high kurtosis (about 3 for Gaussian noise, much higher with impacts). Spectral kurtosis computes kurtosis in many frequency bands; the kurtogram plots it against centre frequency and bandwidth and the maximum suggests the best demodulation band.

Practical tips

Mount the accelerometer stiffly (stud or adhesive) close to the load zone: a magnet base can cut response above a few kHz. Keep the same sensor, mounting and settings for trend comparisons. Confirm by checking that peaks move with shaft speed in the expected ratio.
