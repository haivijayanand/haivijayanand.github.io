    Parents, children and failure

    A feature's parents are the things it was built on: its sketch plane, sketch references, the edges it rounds, the axis it is coaxial with. Its children are the features built on it. Delete or change a parent and every child must find its references again — or fail.

    See themSelect a feature ▸ Tools ▸ Reference Viewer, or right-click ▸ Information ▸ Reference Viewer. Parents left, children right.
    Suppress / ResumeTemporarily remove a feature and its children from regeneration. Nothing is lost.
    ReorderDrag a feature up or down the tree. Creo will not let a child move above its parent.
    Insert modeDrag the green Insert Here arrow up the tree; new features go there. Drag it back to the end when done.
    When regeneration fails (Creo 4 and later), the model stays open, the failed feature is flagged in the tree, and the Notification Center explains. With regen_failure_handling resolve_mode Creo stops in a Resolve environment until you fix it. Your options: Undo Changes; Edit References (reroute — pick new references for the missing ones); Edit Definition; Suppress (fix later — but don't forget); Delete.
    Fail-safe habits: sketch on datum planes; dimension to datums; round and chamfer late; reference axes and datum planes rather than edges; avoid references to rounds; keep sketches simple.

    Do it in Creo

    1. Build the lab's part: base plate 120 × 80 × 12 on TOP; Ø40 × 30 boss sketched on the plate's top face; Ø20 hole in the boss; R3 round at the boss root; Ø9 hole + 2 × 2 pattern in the plate.
    2. Select the boss ▸ Tools ▸ Reference Viewer. Note parents (plate, its sketch references) and children (hole, round).
    3. Suppress the boss: Creo lists the children and suppresses them too. Resume All.
    4. Edit the boss sketch: delete the circle and sketch a 40 × 40 square instead. Finish. Watch Round 1 fail.
    5. Read the Notification Center. Choose Edit References on Round 1 and pick the four bottom edges of the square boss. Regenerate — fixed.
    6. Drag the Insert Here arrow above the boss and add a 1 mm chamfer to the plate edges. Drag the arrow back to the bottom.
    7. Try to drag the hole above the boss: Creo refuses — a child cannot go above its parent.

    Lab: break it and fix it

    Click features to see parents (orange edge) and children (blue edge). Suppress or delete to see what goes with them. Then redraw the boss as a square and choose how to fix the failed round.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Fix the first failure first. Later failures are often just children of the first.
    • "Suppress and fix later" is how parts leave the office with a missing round. Add a note or a tree column (Tree Filters ▸ Suppressed objects) and check before release.
    • Look at the tree column Status and the Notification Center rather than guessing which feature failed.
    • When you inherit a fragile model: use Reference Viewer on the failing feature and reroute it to a datum, not to another edge.
    • Deleting a sketch entity and drawing a new one gives it a new ID — any feature referencing the old edge loses it. Modify geometry instead of redrawing where you can.

    Practice

    1. Open any of your Week 1–2 parts and run Reference Viewer on its round features. Re-reference any that use edges of other rounds.
    2. Model a pump bracket badly on purpose (sketch on faces, dimension to rounded edges). Make three big changes and record what fails. Then rebuild it using datums and repeat — count the failures.
    3. Set regen_failure_handling to resolve_mode for one session and compare.

    Draft and ribs

    DraftModel ▸ Engineering ▸ Draft. Pick draft surfaces, the draft hinge (the plane or edge chain that does not move) and the pull direction, then the angle.
    Split draftIn the Split panel split by the hinge plane: each side drafts away from the parting line, as a two-part mould needs.
    Profile RibModel ▸ Engineering ▸ Rib ▸ Profile Rib. Sketch an open line between walls; Creo fills it with thickness, symmetric or to one side.
    Trajectory RibSketch rib paths on a plane; Creo grows them down to the next surfaces, with draft and rounds options — ideal inside shelled parts.
    Rule of thumbSand castingDie castingInjection moulding
    Draft1–3°0.5–2°0.5–1° outside, 1–2° textured
    Wall≥ 5 mm1.5–4 mm1–3 mm, uniform
    Rib thickness0.8 × wall0.6–0.8 × wall0.5–0.6 × wall

    Draft analysis: Analysis ▸ Inspect Geometry ▸ Draft colours faces by their angle to the pull direction against a limit you set.

    Do it in Creo

    1. New part housing_cast. Extrude a 160 × 100 × 60 block on TOP, Symmetric about TOP (so TOP is the parting plane).
    2. Draft: pick the four side faces (Shift for the loop), hinge = TOP, pull direction = TOP, angle 2°. In Split choose Split by draft hinge, sides draft independently. Finish.
    3. Round the vertical edges R10, then Shell 6 with the bottom face open.
    4. Analysis ▸ Inspect Geometry ▸ Draft: pick the part, direction TOP, angle 1°. Check every side face passes.
    5. Trajectory Rib: sketch on a plane 10 below the top inside face two lines crossing the inside. Thickness 4. Add draft and top rounds in the rib's Shape panel. Finish.
    6. Profile Rib: on FRONT, sketch an open line from the inside wall to the floor. Thickness 4 symmetric. Finish.
    7. Add Auto Round R2 for remaining inside edges at the end of the tree.

    Lab: draft analysis

    Change the draft angle and the pull direction and see which faces would stick in the mould.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Draft before shell, so the inside walls get draft too.
    • Draft faces, then round — rounds on undrafted edges make draft fail.
    • Machined faces (bearing bores, joint faces) need no draft; model the casting with machining allowance, then a machined version (family table or separate part).
    • Thick ribs cause sink marks and shrinkage porosity — thin, tall ribs are better than thick ones.
    • Use a datum plane as the hinge rather than a face — the parting line then stays put.

    Practice

    1. Model a die-cast gearbox cover 180 × 120 × 40 with 1.5° split draft, 3 mm walls, four internal ribs 2 mm thick with 0.5° draft, and four screw bosses Ø12 with Ø5.5 holes.
    2. Run draft analysis at 1° and fix any failing faces.
    3. Make a "machined" instance with a family table: suppress the draft on the joint face and add a 0.5 mm skim cut.

    Model ▸ Assemble brings in a component and opens the Component Placement dashboard. Each constraint you add removes degrees of freedom; the dashboard shows the status.

    Constraints are stored in the assembly. The part file knows nothing about where it is used.

    Do it in Creo

    1. Set a new working directory. File ▸ New ▸ Assembly ▸ Design, untick default template, choose mmns_asm_design_abs.
    2. Model ▸ Assemble the base plate from Day 15. Choose Default in the constraint list. Status: Fully Constrained. OK.
    3. Assemble a pin (Ø10 × 40 revolve). Pick the pin's axis and the plate hole axis → Coincident. Pick the pin's shoulder face and the plate top → Coincident. Status: Partially Constrained — the pin can still spin. That is correct for a pin.
    4. Assemble a bracket: Coincident bottom face to plate top; Coincident hole axis to pin axis; Parallel side face to plate side face. Fully Constrained.
    5. Edit the bracket's placement (right-click ▸ Edit Definition), change Parallel to Angle Offset 30°.
    6. Use the Move tab or the 3D dragger to drag a partly constrained part and see what is free.
    7. Open the bracket from the tree (right-click ▸ Open), change its thickness, save, return to the assembly and regenerate.

    Lab: constraints and degrees of freedom

    Tick constraints and watch which degrees of freedom remain. Press P to drag the bracket.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Place the first component with Default. Every other part then has a stable base.
    • Constrain to datum planes and axes of parts, not to faces of rounds or chamfers — the same reason as in parts.
    • Coincident between two axes is the Creo way of saying "concentric".
    • If something should move (shaft, slide), leave it partially constrained or — better — use a connection (Day 18).
    • Do not over-use Fix: the assembly then stops updating when parts change.
    • Rename components through the assembly (File ▸ Manage File ▸ Rename) so the assembly follows the new name.

    In the Component Placement dashboard the list next to the constraint type has user-defined (ordinary constraints) and predefined connections. A connection is a constraint set that leaves exactly the motion you want, and Mechanism knows how to drive it.

    Mechanism (Applications ▸ Mechanism) adds gear pairs, cams, belts, 3D contacts, springs, dampers, servo motors (prescribed motion on a connection axis) and analyses: Position (kinematics), Kinematic, Dynamic, Static, Force Balance.

    Gear pair: the two gears each need a Pin connection; you give the pitch diameters and Creo applies the ratio N₂/N₁ = d₂/d₁.

    Do it in Creo

    1. Assembly gear_unit: housing placed with Default.
    2. Assemble the input shaft. In the dashboard choose Pin from the connection list. Axis alignment: shaft axis ↔ bore axis. Translation: shaft shoulder ↔ bearing face. Status: connection definition complete.
    3. Same for the output shaft (Pin). Model ▸ Drag Components (the hand icon) — drag a shaft: it only turns.
    4. Applications ▸ Mechanism ▸ Gears: Gear 1 = input pin, pitch Ø 34; Gear 2 = output pin, pitch Ø 84. OK.
    5. Servo Motor on the input pin: velocity 1450 rpm = 8700 deg/s.
    6. Mechanism Analysis ▸ Position, 1 s, 100 frames, motor on. Run. Play back with Playback; tick Global Interference to check collisions during motion.
    7. Add a slider: a piston with a Slider connection in a cylinder, and a Pin-connected con-rod — then drive the crank and watch the slider follow.

    Lab: connections and the gear pair

    Pick a connection and press P to see the motion it allows.

    Then size a gear pair: centre distance, ratio and the pitch diameters to enter in Mechanism.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Use connections for anything that moves — constraints leave things free by accident; connections say what is free on purpose.

    • Gear pitch circles must touch: centre distance = (d₁ + d₂)/2. Check it in the skeleton, not by eye.
    • Kinematic analyses ignore mass; dynamic ones need mass properties (Day 14) and forces.
    • Save snapshots (Drag ▸ Snapshots) for positions you want on drawings: open, closed, mid-stroke.

    Skeletons

    A skeleton model is a special part in an assembly (Model ▸ Create ▸ Skeleton Model). It sits at the top of the tree, has no mass, is left out of BOMs, and holds the layout: datum axes for shafts, planes for joint faces, curves for paths, surfaces for envelopes.

    Publish GeometryIn the skeleton: a named package of references ("GEARBOX_INTERFACE") so parts take exactly that set and nothing else.
    Copy GeometryIn each part: copies references (or a published package) from the skeleton. The part's features are built on the copies — local references.
    DependentCopy Geometry can stay dependent (updates when the skeleton changes) or be made independent (frozen).
    Reference ControlModel ▸ Reference Control (or in config.pro) limits which external references are allowed — e.g. only to skeletons.
    Why not just sketch in context? A part that references other parts' faces directly breaks when they change and drags all of them into memory. Referencing one skeleton through Copy Geometry gives one clear source of truth and a part that opens on its own.

    Do it in Creo

    1. New assembly reducer. Model ▸ Create ▸ Type Skeleton Model ▸ name reducer_skel ▸ Copy from existing (empty mmns part). Place it with Default.
    2. Activate the skeleton (right-click ▸ Activate). Add datum axes A_IN and A_OUT 59 apart, a plane JOINT_FACE, and a sketched envelope curve for the housing.
    3. Name the centre distance C in the skeleton. Model ▸ Publish Geometry: A_IN, A_OUT, JOINT_FACE → name LAYOUT.
    4. Activate the assembly again. Create a part housing (Model ▸ Create ▸ Part ▸ Solid, empty mmns). Activate it.
    5. Model ▸ Get Data ▸ Copy Geometry ▸ Publish geometry ▸ pick LAYOUT. Finish. Build the housing bores on the copied axes.
    6. Do the same for the cover. Activate the assembly.
    7. Change C in the skeleton from 59 to 70. Regenerate the assembly — housing and cover bores move together.

    Lab: skeleton-driven parts

    Move the skeleton's centre distance and bearing size and see which design approach keeps the parts matching.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Keep skeletons light: datums, curves and simple surfaces. No solid features.
    • Name everything in the skeleton; parts will show those names in Copy Geometry.
    • Use Publish Geometry for anything more than one or two references — it documents the interface.
    • Information ▸ Reference Viewer on a part shows its external references; anything not going to the skeleton is a warning sign.
    • Top-down pays off on products with many interacting parts that change together — gearboxes, frames, housings with covers. For a single bracket it is overkill.

    Practice

    1. Build the gear reducer skeleton: shaft axes, bearing planes, joint face, bolt-pattern points, envelope surface.
    2. Housing and cover take geometry only from published packages. Bolt holes in both come from the same skeleton points.
    3. Change the bolt PCD and C in the skeleton; check holes still line up with Global Interference on bolts.

    Sheetmetal in Creo

    File ▸ New ▸ Part ▸ Sheetmetal (template mmns_part_sheetmetal_abs). The whole part has one thickness; every wall knows how to unbend.

    Planar wallThe first flat wall from a closed sketch.
    Flat wallAttached to an edge, flat shape from a library (rectangle, trapezoid, user sketch) at an angle.
    Flange wallProfile along an edge or chain (I, L, J, S, hem, open…). The workhorse.
    Bend / Unbend / Bend BackBend a flat region on a line; unbend for flat work; bend back after.
    ReliefsBend relief (rip, stretch, rectangle, obround) and corner relief stop tearing.
    Flat PatternA feature that shows the part flat; Flat Pattern Preview shows it in a separate window.
    Y-factor vs K-factor. Creo uses the Y-factor by default: Y = K·π/2. Bend allowance BA = (π/2·R + Y·T)·θ/90° for angle θ. Default Y = 0.50 means K ≈ 0.32. Many companies use bend tables instead (measured values per material/thickness/radius). Set it in File ▸ Prepare ▸ Model Properties ▸ Bend Allowance.

    Do it in Creo

    1. New Sheetmetal part bracket_sm.
    2. Planar wall: sketch 100 × 60 on TOP, thickness 2 in the dashboard (this sets the thickness of the whole part). Finish.
    3. Flange wall on one long edge: I-type, height 40, angle 90°, inside radius 2. Relief: Rip.
    4. Flange on the opposite edge: L-type (flange with a return of 15).
    5. Cut two Ø9 holes with an extrude Sheetmetal cut (normal to the sheet).
    6. Check bend allowance: File ▸ Prepare ▸ Model Properties ▸ Bend Allowance — note Y-factor 0.5. Change it to K 0.44 (Y 0.69) and watch the flat length change.
    7. Flat Pattern: pick the base wall as the fixed geometry. Measure the flat length; compare with the lab.
    8. Convert a solid: open a shelled box from Day 6, Operations ▸ Convert to Sheetmetal → Shell, add Edge rips at the corners.

    Lab: flat length and Y-factor

    Thickness, radius, angle, K and flange lengths → bend allowance, bend deduction, flat length, and the Y-factor Creo would use.

    Pro tips and common mistakes

    • Agree Y/K or bend tables with the shop. A wrong factor makes every flat pattern slightly wrong — enough to make flanges miss holes.
    • Keep holes at least 2T + R from a bend edge or they stretch into ovals.
    • Use Flat Pattern as the last feature; Unbend/Bend Back in the middle of the tree only for features that must be cut flat across a bend.
    • Minimum flange height ≈ 2.5T + R so the press-brake die can grip it.
    • Drawings of sheetmetal parts: one sheet with the bent views and a flat state view with bend lines and bend notes.

    Practice

    1. Electrical enclosure lid 300 × 200 × 2, four 25 flanges with 3 mm corner reliefs, louvres in the top (Form or Punch from a library if you have one, or simple slots).
