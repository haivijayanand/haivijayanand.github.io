Graphics software: vectors, pixels and plugins

Every printed carton, poster, logo and magazine photo passes through two kinds of program: a vector drawing program that describes shapes as mathematics, and an image editor that works on pixels. Illustrator, FreeHand and CorelDRAW fought over the first; Photoshop won the second so completely that its name became a verb. Around them grew an industry of plugins — and, in 2013, a switch from boxed software to subscriptions.

01The requirement: artwork without the drawing board and darkroom

Before these programs, commercial art had two separate crafts. Line art — logos, maps, technical illustrations, packaging artwork — was drawn in ink with technical pens, French curves, ruling pens and rub-down Letraset type, then photographed. Photographs were retouched with airbrushes and dyes on prints, combined by stripping film in the darkroom, and colour-separated on drum scanners costing as much as a building. Every change meant redrawing, rephotographing or re-scanning.

03Try it: the Bézier curve

Pierre Bézier developed these curves at Renault in the 1960s to describe car body panels; Paul de Casteljau did similar work at Citroën. PostScript, Illustrator, CorelDRAW, fonts and SVG all use them. Drag the four points: the two anchors (squares) fix the ends; the two handles (circles) pull the curve.

07Version explorer

Blue chips: vector programs. Amber chips: image editors.

The FreeHand story: when Adobe bought Aldus in 1994, competition regulators made FreeHand go back to its developer Altsys, and Macromedia took it over. In 2005 Adobe bought Macromedia anyway — and stopped developing FreeHand in 2007. Its loyal users never quite forgave Adobe.

08Why CorelDRAW ruled the Indian print shop

In the West, the Mac and Adobe dominated design studios. In India, the print trade — sign makers, screen printers, offset and flexo shops, visiting-card and wedding-card printers, sticker and label makers — grew up on Windows PCs with CorelDRAW. The reasons were practical:

  • It ran on the cheaper Windows PCs that small shops could afford, from the early 1990s.
  • One program did almost everything: drawing, page layout of a card or a carton face, text with Indian-language fonts, and bitmap tracing, plus PHOTO-PAINT in the same box.
  • Cutting plotters and vinyl sign machines worked directly from CorelDRAW files.
  • A self-taught operator could become productive quickly, and the .CDR file became the common language between customer, designer and printer.

CorelDRAW is still sold — Corel renamed itself Alludo in 2022 — with a browser version (CorelDRAW Web, 2025) and AI vector generation in CorelDRAW Graphics Suite 2026, available both as a subscription and as a one-time purchase.

09The plugin era

Photoshop shipped with a published plugin interface. Third-party filters (files ending in .8BF) appeared in the Filter menu, and many other programs — Corel PHOTO-PAINT, Paint Shop Pro, later Affinity Photo — learned to load the same plugins.

Many plugin effects were later absorbed into the host programs (layer styles, smart filters, content-aware fill). Adobe's plugin technology moved from C++ filters to HTML-based CEP panels (2013) and to the JavaScript-based UXP platform (2021).

11From boxes to subscriptions, and AI

  • Creative Suite (2003): Photoshop, Illustrator, InDesign and Acrobat sold together, one upgrade cycle every 18–24 months.
  • CS6 (2012) was the last version sold as a perpetual licence. In May 2013 Adobe announced that its tools would be available only through the Creative Cloud subscription. Many users protested; the company's revenue grew strongly anyway.
  • The move pushed price-sensitive users — including many small Indian studios — to CorelDRAW's one-time licence, Affinity, GIMP, Inkscape and Photopea.
  • AI (2023 →): Adobe Firefly and Photoshop's Generative Fill, CorelDRAW's AI vectors, Canva's Magic tools. The questions now are about training data, licensing of generated images, and authenticity labels (Content Credentials).

12Lessons for engineers

  1. Store geometry, not pixels, when you can. A vector drawing — like a CAD model — never loses resolution.
  2. Non-destructive editing pays off. Layers, masks and history let you change your mind without starting again.
  3. An open extension interface multiplies a product. Photoshop's plugin ecosystem helped make it the standard.
  4. Price model is a feature. Subscription, one-time and free licences each create their own user base.
