@tnotes Heat-seal quality on a bag-making machine comes down to four things: jaw temperature, dwell time, pressure and sealant thickness. This talk gives starting parameters for five common structures, explains the trends with a simple heat-conduction model, and ends with a practical test for finding the seal window on your own machine.

T: Five structures and their seal windows | tab1
N: The five structures range from plain LDPE film to PET laminates and BOPP films. For each, the table gives the build, the sealing layer, the jaw temperature at which sealing starts and the upper limit, and what sets that limit. Notice that the upper limit is set by different things: melting through for LDPE, shrinkage for BOPP, the sealant itself for PET laminates.

B: Temperature, time and pressure
- Jaw temperature and dwell set the interface temperature: whether a seal forms at all
- Pressure only needs full contact and air removal: 0.2–0.5 MPa
- Excess pressure squeezes molten sealant out and thins the seal edge
- Hot tack (molten seal strength) matters on form-fill-seal; cold strength (ASTM F88) is what the customer sees
N: Temperature and time together decide whether the sealant at the interface melts and fuses. Pressure only has to bring the faces into contact; above 0.2 to 0.5 MPa it adds nothing and can squeeze out molten polymer. Hot tack is the strength of the still-molten seal, important where product drops onto the bottom seal; cold seal strength is measured after cooling.

E: Heat conduction sets the dwell | eq1
W: Conduction time scales with the square of thickness: t ≈ L²/α, with LDPE diffusivity α ≈ 0.16 mm²/s. Double the sealant thickness and the dwell needed roughly quadruples.
N: The physics is heat conduction. The time for heat to cross a layer goes with thickness squared over thermal diffusivity. For LDPE the diffusivity is about 0.16 square millimetres per second. So a thicker sealant needs a hotter jaw and disproportionately longer dwell. Set the dwell for the thickest sealant a job uses.

B: The effect of a PET outer layer
- PET is a heat shield: jaws can run 30–50 °C hotter without damage
- But 12 µm PET adds conduction lag: higher jaw temperature than plain LDPE
- Usable seal window roughly doubles: 35–45 °C vs ≈20 °C for plain LDPE
- BOPP: the oriented core shrinks close to the seal temperature → narrowest window
N: A PET outer layer changes everything. It shields the sealant from the jaw, so the jaw can run much hotter without damaging the outside of the bag. Its thickness adds a conduction lag, so the jaw must be hotter than for plain LDPE. The net result is a much wider usable window. BOPP is the opposite: its oriented core shrinks near the sealing temperature, so its window is narrow.

T: Sealing methods | tab3
N: Constant-heat bars, impulse sealing, hot-knife side-weld, ultrasonic and hot-air sealing each suit different structures and machines. Ultrasonic sealing, for example, heats only the interface and works through contamination, which is attractive for mono-material films with narrow windows.

T: Defects and troubleshooting | tab4 | cols=0,1,2,3
N: The troubleshooting table lists common symptoms, weak or leaking seals, wrinkles, burn-through, delamination, stringing and curl, with the structures most affected, the likely cause and the first fix. Most seal problems turn out to be temperature variation along the bar, insufficient dwell for the actual thickness, or wrong pressure.

T: Evolution of bag-making machines | tab5
N: Machines have gone from mechanical cam drives through electronic controls to full servo drives. Servo machines decouple dwell from cycle speed, removing the historical trade-off between speed and seal quality, and rated speeds have risen severalfold.

T: Current benchmarks | tab6
N: Today's machine classes, wicketers, side-weld machines, three-side-seal pouch machines and others, with typical structures, rated speeds and example makers, give a benchmark for what a modern line should achieve.

T: Future directions | tab7
N: Looking forward: mono-material recyclable films with narrow windows, ultrasonic and laser sealing, closed-loop temperature control along the bar, and inline seal inspection. Each changes how sealing parameters are set and monitored.

B: A practical seal-window test
- Condition film 24 h at 23 °C / 50 % RH; cure PU adhesive 48–72 h
- Fix pressure and dwell; map jaw temperature at five points (±3 °C)
- Step temperature by 5 °C from 20 °C below to 20 °C above the range
- Peel 15 mm strips at 300 mm/min after 24 h (ASTM F88); record failure mode
- Window = film-tear failure with no visual defect; set production at the centre
N: The paper recommends this procedure to set up any job. Condition the film and make sure the adhesive is cured. Map the jaw temperature along the bar first, because non-uniformity is the most common hidden problem. Step through temperatures, peel-test the seals, and define the window by film-tearing failure. Then repeat at a second dwell and run production at the window centre.

C: Conclusions
- PET-outer laminates: easiest and most forgiving to seal
- Plain LDPE: easy but bounded at both ends
- BOPP structures: least forgiving; oriented core shrinks near sealing temperature
- Thicker sealant: hotter jaws and disproportionately longer dwell
- Verify 0.2–0.5 MPa interface pressure by calculation, not the gauge
FW: Practice
- Set dwell for the thickest sealant a job uses
- Map jaw temperature before blaming the film
- Run the seal-window test for every new structure
N: Servo machines have removed the old trade-off between speed and seal quality, but the physics of heat conduction has not changed. Set up each job with the seal-window test and the parameters here as a starting point.
