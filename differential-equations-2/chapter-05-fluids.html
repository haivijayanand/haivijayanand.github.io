Fluids and Lubrication

From the full Navier–Stokes equations down to the special cases engineers solve every day: Bernoulli and flow meters, pipe flow, Couette shear and bearing friction, settling particles, the Blasius boundary layer, the Reynolds equation of hydrodynamic lubrication, water hammer, Burgers' shocks and Darcy groundwater flow.

Fluid mechanics is one great PDE system — mass, momentum and energy conservation — and almost nobody solves it whole. Engineering progress came from knowing what to throw away: viscosity (Bernoulli), inertia (Stokes, Reynolds lubrication), the downstream direction (boundary layers), or two space dimensions (pipes). Each card shows one such simplification.

41Navier–Stokes equations

PDEnonlinearsystemNavier 1822; Stokes 1845
ρ·(∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = −∇p + μ·∇²u + f ∇·u = 0 (incompressible)

u velocity field · p pressure · μ viscosity · f body force · Reynolds number Re = ρUL/μ = inertia/viscous

Newton's second law for every fluid particle: acceleration (including the nonlinear "carried along" term u·∇u) equals pressure push plus viscous rubbing plus gravity. The nonlinearity makes turbulence possible; whether smooth solutions always exist in 3-D is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems. The Reynolds number decides the character of the flow: below about 2300 in a pipe, smooth and laminar; far above, turbulent.

Stokes' first problem — a plate suddenly set moving

For flow along x over a plate that starts moving at speed U, only u(y, t) survives: ∂u/∂t = ν·∂²u/∂y². It is the heat equation (ν = μ/ρ plays the role of α), so u = U·erfc(y/(2√(νt))): momentum diffuses into the fluid, and the "boundary layer" thickens like √(νt). The lab shows this exact special case.

Reynolds numbers: water at 2 m/s in a 50 mm pipe, and gear oil (ν = 50 mm²/s) at the same speed.Water: Re = 1000 × 2 × 0.05/0.001 = 100 000 — turbulent Oil: Re = 2 × 0.05/50e-6 = 2000 — laminar, just below transition Same pipe and speed, opposite flow regimes: which equation applies depends on Re.
Where you meet itEverything that flows: aerodynamics, pumps and turbines, weather and ocean models, blood flow, lubrication and cooling oil jets in gearboxes — solved numerically by CFD.

42Euler equations and Bernoulli's equation

PDEnonlinearinviscidEuler 1757; D. Bernoulli 1738
ρ·Du/Dt = −∇p + ρg along a streamline, steady: p + ½·ρ·v² + ρ·g·z = constant

p static pressure · ½ρv² dynamic pressure · ρgz hydrostatic head

Drop viscosity from Navier–Stokes and integrate along a streamline: pressure, kinetic and potential energy trade with each other. Faster flow means lower pressure — the principle of venturi and orifice meters, carburettors, aerofoil lift and cavitation.

A venturi meter: 100 mm pipe narrowing to 50 mm, pressure difference 20 kPa, water. Flow rate?Continuity: v₁ = v₂/4. Bernoulli: Δp = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²) = ½ρv₂²(1 − 1/16) v₂ = √(2 × 20 000/(1000 × 0.9375)) = 6.53 m/s Q = v₂ × π × 0.05²/4 = 0.0128 m³/s = 12.8 L/s (× C_d ≈ 0.98 for a real venturi)
Where you meet itFlow meters (venturi, orifice, Pitot tube for airspeed), siphons, spray nozzles, cavitation risk at pump inlets and in hydraulic valves, wind loads, lube-oil jets.

43Hagen–Poiseuille pipe flow

ODElinearboundary valueHagen 1839; Poiseuille 1840
(1/r)·d/dr( r·du/dr ) = (1/μ)·dp/dx → u = (Δp/(4μL))·(R² − r²), Q = π·R⁴·Δp/(8·μ·L)

R pipe radius · Δp/L pressure gradient · μ dynamic viscosity

Steady laminar flow in a straight pipe: Navier–Stokes collapses to a one-line ODE across the radius. The profile is a parabola, peak speed twice the mean. Flow goes as R⁴: halve the bore and you need sixteen times the pressure — the reason narrowed arteries and partly blocked oil galleries are so serious.

A gearbox lube line: 4 mm bore, 2 m long, oil μ = 0.03 Pa·s (ρ = 870), 1 L/min. Pressure drop? Check laminar.Q = 1.667×10⁻⁵ m³/s, R = 2 mm Δp = 8μLQ/(πR⁴) = 8 × 0.03 × 2 × 1.667e-5/(π × 1.6e-11) = 1.59×10⁵ Pa ≈ 1.6 bar Mean speed 1.33 m/s, Re = 870 × 1.33 × 0.004/0.03 = 154 → laminar ✓ With a 3 mm bore: Δp × (4/3)⁴ = 5.0 bar.
Where you meet itLube-oil and hydraulic lines, capillary viscometers, blood flow in vessels, IV drips and syringes, microfluidics, oil-gallery design in gearbox housings.

44Couette flow and Petroff's bearing friction

ODElinearboundary valueCouette 1890; Petroff 1883
μ·d²u/dy² = 0 → u = U·y/h, τ = μ·U/h Petroff torque T = 2π·μ·R³·L·ω / c

U sliding speed · h gap (in a bearing the radial clearance c) · τ shear stress

Fluid sheared between a moving and a fixed surface takes a straight-line velocity profile. Applied to a lightly loaded, concentric journal bearing, it gives the friction torque — Petroff's law, still the first estimate of bearing power loss and heat generation. Friction rises with speed and viscosity and falls with clearance.

A journal bearing: D = 50 mm, L = 25 mm, radial clearance 50 µm, 6000 rpm, oil μ = 0.02 Pa·s. Friction force and power loss?U = ωR = 628.3 × 0.025 = 15.7 m/s τ = μU/c = 0.02 × 15.7/50e-6 = 6280 Pa F = τ·πDL = 6280 × π × 0.05 × 0.025 = 24.7 N → power = F·U = 388 W of heat per bearing This heat is the P₀ of the heat balance in equation 40.
Where you meet itPlain-bearing friction and heat, viscometers (rotating cylinder), seals, lubricated slideways, the oil film between piston rings and liner, the viscous coupling.

45Stokes drag and particle settling

ODElinearfirst orderStokes 1851
m·dv/dt = (ρp − ρf)·V·g − 3π·μ·d·v → vt = (ρp − ρf)·g·d²/(18·μ)

d particle diameter · ρp, ρf particle and fluid densities · valid for particle Reynolds number < 1

At very low Reynolds number drag is proportional to speed, not speed squared (compare equation 11), so the equation is linear and the particle reaches terminal speed after a tiny time constant ρpd²/(18μ). Settling speed goes as d²: big particles drop out fast, fine ones stay suspended for hours — the principle behind sumps, magnetic plugs, clarifiers and the centrifuge.

Steel wear debris, 10 µm, in gearbox oil (μ = 0.03 Pa·s, ρ = 870). Settling speed? And for 100 µm?v_t = (7850 − 870) × 9.81 × (10e-6)²/(18 × 0.03) = 1.27×10⁻⁵ m/s ≈ 4.6 cm per hour 100 µm: ×100 → 1.27 mm/s ≈ 4.6 m per hour So large wear particles reach the magnetic plug, while fine ones circulate to the filter — and to the oil sample bottle, which is why oil analysis sees them.
Where you meet itOil sumps and debris monitoring, sedimentation tanks, centrifuges and cyclones, blood sedimentation rate, aerosol and dust behaviour, Millikan's oil-drop experiment, viscometers (falling ball).

46Blasius boundary layer

ODEnonlinearthird orderPrandtl 1904; Blasius 1908
f‴ + ½·f·f″ = 0, f(0) = f′(0) = 0, f′(∞) = 1 δ ≈ 5x/√Rex, Cf = 0.664/√Rex

η = y·√(U/(νx)) similarity variable · f′(η) = u/U velocity profile · Rex = Ux/ν

Prandtl's great insight: viscosity matters only in a thin layer near a surface; outside it, Bernoulli rules. For a flat plate, the boundary-layer PDEs reduce — by the same similarity trick as equation 31 — to Blasius's ODE. It has no closed form; the shooting method finds f″(0) = 0.332, which gives skin friction directly.

Air at 10 m/s over a 0.5 m plate (ν = 1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s). Is the flow laminar at the end? Boundary-layer thickness there?Re_x = 10 × 0.5/1.5e-5 = 3.3×10⁵ (< 5×10⁵, still laminar) δ = 5x/√Re = 2.5/577 = 4.3 mm Local skin friction C_f = 0.664/577 = 0.00115 → wall shear = C_f·½ρU² = 0.07 Pa
Where you meet itDrag of wings, hulls and car bodies, heat transfer from surfaces (the thermal boundary layer is its twin), cooling-fin spacing, hot-wire anemometers, why turbulence promoters improve heat exchangers.

47Reynolds equation (hydrodynamic lubrication)

PDEnonlinearellipticOsborne Reynolds 1886
∂/∂x( h³·∂p/∂x ) + ∂/∂z( h³·∂p/∂z ) = 6·μ·U·∂h/∂x journal: h = c·(1 + ε·cos θ)

p film pressure · h film thickness · U surface speed · ε eccentricity ratio · c radial clearance

In a thin film, inertia is negligible and pressure is constant across the gap; Navier–Stokes integrates across the film into one equation for pressure. A converging wedge of oil, dragged by the moving surface, builds pressure large enough to float a shaft on a film thinner than a hair — no contact, almost no wear. The short-bearing (Ocvirk) solution drops the circumferential term and gives closed-form pressure and load.

Short-bearing solution

For L/D < ½, ∂/∂z(h³∂p/∂z) dominates: integrate twice in z with p = 0 at both ends: p = (3μU/(R·c²))·(L²/4 − z²)·ε sin θ/(1 + ε cos θ)³, kept only where positive (the rest cavitates). Integrating p over the bearing gives the load W = (μUL³/c²)·ε/(4(1 − ε²)²)·√(π²(1 − ε²) + 16ε²) and the attitude angle tan φ = π√(1 − ε²)/(4ε).

The lab's bearing: D = 50 mm, L = 25 mm, c = 50 µm, 6000 rpm, μ = 0.02 Pa·s, ε = 0.6. Load capacity?U = 15.7 m/s W = (0.02 × 15.7 × 0.025³/(50e-6)²) × 0.6/(4 × 0.64²) × √(π² × 0.64 + 16 × 0.36) = 2.50 kN Minimum film h_min = c(1 − ε) = 20 µm; attitude angle 46°. At ε = 0.8 the same bearing carries about 11 kN with a 10 µm film.
Where you meet itJournal and thrust bearings in turbines, compressors, engines and gearboxes; piston rings; slipper pads; magnetic-disk heads (air bearings); joint lubrication in knees and hips.

48Water hammer equations

PDElinearhyperbolicJoukowsky 1898; Allievi 1902 · Part I, Day 13: Waves and Laplace
∂H/∂t + (a²/g)·∂V/∂x = 0 ∂V/∂t + g·∂H/∂x + f·V|V|/(2D) = 0 Joukowsky: ΔH = a·ΔV/g

H head · V flow velocity · a pressure-wave speed (≈ 1200 m/s in steel water pipe, less in plastic) · f friction factor

Shut a valve quickly and the moving water column has to stop. Fluid compressibility and pipe elasticity turn the change into a pressure wave that travels back and forth at speed a — a wave equation. A closure faster than the round-trip time 2L/a produces the full Joukowsky surge, which can burst pipes; a slow closure reduces it in proportion.

A 600 m steel water main carries 1.5 m/s. The valve shuts in 0.2 s. Surge? Safe closing time for a 20 m surge?2L/a = 1200/1200 = 1.0 s > 0.2 s → rapid closure ΔH = a·V/g = 1200 × 1.5/9.81 = 183 m ≈ 18 bar on top of the static pressure For 20 m: t_c ≈ (2L/a)·(183/20) = 9.2 s
Where you meet itPump trips and valve closures in water mains and penstocks, hydraulic systems (pressure spikes in fuel and oil lines), fire-sprinkler piping, surge tanks and air vessels, the thump in household plumbing.

49Burgers' equation (shocks)

PDEnonlinearparabolicBateman 1915; Burgers 1948
∂u/∂t + u·∂u/∂x = ν·∂²u/∂x² inviscid shock forms at t* = −1/min(u₀′(x))

u velocity (or traffic density, or wave amplitude) · ν viscosity

The simplest equation with both nonlinear steepening and diffusion. Faster parts of a wave catch up with slower parts ahead, so the profile steepens; without viscosity it would overturn at a finite time. Viscosity holds the front as a thin shock of width ∝ ν. It is a toy model for shock waves, traffic jams and turbulence — and the standard test for CFD schemes.

Inviscid Burgers' equation with u₀(x) = sin x. When does the shock form?Characteristics x = x₀ + u₀(x₀)·t cross first where u₀′ is most negative: u₀′ = cos x, minimum −1 at x = π t* = −1/(−1) = 1. After that, a shock sits at x = π and dissipates energy.
Where you meet itShock waves in gases, traffic flow (jams form as backward-moving shocks), sediment transport, nonlinear acoustics (sonic booms), testing numerical schemes.

50Darcy flow and groundwater diffusion

PDElinearparabolicDarcy 1856; Theis 1935
q = −K·dh/dx (Darcy) Ss·∂h/∂t = K·∂²h/∂x² (diffusion of head)

q specific discharge · K hydraulic conductivity (gravel 10⁻², sand 10⁻⁴, clay 10⁻⁹ m/s) · h hydraulic head · Ss specific storage

Flow through porous media is proportional to the head gradient — Darcy's experiments with sand filters in Dijon. Add storage and you get another heat equation, with hydraulic diffusivity K/Ss. The same equations describe oil reservoirs, soil consolidation under foundations (Terzaghi) and seepage under dams.

A sand aquifer (K = 10⁻⁴ m/s) with a water-table slope of 0.5 % and a flow cross-section of 100 m². Daily flow?Q = K·i·A = 1e-4 × 0.005 × 100 = 5×10⁻⁵ m³/s = 4.3 m³ per day Through clay (K = 10⁻⁹) the same gradient passes 0.04 litres per day — which is why clay liners work.
Where you meet itGroundwater and wells, oil and gas reservoirs, seepage under dams and through embankments, consolidation of soil under buildings, filters, porous bearings (sintered bronze bushes), drainage design.
