1Power iteration and its relatives

Day 3 showed that repeated multiplication lines a vector up with the dominant eigenvector. Normalise each step to stop it overflowing and you have an algorithm:

Power iteration: v ← A v / |A v|, λ ≈ vᵀAv / vᵀv (the Rayleigh quotient)
Example 1 — A = [2 1; 1 2], start v = (1, 0)Av: (2, 1) → (5, 4) → (14, 13) → (41, 40) → (122, 121) Rayleigh quotient: 2.80, 2.976, 2.9973, 2.99970 … → 3 The error falls by (λ₂/λ₁)² = (1/3)² ≈ 1/9 per step (squared because A is symmetric). Rayleigh quotient at v = (1, 0.9): (2.9 + 2.52)/1.81 = 2.9945 — the vector is 5 % off but λ only 0.18 %.

Two transformations from Day 2 turn power iteration into a tool for any eigenvalue:

  • Inverse iteration: apply the power method to (A − σI)⁻¹, whose eigenvalues are 1/(λ − σ). The one nearest σ becomes dominant. You never form the inverse — each step solves (A − σI)w = v. Convergence factor |λ_near − σ| / |λ_next − σ|: excellent when σ is a good guess.
  • Rayleigh quotient iteration: update σ to the current Rayleigh quotient every step. For symmetric matrices the number of correct digits roughly triples each step (cubic convergence).

Try "Two close eigenvalues" with the power method: |λ₂/λ₁| = 0.91 and it crawls. Switch to inverse iteration with σ = 5.3, or to Rayleigh, and it finishes in a handful of steps. The orange dashed line keeps the previous run for comparison.

2What the libraries do

MethodIdeaUsed for
QR algorithm (Francis, Kublanovskaya, 1961)Factor A = QR, form RQ, repeat. Each step is a similarity transform (same eigenvalues); the matrix converges to triangular form with the eigenvalues on the diagonal. Practical versions first reduce to Hessenberg/tridiagonal form and use shifts — about 10n³ operations for all eigenvalues.Dense matrices up to a few thousand: NumPy/LAPACK eig, MATLAB eig. This course's labs use it for non-symmetric matrices.
Jacobi (1846)Rotate away the largest off-diagonal entry, again and again, until the symmetric matrix is diagonal. Slow but very accurate and simple.Small symmetric matrices; this course's labs use it for stresses, modes, PCA and the Laplacian.
Lanczos / ArnoldiBuild an orthonormal basis from v, Av, A²v, … (a Krylov space) and solve a small projected problem. Needs only matrix–vector products.Huge sparse problems where you want a few eigenvalues: FE modal and buckling analysis (Block Lanczos in ANSYS, Lanczos in NASTRAN), PageRank-type and quantum problems. SciPy eigsh / eigs.
Subspace iterationPower iteration on a block of vectors at once, orthogonalised each step.Classic structural dynamics codes; the first 20–100 modes of a building or vehicle.
Practical rule for FE modal analysis: you never need all million modes of a model — the lowest few dozen contain almost all the mass participation, and they are exactly what shift-and-invert Lanczos delivers fastest. Ask for modes up to 1.5–2 × the highest excitation frequency of interest.
Why the QR algorithm converges

QR iteration is secretly power iteration on a whole set of vectors at once. After k steps, the first column of the accumulated Q is Aᵏe₁ normalised — power iteration — and the first m columns span the same space as m vectors after k power steps, orthogonalised. So the columns line up with the eigenvectors in order of |λ|, and the sub-diagonal entries shrink like |λᵢ₊₁/λᵢ|ᵏ. Shifting by a good estimate of an eigenvalue (as in inverse iteration) makes the last sub-diagonal entry vanish quadratically or cubically, then the problem deflates to a smaller one.

3Gershgorin discs: bounds for free

Gershgorin circle theorem. Every eigenvalue of A lies in at least one of the discs centred at a diagonal entry aᵢᵢ with radius Rᵢ = Σⱼ≠ᵢ |aᵢⱼ| (the rest of row i).
Example 2 — A = [10 1 −2 ; 1 6 1 ; 0.5 −1 2]Row 1: centre 10, radius 3 → [7, 13] Row 2: centre 6, radius 2 → [4, 8] Row 3: centre 2, radius 1.5 → [0.5, 3.5] 0 lies in no disc → A is invertible. Actual eigenvalues: 10.18, 5.27, 2.54 — one in each disc. (When discs do not overlap, each contains exactly one eigenvalue.)
Why engineers like itA strictly diagonally dominant stiffness matrix is positive definite without solving anything; if every disc of a system matrix lies in the left half-plane, the system is stable (Day 6); it gives the starting interval for bisection and a quick sanity check on any eigen-solver output.

4When can you trust an eigenvalue?

Symmetric matrices are safe: a perturbation of size ε moves every eigenvalue by at most ε (Weyl's theorem). Stresses, modes, PCA and Laplacians are well-conditioned eigenproblems.

Non-symmetric matrices can be treacherous. When eigenvectors are nearly parallel, tiny changes in the matrix move the eigenvalues a lot:

Example 3 — A = [1 1; ε 1]Eigenvalues 1 ± √ε ε = 0 → 1, 1 (defective) ε = 10⁻¹⁰ → 1 ± 10⁻⁵ A change of 10⁻¹⁰ in one entry moved the eigenvalues by 10⁻⁵ — 100 000 times larger. For x′ = Ax stability margins of such systems should be judged with care (pseudospectra), not by eigenvalues alone: transient growth can be large even when all Re λ < 0.

The other lesson of the course in one line: compute eigenvalues from the matrix, never from the characteristic polynomial, and prefer symmetric formulations (K φ = ω²Mφ, covariance, Laplacian) whenever the physics allows.
