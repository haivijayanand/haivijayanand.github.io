2The diagnosis, in one paragraph

Kaveri Foods grew revenue 20% and profit 9%, but the growth was low quality. Gross margin slipped a point on input costs. Collections slowed (DSO 62 → 73 days) and stock built up (DIO 91 → 98 days), lengthening the cash cycle by 23 days and locking up about ₹75 crore. That was funded by ₹80 crore of new borrowing, so finance costs rose 36%, interest cover fell from 4.8× to 4.0×, and operating cash flow (₹48 crore) covered only two-thirds of profit. ROE held at 12.8% only because leverage rose. The market noticed: P/E fell from 17.9× to 15.1×. Priorities: tighten credit and collections, cut slow-moving stock, recover price on key products, and stop adding debt until cash flow catches up with profit.

Level 3 · For experts — a review

How industry economics shape ratios, and where ratio analysis fails.

1Guess the industry

A classic exercise (from Harvard's "case of the unidentified industries"): ratio profiles reflect business economics. Match each profile to its industry.

2The limits of ratio analysis

  • Backward-looking: ratios describe the past; value depends on the future.
  • Accounting choices: depreciation methods, revenue timing, capitalisation, leases and revaluations change ratios without changing cash.
  • Window dressing and seasonality distort year-end balances.
  • Inflation and historical cost overstate returns of old asset bases.
  • Diversified groups mix businesses; use segment data.
  • No single benchmark: "good" depends on industry, strategy and cycle.
  • Missing assets: brands, data, people and relationships are not on the balance sheet.
  • Manipulation: managers know which ratios lenders and analysts watch (covenants, targets) and may manage them (Beneish's M-score is one screen for earnings manipulation).

Use ratios to ask better questions, then answer them with the notes, cash flows, industry data and conversations with management.
