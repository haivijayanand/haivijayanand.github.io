Unbalance → Velocity

Residual unbalance (g·mm) · rotor mass (kg) · speed (rpm) → vibration velocity (mm/s)
01

The question has two answers

"A shaft of mass m with residual unbalance U g·mm runs at N rpm — what velocity does it produce?" There are two legitimate answers, and confusing them is the single most common error in balancing work.

  1. The kinematic velocity — how fast the mass centre travels around the rotational axis. This follows from U, m and N alone, needs no other information, and is the ISO 1940 balance quality grade G.
  2. The response velocity — what a transducer actually measures on the machine. This is the kinematic value multiplied by a dynamic amplification factor that depends on the structure: natural frequency, damping, and where you put the probe.

The first is a property of the rotor. The second is a property of the machine. A perfectly acceptable G 2.5 rotor can read 15 mm/s on the bearing housing if you park it on its critical speed.

02

Specific unbalance, and a convenient unit identity

Unbalance U is a mass–radius product: 400 g·mm is 4 g sitting 100 mm off-axis, or 0.4 g at 1000 mm — dynamically identical. Dividing by rotor mass gives specific unbalance e, the offset of the mass centre from the rotational axis:

e = U / m With U in g·mm and m in kg, note that m [kg] = 1000 m [g], so
e [mm] = U / (1000 m)  ⇒  e [µm] = U [g·mm] / m [kg]
The units cancel to give micrometres directly. 440 g·mm on a 44 kg rotor is exactly 10 µm of mass-centre offset.
03

Kinematic velocity, and why it is the G grade

The mass centre travels a circle of radius e once per revolution. Its tangential speed is radius × angular velocity:

ω = 2πN / 60 [rad/s] v = e · ω [mm/s, zero-to-peak] e in mm, ω in rad/s. Substituting the identity above: v [mm/s] = U [g·mm] × ω / (1000 m [kg]).

ISO 1940-1 defines the balance quality grade as precisely this product, eper·ω, in mm/s. That is why the grade table reads G 0.4, G 1, G 2.5, G 6.3, G 16… — each grade is a constant-eω hyperbola on the familiar log–log nomogram. Doubling the speed halves the permissible unbalance for the same grade.

Uper [g·mm] = 1000 · G · m [kg] / ω Total permissible residual unbalance for a target grade G at service speed. For a two-plane rotor this is then allocated between the correction planes — see section 06.

What this number is not eω is the velocity of an imaginary point — the mass centre — relative to the shaft axis. No transducer measures it directly. It is a specification quantity for the balancing machine, not a condition monitoring quantity for the rig.
04

From unbalance to a measured response

Rotating unbalance produces a force that grows with the square of speed:

F = m · e · ω2 = U · ω2 × 10−6 [N, with U in g·mm] Rotating in space at 1× shaft speed. This is why unbalance always appears at the 1× order line, and why it dominates high-speed rigs: at 16,800 rpm the force is 78× what it was at 1,900 rpm.

That force feeds a structure with mass, stiffness and damping. The equation of motion for a single degree of freedom driven by rotating unbalance is

M ẍ + c ẋ + k x = Uω2 sin ωt X / eeff = r2 / √[ (1 − r2)2 + (2ζr)2 ] r = ω/ωn is the frequency ratio, ζ = c / (2√(kM)) the damping ratio,
ωn = √(k/M) the undamped natural frequency, and eeff = U / M the effective offset.
Phase lag: φ = arctan[ 2ζr / (1 − r2) ], running from 0° below resonance to 180° above.

Once the displacement amplitude X is known, velocity and acceleration follow from harmonic differentiation:

v̂ = ωX â = ω2X

Three regimes worth memorising

  • r << 1 (stiff, sub-critical): X → e·r2. The structure barely moves; almost all the unbalance force goes into the foundation. Velocity rises as the cube of speed here, not linearly.
  • r ≈ 1 (resonance): X → e / 2ζ. With ζ = 0.02 that is 25× amplification. Phase passes through 90° — the reliable resonance indicator, far more trustworthy than the amplitude peak.
  • r >> 1 (super-critical): X → e, and the rotor self-centres: it spins about its mass centre and the geometric centre orbits by e. Velocity then approaches e·ω — the kinematic answer. The two curves converge.
Why the two answers meet at high speed Well above the critical speed the response asymptote is exactly the ISO 1940 kinematic line. That is not a coincidence: a self-centred rotor's geometric centre really does orbit at eω. Below and near the critical speed the two diverge sharply — which is the whole reason both are plotted together in the simulator.
05

Single-plane models — two different measurements

The algebra above is identical for both single-plane models offered here. What differs is which stiffness sets ωn, which mass appears in eeff, and — critically — what the resulting amplitude physically means.

Model A — rigid rotor on flexible supports

The shaft is treated as rigid; all the flexibility is in the bearings, pedestals and mounts. M is the total supported mass and k the support stiffness. The computed X is the absolute motion of the housing — what an accelerometer or velocity pickup on the bearing cap reads, and what ISO 20816 zone limits are written against. Force transmitted onward to the foundation:

FT = k X √[ 1 + (2ζr)2 ]

Model B — Jeffcott rotor (flexible shaft, stiff bearings)

The classic Jeffcott/Laval model: a disc of mass m at mid-span on a massless elastic shaft of stiffness k, in rigid bearings. Here ωn = √(k/m) is the shaft's critical speed, and X is the lateral deflection of the shaft relative to the bearings — what an eddy-current proximity probe measures, not an accelerometer. In the idealised case the housing does not move at all, which is exactly why turbomachinery with stiff bearings is instrumented with proximity probes.

Choose the model to match the probe Casing-mounted accelerometer or velocity pickup → Model A. Shaft-riding proximity probe → Model B. Applying an ISO 20816 casing limit to a Model B shaft-relative number, or vice versa, is a category error.
06

Model C — the two-plane rigid rotor

Models A and B collapse the rotor to a single point, which cannot represent the most common real situation: a rotor between two bearings, with unbalance distributed along its length. A rigid rotor needs two correction planes and has two rigid-body modes.

Static and couple decomposition

Given unbalance vectors U1∠θ1 and U2∠θ2 at axial positions z1 and z2, with the mass centre at zg, the rotor's dynamic state reduces to exactly two quantities:

Static (force) unbalance: Us = U1 + U2 [g·mm, vector sum] Couple (moment) unbalance: Cu = U1(z1−zg) + U2(z2−zg) [g·mm·mm] Both are complex quantities — add them as vectors, not magnitudes. Two 200 g·mm unbalances 180° apart give Us = 0 and a large couple; the same two in phase give Us = 400 g·mm and, if symmetric about the mass centre, no couple at all.
The trap that costs bearings Pure couple unbalance has Us = 0, so the mass centre sits perfectly on the axis and the kinematic velocity eω is zero. The rotor is "balanced" by any single-plane measure, and a static balancing check on knife edges will pass it. It will still rock the rotor about its mass centre hard enough to destroy both bearings. Only a two-plane measurement sees it.

Two rigid-body modes

With lateral displacement x of the mass centre and tilt angle θ as the two degrees of freedom, bearing A at distance a to one side and B at distance b to the other, the stiffness matrix is

K11 = kA + kB K12 = K21 = b kB − a kA K22 = a2kA + b2kB Bearing motions follow from xA = x − aθ and xB = x + bθ.

The off-diagonal term K12 is the crux. When bkB = akA — the stiffness centre coincides with the mass centre — it vanishes and the modes separate cleanly into:

  • Translational (cylindrical / bounce) mode, ωt = √(K11/M). Both ends move in phase. Driven only by static unbalance.
  • Conical (rocking / pitching) mode, ωc = √(K22/Id). The ends move in antiphase about a node near the mass centre. Driven only by couple unbalance.

Any asymmetry — unequal bearing stiffness, or a mass centre off mid-span — couples them, so a purely static unbalance will then also excite rocking. The simulator solves the full coupled 2×2 system rather than assuming the modes separate:

| K11(1+2ζi) − Mω2 K12(1+2ζi) | | X | | Usω2 | | | | | = | | | K12(1+2ζi) K22(1+2ζi) − Jω2 | | Θ | | Cuω2 | Complex (structural) damping is used here so that the damping ratio ζ is the same at both modes; the resonant amplification is 1/2ζ, as for viscous damping. J is the effective tilt inertia — see gyroscopics below. Solving gives complex X and Θ, then xA = X − aΘ and xB = X + bΘ as phasors, so the two bearings add the two modal contributions with their correct relative phase.

This is why the two bearings almost never read the same. Near the translational critical they move together and read alike; near the conical critical they move in antiphase, and whichever bearing is further from the rocking node reads considerably higher. Watch the bearing A and B curves swap dominance across the two criticals in the sweep plot.

Gyroscopic stiffening

A spinning rotor resists tilting. For forward synchronous whirl the polar inertia Ip subtracts from the diametral inertia Id, so the effective tilt inertia is

J = Id − Ip γ = Ip / Id Cylinder estimates: Id = m(3R2 + Lr2)/12 about the mass centre, Ip = mR2/2.
  • Long, slender rotors (γ < 1): J is positive but reduced, so the conical critical is pushed up in speed. The translational critical is untouched — gyroscopics act only on the tilting mode.
  • Disc-like rotors (γ ≥ 1, Ip ≥ Id): J ≤ 0 and the forward conical critical ceases to exist — it is stiffened out to infinity and the rotor can never run through it. A thin disc on a shaft has only one synchronous critical.

Toggle the gyroscopic option in the simulator and watch the second critical move; on the default rotor it climbs from about 12,300 to 13,300 rpm. Ignoring this is a common way to mis-site an operating point that you believed was clear of resonance.

Allocating permissible unbalance between planes

ISO 1940-1 splits the total Uper between the two correction planes in inverse proportion to their distance from the mass centre, so that the resulting static unbalance stays within the grade:

Uper,1 = Uper · |z2 − zg| / |z2 − z1| Uper,2 = Uper · |zg − z1| / |z2 − z1| The standard additionally recommends that neither plane be allocated less than 30% nor more than 70% of Uper, which matters when the mass centre sits well outside the middle third of the plane spacing. The simulator applies that clamp and flags when it bites.
07

Amplitude conventions — the √2 that ruins reports

For a pure sinusoid at a single frequency the three conventions are rigidly related:

vpk = ωX vrms = vpk / √2 = 0.7071 vpk vpk-pk = 2 vpk The √2 relation holds only for a single sinusoid. For a real broadband signal, RMS must be computed from the time record or by summing spectral lines — peak/√2 will understate it.
  • ISO 1940 balance grades are stated as eω, an amplitude (zero-to-peak) quantity.
  • ISO 20816 / 10816 evaluation zones are stated in mm/s RMS, broadband 10–1000 Hz.
  • Displacement is conventionally quoted peak-to-peak (µm pk-pk) for shaft-relative measurements, adding a further factor of 2.

A G 6.3 rotor produces 6.3 mm/s peak, which is 4.45 mm/s RMS — comparing 6.3 against a 4.5 mm/s RMS zone boundary would fail a machine that actually passes.

08

09

Worked example

A 44 kg rotor carries 440 g·mm residual unbalance and runs at 6,000 rpm.

  • e = 440 / 44 = 10 µm; ω = 2π(6000)/60 = 628.3 rad/s
  • v = 0.010 mm × 628.3 = 6.28 mm/s peak → grade G 6.3, and 4.44 mm/s RMS
  • Unbalance force = 440 × 628.32 × 10−6 = 174 N rotating
  • If the critical speed is 3,500 rpm with ζ = 0.05, then r = 1.714 and the amplification is 1.51, so the deflection is 15.1 µm and the velocity 9.5 mm/s peak — 6.7 mm/s RMS, zone D, despite a perfectly ordinary balance grade.

Now split that same 440 g·mm into 220 g·mm in each plane, 180° apart, planes 280 mm apart astride the mass centre. The static unbalance is zero, the kinematic velocity is zero, and every single-plane check passes — while a couple of 220 × 140 = 30,800 g·mm·mm drives the conical mode. Switch the simulator to Model C and enter it to see what the bearings do.

10

Assumptions and limits

  • Linear, isotropic supports. Real bearings are anisotropic (different horizontal and vertical stiffness), producing elliptical orbits and split criticals.
  • Models A and B are single-DOF and cannot represent couple unbalance at all. Model C is a rigid rotor: valid below roughly 70% of the first bending critical, above which shaft flexibility must be modelled.
  • Model C includes gyroscopic effects for forward synchronous whirl only — no backward whirl, no full Campbell diagram, no cross-coupled stiffness or oil-film instability.
  • Inertia estimates assume a uniform cylinder. For a stepped or bladed rotor, override Id with a value from the CAD model or a bifilar pendulum test — the conical critical scales as 1/√Id.
  • 1× order only. Measured broadband RMS will always exceed these values, since it also carries gear mesh, bearing and blade-pass content.
  • Damping is rarely known a priori. Extract it from a coast-down: ζ ≈ Δf/(2fn) by the half-power bandwidth, or ζ ≈ 1/(2Q) from the peak amplification.
