@sub Predicting the stiffness, strength, toughness and cost of blended and coextruded PE films from datasheets, and the places where mixture rules break down
@outline Why predict film properties | Resins, additives and data | The two-level method of mixtures | Why the method fails | Results: LDPE, HDPE and CaCO₃ blends | Three-layer structures and layer ratio | Cost per kg versus cost per m² | The companion calculator and conclusions
@tnotes Blown-film converters decide film properties by blending resins in each layer and choosing the layer ratio. Usually this is done by trial on the line. This talk shows how far simple mixture rules applied to datasheet values can go in predicting film properties and cost, and, equally important, exactly where and why they fail.

B: The problem
- Converters set stiffness, strength and toughness by blending LDPE, LLDPE, mLLDPE, HDPE and additives
- Up to three coextruded layers with chosen thickness ratios
- Trials on the line are slow and expensive
- Can datasheet values plus mixture rules screen recipes before the trial?
N: A packaging film is a recipe: resins and masterbatches blended in each layer, and layers combined in a ratio. The question is whether datasheet values and simple mixture rules can predict modulus, strength, dart impact and tear well enough to screen recipes and their cost before going to the line.

T: End-member data from resin datasheets | tab1 | cols=0,1,2,3,4,5
N: The method starts from representative datasheet values for each resin class: density, gauge and blow-up ratio of the test film, and modulus, yield, break strength, dart and tear. Note that these were measured under different conditions, different BURs and gauges. That turns out to matter a lot.

F: The two-level method | fig1
- Level 1: blend inside each layer (resins + masterbatches)
- Level 2: layers combined by thickness fraction
- Density exact; other properties by the appropriate rule
N: The method works on two levels. First, the properties of each blended layer are estimated from the resins and additives in it. Second, the layer properties are combined using the layer thickness fractions. Each property gets the rule that suits its physics.

E: Level 1: mixture rules | eq1,eq2,eq4
W: Linear (Voigt) for stiffness and strength; inverse (Reuss) for weakest-link properties such as dart impact; both are special cases of the power mean M_m with m = 1 and m = −1.
N: The linear rule, a weighted average, suits stiffness and strength. The inverse rule, like springs in series, suits properties where the weakest component dominates, such as dart impact. Both are members of one family, the power mean with exponent m, which also contains the logarithmic rule as m goes to zero.

E: Excess terms and fillers | eq5,eq7,eq8
W: Pairwise excess term ΔP_AB (Redlich–Kister form) captures synergy or antagonism in tear. Filler (CaCO₃) raises modulus (Guth form) but lowers strength with volume fraction φ^(2/3).
N: Some properties are not averages at all. Tear in LDPE–LLDPE blends shows a minimum below both pure resins, so a pairwise excess term is added. Calcium carbonate filler stiffens the film but weakens it, following classical particle-composite relations in volume fraction.

E: Level 2: layers in the coextruded film | eq9,eq10
W: Modulus, strength and density by thickness-weighted average. For toughness, report bounds: lower (inverse), upper (linear) and a central (logarithmic) estimate.
N: At the film level, stiffness, strength and density follow the thickness-weighted average. For dart and tear, where layers differ greatly in toughness, the honest answer is a range: an inverse-rule lower bound, a linear upper bound and a logarithmic central estimate.

T: Confidence by property | tab3 | cols=0,1,2,3
N: This table is the summary of reliability. Density is exact. Modulus and yield are good at both levels. Break strength is fair. Dart and tear are poor to fair, because they depend on structure that composition alone does not describe.

B: Why the method of mixtures fails
- Toughness comes from tie chains, not composition
- The bubble freezes in orientation, and the blend changes it
- End-member data come from different processes (BUR, gauge)
- Fracture is a weakest-link event
- Co-crystallisation and synergy; filler dispersion and adhesion
N: Seven mechanisms explain the failures. Toughness depends on tie molecules between crystals. Orientation frozen in during blowing controls tear and its balance between machine and transverse directions, and adding LDPE changes that orientation. Datasheet values come from different processes. And fracture starts at the weakest point, not at the average.

F: LDPE in LLDPE: dart and tear | fig2
- Reported: 10 % LDPE cuts mLLDPE dart by 43 %
- Inverse rule predicts 39 %; linear rule only 9 %
- MD tear shows a minimum below both resins
N: Adding a little LDPE to LLDPE for bubble stability is common practice, and it costs toughness. The literature reports a 43 per cent dart loss at 10 per cent LDPE in metallocene LLDPE. The inverse rule predicts 39, close; the linear rule predicts only 9. So dart must be treated as a weakest-link property.

F: HDPE in LLDPE: stiffness up, toughness down | fig3
- Modulus rises linearly with HDPE
- Linear rule from datasheets predicts the wrong direction for dart
- Because HDPE datasheet dart is measured at BUR 4, high stalk, thin gauge
N: With HDPE, modulus behaves perfectly. But the linear rule using datasheet values even predicts the wrong direction for dart, because HDPE's datasheet value was measured on a high-stalk bubble at a different blow-up ratio. This is the clearest demonstration that mixture rules fail when end-member values do not describe the components as they exist in the blend.

F: Layer ratio: a strong, cheap lever | fig5
- Structure S1, 55 µm milk-pouch type film
- Core share changes dart and MD tear substantially
- Report lower, central and upper estimates
N: At the film level, changing the layer ratio is a strong and cheap lever on toughness. For a 55 micron milk-pouch film, shifting the core share moves dart and tear substantially at no extra material cost. The shaded band between inverse and linear bounds shows why a single number would be misleading.

F: Stiffness–toughness trade-off | fig6
- Structure S2, 40 µm: HDPE in the core from 0 to 50 %
- Modulus nearly doubles (209 → 386 MPa)
- Dart falls from 272 to 168 g; cost per m² −2.6 %
N: For a 40 micron laminating film, adding HDPE to the core nearly doubles stiffness while dart falls by about 40 per cent. Cost per square metre falls only 2.6 per cent, so the case for HDPE rests on stiffness, not on price.

E: Film cost | eq11,eq13,eq14
W: Cost = material + net waste + conversion (Rs/kg); material is close to 90 %. Cost per m² = cost per kg × density × thickness / 1000.
N: Film cost is material, net waste and conversion. Material is close to 90 per cent of the total, so formulation and gauge decide the cost. The final equation is the key one: the customer pays for area, so cost per square metre, which includes density and gauge, is what matters.

T: Pay more per kg to pay less per m² | tab15 | cols=0,1,4,5,6,7
N: Here is the commercial argument. Replacing C8-LLDPE in the skins with metallocene raises the cost per kilogram by only 1.5 per cent, but allows the gauge to drop from 55 to 44 microns for the same dart, so the cost per square metre falls by 18 per cent. Conversely, 30 per cent calcium carbonate masterbatch saves 18 per cent per kilogram but only 3 per cent per square metre, because density rises.

F: The companion calculator | fig8
- Offline tool: up to three layers, resin library, additives
- Properties with bounds, cost build-up per kg and per m²
- Screening tool, not a substitute for line trials
N: All of this is built into an offline calculator: choose resins and masterbatches for up to three layers, set the ratio and gauge, and it returns the predicted properties with bounds and the full cost build-up. It is meant for screening recipes before a trial, not for replacing the trial.

C: Conclusions
- Density exact; modulus and yield follow the linear rule
- Dart is weakest-link: inverse rule predicts 39 % vs reported 43 % loss
- Tear needs excess terms; orientation is beyond composition averages
- Datasheet data from different processes can reverse predicted trends
- Material ≈90 % of cost: judge per m², not per kg
FW: Next steps
- Correct each resin to actual line conditions (BUR, DDR, frost line): Part 2
- Calibrate excess terms with plant data
- Use bounds, not single values, for toughness
N: The method of mixtures is a useful, honest screening framework if each property gets the right rule and toughness is reported as a range. Its biggest limitation, that datasheet values come from different process conditions, is the subject of Part 2.
