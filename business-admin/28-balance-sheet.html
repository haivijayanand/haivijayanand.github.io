The Balance Sheet

The profit and loss statement is a film of a period; the balance sheet is a photograph on one day. It shows everything the business owns and, on the other side, who provided the money: lenders, suppliers and owners. Both sides are always equal. Pick your level.

Level 3 · For experts — a review

Classification rules, Ind AS 116 leases and items that hide outside the balance sheet.

1Current or non-current?

Ind AS 1 / Schedule III: an asset is current if expected to be realised, sold or consumed in the normal operating cycle, held for trading, expected to be realised within 12 months, or is unrestricted cash. A liability is current if expected to be settled in the operating cycle, held for trading, due within 12 months, or the entity does not have a right at the reporting date to defer settlement for at least 12 months. Covenant breaches before year-end can make a long-term loan current even if the lender has not demanded repayment. Where the operating cycle is not identifiable, it is assumed to be 12 months.

Schedule III Division II order: Assets (non-current: PPE, CWIP, investment property, goodwill, other intangibles, financial assets, deferred tax assets; current: inventories, financial assets — investments, trade receivables, cash, bank balances, loans — other current assets), then Equity (share capital, other equity) and Liabilities (non-current, then current).

2Ind AS 116: leases come on balance sheet

Capitalise a shop lease

Lessees recognise a right-of-use (ROU) asset and a lease liability for almost all leases (exemptions: short-term ≤12 months, low-value). Rent disappears from operating expenses; depreciation and interest replace it. EBITDA rises, debt rises, and expense is front-loaded, which changes ratios (Part D).

3What the balance sheet does not show

Internally generated brands, customer relationships and know-how; most contingent liabilities (notes only); guarantees given for group companies (disclosed); purchase commitments; and the value of human capital. Book equity is therefore a floor-ish anchor, not a valuation: the price-to-book ratio (Chapter 38) measures how much the market believes these unrecognised assets are worth.
