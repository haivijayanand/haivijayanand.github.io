How to read a pocket colour wheel — definitions
Primary colours
Red, yellow and blue. On the painter's wheel they cannot be mixed from other colours.
Secondary colours
Orange, green and violet, halfway between two primaries and mixed from them (red + yellow = orange, yellow + blue = green, blue + red = violet).
Tertiary colours
The six colours between a primary and a secondary, mixed from the two: yellow-orange, red-orange, red-violet, blue-violet, blue-green, yellow-green.
Warm colours
Reds, oranges and yellows (this half of the wheel). They tend to come forward.
Cool colours
Greens, blues and violets. They tend to recede. Each paint also has its own warm or cool bias.
Complementary
Two colours directly opposite. Side by side they make each other vivid; mixed together they neutralise to a grey or brown.
Split complementary
A colour and the two colours on either side of its complement (blue with yellow-orange and red-orange).
Triad
Three colours evenly spaced — the three primaries, or the three secondaries.
Tetrad
Four colours made of two complementary pairs (for example yellow and violet with red and green).
Square
Four colours evenly spaced around the wheel.
Analogous
Two to four neighbours on the same side of the wheel, such as yellow, yellow-green, green and blue-green. Usually calm and soothing.
Monochromatic
One hue only, with its tints, tones and shades.
Tint
The pure colour with white added — lighter (red becomes pink).
Tone
The pure colour with grey added — less bright, more neutral.
Shade
The pure colour with black added — darker and less saturated.
Value
How light or dark a colour is, regardless of hue. A painting with a wide range of values looks less flat.
