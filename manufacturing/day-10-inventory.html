Inventory Management: EOQ, Safety Stock and ABC

Stock is money on the floor. Why we hold it, what it costs, how much to order (EOQ), when to order (reorder point and safety stock), and where to focus (ABC and XYZ).

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: EOQ and reorder point❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • List the purposes and costs of inventory
  • Calculate EOQ and the total annual cost of ordering and holding
  • Set safety stock and reorder point for a service level
  • Classify items by ABC (value) and XYZ (variability)
  • Calculate inventory turns and days of inventory

2Why hold stock, and what it costs

ReasonsCover lead time; absorb variation in demand and supply; allow economic batches; buy ahead of price rises; decouple processes.
Holding cost (20–30 % of value per year)Interest on money, storage space, handling, insurance, obsolescence (artwork changes!), damage and ageing (films, adhesives with shelf life).
Ordering / set-up costPurchase paperwork, freight, inspection — or for made items, the changeover cost.
Inventory turns = annual cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory value Days of inventory = 365 ÷ turns

3How much to order: EOQ

EOQ = √(2·D·S ÷ H) D annual demand, S cost per order, H holding cost per unit per year Total cost = (D ÷ Q)·S + (Q ÷ 2)·H

The total-cost curve is flat near the EOQ: ordering 30 % more or less than EOQ raises cost only a few percent. So round EOQ to a convenient pack or truck load. The real power of the formula is what it says about set-up: cut S (Day 11) and the economic batch falls with its square root.

4When to order: reorder point and safety stock

Reorder point = d̄·L + SS Safety stock SS = z·σ_d·√L (d̄ average daily demand, L lead time in days, σ_d daily demand standard deviation, z from service level)

5Inventory in flexible packaging

Raw, WIP and finished — and the special problemsRaw material: films (PET, BOPP, CPP, Al foil, paper) by grade, thickness and width; resins and masterbatches; inks (with Pantone shades) and solvents; adhesives with shelf life. Too many widths multiply stock — standardise mother-roll widths and slit. WIP: printed and laminated rolls waiting for cure, slitting or pouching — often the largest pile. Finished goods: customer-specific and printed, so obsolete stock appears overnight when artwork changes; agree with customers who owns the stock of their printed material. Also watch leftover ink: reuse returned ink with colour correction, or it becomes hazardous waste.

6Worked example

Adhesive for the laminatorAnnual use D = 60 000 kg; cost per order S = ₹4 000 (freight + handling + QC); price ₹320/kg; holding 25 % → H = ₹80/kg/yr EOQ = √(2 × 60 000 × 4 000 / 80) = 2 449 kg → order 2 400 kg (two pallets of 1 200 kg) Daily use 200 kg (300 days), σ_d = 50 kg, lead time 10 days, 98 % service (z 2.05) SS = 2.05 × 50 × √10 = 324 kg → ROP = 200 × 10 + 324 = 2 324 kg Shelf life 6 months → 2 400 kg lasts 12 days — fine.

7Setting inventory policy

  1. Do an ABC analysis by annual value: A ≈ 20 % of items, 80 % of value — control tightly.
  2. Add XYZ by demand variability: X steady, Y variable, Z lumpy. AX items can run lean; CZ items may be made only to order.
  3. For A and B items set EOQ (or pack size) and ROP with a chosen service level.
  4. For C items use simple two-bin or periodic review with generous stock — they are cheap.
  5. Measure turns and slow / obsolete stock monthly; clear what has not moved in 180 days.
  6. Review supplier lead times twice a year; they drift.

9Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Average stock is Q/2 + SS — the safety stock is always there; size it carefully.
  • 99.9 % service on every item is ruinously expensive; reserve it for items that stop the line.
  • Count a sample of items every week (cycle counting) instead of a year-end stock take.
  • Managers: inventory hides problems — long changeovers, unreliable suppliers, poor forecasts. Lower it slowly and fix what appears.
