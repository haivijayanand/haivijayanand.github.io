Value Streams, Takt Time and Lead Time

Following one order from raw material to the customer: takt time, cycle time, lead time, value-added time, and value stream mapping — the one-page picture that shows where the days are lost.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: Value stream timeline❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Calculate takt time from demand and available time
  • Tell cycle time, lead time and value-added time apart
  • Draw a current-state value stream map with process boxes, inventory triangles and a timeline
  • Find the value-added ratio and the biggest waiting points
  • Sketch a future state that cuts lead time

2Takt, cycle and lead time

Takt time = available production time ÷ customer demand in that time
TermMeaningExample
Takt timeThe pace the customer needs (from German Takt, beat)7.5 h × 3 shifts = 81 000 s ÷ 900 000 pouches/day = 0.09 s per pouch
Cycle timeHow often a process actually completes one unitPouch machine at 120 pouches/min → 0.5 s per pouch per machine
Lead timeTime for one unit to go from raw material to dispatch18 days from film roll to pouch carton
Value-added timeTime spent actually transforming the productAbout 2 hours of printing, laminating, curing… out of 18 days

If cycle time is longer than takt, you cannot meet demand — add machines, shifts or speed. If it is much shorter, you overproduce or sit idle. For the pouch example, 0.09 s takt against 0.5 s per machine means about six machines must run in parallel.

3Value stream mapping

A value stream is every step, value-adding or not, that brings a product family from supplier to customer, plus the information that triggers each step. A value stream map (VSM) draws it on one page.

Process boxesOne per step, with a data box: cycle time, changeover time, uptime, operators, shifts, batch size, scrap %.
Inventory trianglesMaterial waiting between steps, converted to days of demand: quantity ÷ daily demand.
Information flowHow orders reach each step — schedule pushed by planning, or a pull signal from downstream.
TimelineA saw-tooth line at the bottom: waiting days on the top, processing time on the bottom. Total the two.
Value-added ratio = total processing (value-added) time ÷ total lead time

In most batch plants the ratio is well under 1 %. That is not a scandal; it is the opportunity. The lead time is almost all waiting, and waiting is cut by smaller batches, quicker changeovers, less WIP and better scheduling — not by running machines faster.

4Flexible packaging value stream

Printed laminated pouch — current stateFilm from supplier waits 6 days in the store → gravure print (job of 3 t, 4 h) → waits 3 days → solvent-less lamination (2 h) → cures 2 days (this wait is part of the process — adhesive needs it) → waits 2 days → slitting (1.5 h) → waits 3 days → pouch making (14 h for the order) → waits 2 days in finished goods → dispatch. Lead time ≈ 18 days; processing ≈ 22 h. The cure time is necessary non-value-added time; the other waits are queues created by batch sizes and schedules.

In a machining plant the same map shows bar stock → cutting → turning → heat treatment (sent out) → grinding → assembly, and the outside heat-treatment loop is usually the biggest wait.

5Worked example

Takt and lead timeDemand: 12 t of laminate per day. Available: 3 shifts × 7.5 h = 22.5 h. Takt = 22.5 h ÷ 12 t = 1.875 h per tonne (112 min/t). Printing: 200 m/min × 1 m web × 45 g/m² printed web = 540 kg/h → 111 min/t → just meets takt, no spare. Inventory before lamination: 9 t ÷ 12 t/day = 0.75 day. Before slitting: 30 t = 2.5 days. Total waits 16 days + processing 0.9 day → lead time ≈ 17 days, VA ratio ≈ 5 %.

6How to map a value stream

  1. Pick one product family (same route, similar times). Do not map the whole plant.
  2. Walk the route from dispatch back to receiving, with a stopwatch and a notebook — not from the ERP.
  3. Record each process's data box and count the inventory in front of it.
  4. Convert every inventory to days of demand and draw the timeline.
  5. Draw the information flow: who tells each step what to make next?
  6. Total lead time and processing time; mark the biggest waits with "kaizen bursts".
  7. Draw the future state: where can flow be continuous, where do you need a supermarket (Day 12), where is the pacemaker?

7Lab: value stream timeline

Edit the steps of the stream: processing time per order and waiting days before each step. The timeline, lead time and value-added ratio update. Try halving the batch-driven waits.

8Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Walk it, don't download it. System data hides re-work loops, hold areas and "temporary" stores.
  • Separate necessary waits (adhesive cure, ink drying, heat-treatment soak) from queue waits. Only the second kind is pure waste.
  • Takt is a planning number, not a target to beat — running faster than takt just builds inventory.
  • Managers: lead time is a cash number. 17 days of WIP at ₹2 crore per day of sales is working capital you are financing.
