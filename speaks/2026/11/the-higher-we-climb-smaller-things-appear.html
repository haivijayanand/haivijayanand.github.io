The Higher We Climb, the Smaller Things Appear

2 Nov 20267 min readSelf-GrowthPhilosophy

How age, experience, and maturity change the way we judge success, people, and life's challenges.

1. The Changing View of Life

When we are in our twenties, life appears to be a race.

We want to secure admission into a prestigious institution, obtain a good job, establish ourselves financially, and earn recognition in society.

Everything seems important. Every examination, interview, promotion, and opportunity appears capable of determining our future.

During our thirties, the race continues, but the priorities change. Career progression, family responsibilities, financial commitments, children, and social expectations begin occupying much of our attention.

Then, somewhere in our forties, something interesting happens.

We begin looking at the same world differently.

Things that once appeared enormous no longer seem quite so significant. Distinctions that once mattered greatly begin losing their importance. Our definition of success becomes broader, and our judgement of people becomes more nuanced.

What has changed?

The world has not necessarily changed.

We have changed.

2. The Mountains That Once Frightened Us

Consider how we viewed educational institutions during our student days.

Admission into a premier institution was considered a major achievement. We divided colleges into first-tier, second-tier, and third-tier categories, often assuming that the institution would determine the student's future.

The name of the college seemed almost as important as the individual studying there.

But after twenty years of professional experience, we begin noticing something different.

We encounter extraordinary individuals from relatively unknown institutions. We also encounter graduates of prestigious institutions whose professional contributions do not always match the expectations associated with their academic backgrounds.

Over time, we realise that institutional reputation is only one part of a much larger equation.

A good college certainly provides advantages: better infrastructure, academic exposure, peer groups, and professional networks.

But it cannot manufacture curiosity, commitment, integrity, perseverance, or a lifelong desire to learn.

A prestigious institution may provide a head start, but an individual's attitude determines much of the journey that follows.

Today, when I look at a student, the distinction between a Class I and Class II college no longer appears as decisive as it once did.

I am more interested in whether the student is curious, capable, disciplined, and willing to work hard.

My attention has gradually shifted from the institution to the individual.

3. From Labels to Capabilities

The same transformation occurs in professional life.

Early in our careers, we are naturally impressed by designations, qualifications, institutional affiliations, and positions of authority.

A senior scientist, a director, a professor, or a successful entrepreneur may appear almost extraordinary to a young professional.

But as we gain experience and interact with people across different levels, we begin recognising a fundamental difference between position and capability.

A designation indicates responsibility and authority. It does not automatically establish wisdom, competence, or character.

Similarly, a relatively junior employee may possess remarkable insight, originality, or technical ability.

We gradually learn to look beyond external identifiers.

Instead of asking, "What position does this person hold?" we begin asking, "What does this person understand, contribute, and stand for?"

Instead of being impressed merely by someone's educational qualifications, we become interested in how effectively that knowledge is applied.

Our judgement begins moving from reputation towards substance.

Perhaps maturity is the gradual replacement of labels with understanding.

4. The Paradox of Experience

Experience produces another interesting phenomenon.

Problems that once consumed our thoughts become relatively simple.

A young engineer may spend several days struggling to understand a technical problem that an experienced engineer can identify within minutes.

A student may be deeply anxious about selecting a college course. An experienced professional may see several viable alternatives and understand that no single decision necessarily determines an entire career.

A young employee may consider a delayed promotion a major setback. A senior colleague may recognise that a professional journey unfolds across decades, not merely through individual promotions.

Experience gives us a wider time horizon.

We begin recognising patterns, distinguishing temporary difficulties from lasting consequences, and understanding that many apparent failures are recoverable.

However, there is a subtle danger here.

Because something has become easy for us, we may begin assuming that it should be easy for everyone.

We forget the uncertainty we once experienced.

The problem has become smaller in our minds, but it remains just as large for the person confronting it for the first time.

Maturity is not simply recognising that a problem is small. It is remembering why that problem once appeared enormous.

This distinction separates wisdom from arrogance.

5. Does Age Automatically Bring Wisdom?

Growing older certainly gives us more experiences, but experience alone does not guarantee wisdom.

Two individuals may spend twenty years in the same profession and emerge with entirely different perspectives.

One may become more curious, patient, adaptable, and open-minded.

Another may become rigid, dismissive, and convinced that his past experiences represent universal truth.

The difference lies in reflection.

Experience becomes wisdom only when we examine what happened, question our assumptions, and remain willing to revise our understanding.

For instance, observing successful professionals from ordinary colleges should challenge our assumptions about educational prestige.

But it should not lead us to conclude that all colleges provide identical opportunities.

Similarly, discovering that designations do not always reflect competence should not make us dismiss qualifications or institutional achievements altogether.

Wisdom requires balance.

It is the ability to appreciate genuine differences without exaggerating their significance.

The purpose of experience is not to make us certain about everything. It is to help us recognise where our certainty may be misplaced.

6. When Success Changes Its Meaning

Our understanding of success also evolves.

At twenty, securing a place in a reputed college may be our greatest ambition.

At thirty, professional advancement and financial stability may dominate our thinking.

In our forties, having achieved some measure of stability, we may begin asking different questions.

Am I still learning?

Does my work have meaning?

Am I using my capabilities fully?

What kind of relationships have I built?

What influence am I having on people around me?

The external measures of success do not necessarily become irrelevant. Financial security, professional recognition, and social standing continue to matter.

But they gradually cease to be the only measures.

We discover that a person with a modest designation may lead a deeply satisfying life, while someone occupying an important position may remain perpetually dissatisfied.

We begin distinguishing achievement from fulfilment.

And perhaps, for the first time, we seriously question whether climbing higher always means moving forward.

7. The View from a Higher Mountain

Climbing a mountain offers a useful analogy.

Standing at the bottom, the summit appears enormous. We see the steep slope, the obstacles, and the distance ahead.

Our entire attention is directed towards reaching the top.

But as we climb, our field of vision expands.

Obstacles that once blocked our view become small features in a much larger landscape.

We discover other mountains, alternative paths, and possibilities that were invisible from below.

Life behaves similarly.

The higher we climb through education, professional experience, and personal growth, the broader our perspective can become.

But there is a responsibility that accompanies this improved view.

We must not confuse a broader perspective with personal superiority.

The person standing below is not necessarily less capable. That person simply occupies a different point in the journey.

And some people beginning their climb today may eventually reach heights far beyond our own.

8. A New Definition of Growth

Perhaps the most important change in our forties is not that we have acquired more knowledge, money, or status.

It is that we have begun understanding the limitations of the very measures we once considered absolute.

We recognise that talent is not confined to prestigious institutions.

That intelligence is not monopolised by people holding impressive qualifications.

That success is not guaranteed by a strong beginning.

That failure is not necessarily permanent.

And that every individual must ultimately be understood through his or her own capabilities, choices, and conduct.

This does not mean we have discovered the final truth about life.

It means we have become better equipped to question our earlier conclusions.

The higher we climb, the smaller some things appear. But if we are truly growing, our understanding of people should become larger.

Perhaps that is one of the finest rewards of maturity.

Not the ability to look down upon the world from a greater height, but the ability to see it with greater clarity.

“The higher we climb, the smaller some things appear. But if we are truly growing, our understanding of people should become larger.”
