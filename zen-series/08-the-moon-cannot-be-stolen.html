The Moon Cannot
Be Stolen

Small stories, still minds. A short Zen tale each time, with a thought to carry into the day.

The Story

The hermit-poet Ryokan lived the simplest of lives in a small hut at the foot of a mountain. One evening, while he was out, a thief crept in — only to find there was nothing worth stealing.

Ryokan came back and caught him there. “You have come a long way to visit me,” he said kindly, “and you should not go home with empty hands. Please — take my clothes as a gift.”

The bewildered thief grabbed the clothes and slunk off into the night.

Ryokan sat down, naked, at his doorway and gazed up at the full moon rising over the mountain.

“Poor fellow. I wish I could have given him this beautiful moon.”

Reflection

The thief went away with a bundle of old clothes and thought he had won. Ryokan was left with the moon, the night air and a mind entirely at peace. Who was really the poorer of the two?

The things that most enrich a life cannot be broken into and carried off. And Ryokan’s reply holds a second lesson: he met the thief not with fear or fury but with pity — for a man so busy grabbing that he could not see what was shining right above him.

Carry this

Hold lightly what can be taken. Treasure what cannot.
