Friction Clutches

A clutch connects and disconnects a driven machine from its driver while running. Learn single- and multi-plate and cone clutches, the uniform-pressure and uniform-wear theories, how to size a clutch for torque, and the friction materials, heat and cost that decide its life.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: Plate and cone clutch❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Derive torque capacity of a plate clutch by uniform pressure and uniform wear
  • Know why worn clutches are designed on uniform wear
  • Find the best inner/outer radius ratio
  • Design cone and multi-plate clutches
  • Choose friction materials and estimate heat per engagement

2Types

Single-plateCars, tractors. One plate, two friction faces, spring-loaded.
Multi-plateMotorcycles, automatic gearboxes, machine tools — compact, often oil-cooled (wet).
ConeWedge action multiplies the axial force; synchronisers in gearboxes.
CentrifugalEngages automatically with speed: mopeds, chainsaws.
ElectromagneticRemote switching: packaging machines, AC compressors.
Overrunning (sprag/roller)Drives one way only: starters, backstops on conveyors.

3Torque capacity

For an annular face, inner radius Ri, outer Ro, axial force W, friction μ, n friction surfaces:

Uniform pressure (new clutch): W = π·p·(Ro² − Ri²) T = n·(2/3)·μ·W·(Ro³ − Ri³)/(Ro² − Ri²)
Uniform wear (worn-in): p·r = constant; W = 2π·pa·Ri·(Ro − Ri) T = n·μ·W·(Ro + Ri)/2
Cone clutch: divide T by sin α (semi-cone angle); engaging force is larger than W while sliding

Wear is proportional to pressure × sliding velocity. Outer parts slide faster, wear faster, and lose pressure until p·r is constant. A clutch spends most of its life worn-in, so design on uniform wear — it gives the lower (safe) torque. The maximum pressure is then at the inner radius.

Best proportion: for a given Ro and allowable pa, uniform-wear torque is maximum at Ri = Ro/√3 ≈ 0.577 Ro. Practical clutches use Ri/Ro ≈ 0.55–0.7.

4Heat

During engagement the plates slip until the driven side reaches the driver's speed. The energy dissipated equals the kinetic energy gained by the load (for a load starting from rest, about the same again is lost as heat).

Heat per engagement ≈ ½·I·ω² (load accelerated from rest) Temperature rise ΔT = E / (m·c)
Check the average power: heat per engagement × engagements per hour ≤ what the housing can dissipate

5Friction materials and cost

The mating surface is cast iron or steel. Wet clutches have low μ, so they need more plates, but run cooler and last much longer. Lining replacement is the main running cost; specify a generous area to cut wear rate (wear ∝ energy per unit area).

6Worked example

Single-plate clutch, 2 faces, Do = 300, Di = 180 mm, μ = 0.3, pa = 0.35 MPaUniform wear: W = 2π × 0.35 × 90 × (150 − 90) = 11.88 kN T = 2 × 0.3 × 11 880 × (150 + 90)/2 = 855 N·m → at 1440 rpm: 129 kW Uniform pressure (new): W = 15.83 kN, T = 1164 N·m Ri/Ro = 0.6 — close to the 0.577 optimum.

7Design procedure

  1. Find the design torque: engine/motor torque × service factor (1.3–2.0), plus a margin so the clutch slips before the driveline breaks.
  2. Choose friction material: μ and allowable pa.
  3. Choose Ro from the space; take Ri ≈ 0.6 Ro.
  4. Find the number of friction surfaces needed by uniform wear.
  5. Find the spring force W; choose springs (Day 16) or diaphragm spring.
  6. Check heat per engagement and per hour; temperature rise of the plates.

9Pro tips and common mistakes

  • A clutch that slips under normal load glazes and dies quickly. Size with margin, but not so much that it never protects the driveline.
  • Cone angles below about 12° can jam (self-lock) — keep α above the friction angle.
  • Oil on a dry lining drops μ by half — seal well.
  • Multi-plate clutches lose some force to spline friction; inner plates see less pressure.
