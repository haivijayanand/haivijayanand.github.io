@sub The workhorse sealant web of laminated flexible packaging: history, process windows, station temperatures, properties, additives, trends and troubleshooting
@outline What the sealant web must do | Nine decades of history | Process: BUR, DDR and windows | Temperature profile station by station | Specification and performance trend | Additives and enhancements | Current trends | Troubleshooting and conclusions
@tnotes In almost every laminated pouch, the inside web is a polyethylene blown film. It seals the pack, touches the product and provides most of the toughness. This talk covers how that film evolved from thick monolayer LDPE to thin multilayer metallocene structures, how to run it, how to specify it, and how to trace lamination defects back to the film line.

B: Four jobs of the sealant web
- **Bond:** stable surface energy ≥ 40 dyn/cm; no migrating additives at the interface
- **Seal:** low seal initiation, high hot tack, seal through contamination
- **Survive:** puncture, flex-crack and drop resistance in the filled pack
- **Run:** controlled COF, flatness, gauge uniformity, low gels on laminator and FFS
- LDPE does bond and run well; LLDPE and mPE add seal and survive
N: In a laminate such as PET 12 microns over PE 40 to 100, the outer web carries print, stiffness and barrier, and the PE film must do four jobs at once: bond to the adhesive, seal, survive the filled pack's abuse and run smoothly on the machines. LDPE alone is good at bonding and running; modern films add LLDPE and metallocene PE for sealing and toughness.

T: Nine decades of development (1 of 2) | tab1 | rows=0-4
N: The history begins with ICI's high-pressure polyethylene in 1933, used first for radar cable insulation during the war. The tubular blown-film process was adopted for LDPE in the late 1940s and 1950s, Ziegler and Phillips catalysts brought HDPE, and in the 1970s BOPP and BOPET films with polyurethane adhesive lamination created the modern laminate. The 1980s brought three-layer co-extrusion and LDPE–LLDPE blends with hexene and octene comonomers.

T: Nine decades of development (2 of 2) | tab1 | rows=5-9
N: From 1991, single-site metallocene PE and plastomers lowered seal initiation and raised toughness dramatically. The 2000s brought five- to seven-layer lines with internal bubble cooling and automatic gauge control, the 2010s nine to eleven layers and machine-direction-oriented PE, and the 2020s design-for-recycling guidelines and extended producer responsibility.

B: The blown-film process: key ratios
- BUR = bubble diameter / die diameter
- Layflat width = π · D_die · BUR / 2 ≈ 1.571 · D_die · BUR
- DDR = die gap / (film thickness × BUR)
- Example: 250 mm die, BUR 2.5 → 625 mm bubble, 982 mm layflat; 1.0 mm gap, 50 µm → DDR 8
N: Three ratios define the bubble. Blow-up ratio sets transverse stretch and layflat width. Draw-down ratio sets machine-direction stretch. In the example, a 250 millimetre die at BUR 2.5 gives a 982 millimetre layflat, and a 1 millimetre gap with 50 micron film gives a draw-down of 8. Balanced orientation between the two directions is what gives balanced tear and flat film.

T: Typical process windows | tab2
N: For lamination film, a BUR of 2.0 to 3.0, a frost line at 2 to 4 die diameters and moderate draw-down give balanced, flat, low-gel film. The right-hand column tells the operator what happens when each parameter is raised, which is the basis of troubleshooting.

T: Temperature profile: extruder stations | tab3 | rows=0-7 | cols=0,1,2,3
N: Temperatures are given station by station, from the feed throat through the barrel zones, screen changer, adapter and die. LDPE runs at about 190 to 215 degrees melt; LLDPE and metallocene-rich layers run hotter because of their higher viscosity. The feed throat is kept cool to avoid bridging.

T: Temperature profile: die and cooling | tab3 | rows=8-15 | cols=0,1,2,3
N: Downstream of the die, the air ring, internal bubble cooling and nip conditions complete the profile. Too hot a die gives gels and poor bubble stability; too cold gives melt fracture. The notes column explains the purpose of each setting.

T: Typical specification for a lamination web | tab4 | cols=0,1,2,3
N: A lamination sealant is specified by gauge tolerance, surface energy after treatment, coefficient of friction, gels, haze, dart, tear, seal initiation and hot tack. The last column explains why each matters in lamination, for example surface energy for bond strength and COF for running on form-fill-seal machines.

T: How sealant performance has improved | tab5
N: The trend is striking. From thick monolayer LDPE in the 1970s to today's multilayer metallocene structures, seal initiation has fallen by 20 to 30 degrees, dart at equal gauge has risen several times, and the sealant gauge in a PET//PE pack has roughly halved.

2: Metallocene PE and LDPE as a blend partner
L: mLLDPE and plastomers
- Narrow MWD, even comonomer spread
- Dart 2–5× Ziegler LLDPE; seal initiation −10 to −30 °C
- 20–60 % in core and sealant; 10–40 % plastomer in skin
- Penalty: low melt strength, high motor load
R: LDPE as the additive
- 5–30 % LDPE raises melt strength and bubble stability
- Smoother surface, lower haze, higher BUR and output
- Above 30–40 % LDPE, dart and tear fall sharply
N: Single-site catalysts give polyethylene with a narrow molecular-weight distribution and evenly spread comonomer. That removes the sticky low-molecular-weight fraction and the high-melting fraction, so sealing starts lower and toughness is much higher. Their weakness is low melt strength, and the remedy is LDPE: today LDPE is often the 10 to 30 per cent additive rather than the base resin.

T: Fillers and pigments | tab6
N: Fillers such as calcium carbonate, talc and titanium dioxide reduce cost or add opacity, but each has a penalty: lower toughness, die build-up, or problems at the lamination interface. The caution column is important: fillers must be kept out of the treated surface layer.

T: Surface and process additives | tab7 | cols=0,1,2,3
N: Slip, antiblock, antistat, antifog, processing aids and antioxidants each have a function and a caution. The key rule for lamination is to place additives by layer: migrating slip and antistat must stay away from the treated bonding surface, or the bond strength will fall as they bloom.

B: Toughness, barrier and stiffness by structure
- C6 and C8 LLDPE markedly tougher than C4 at the same density
- EVA (4–18 % VA) and ionomers for low seal initiation and seal through contamination
- EVOH or PA core with tie layers for oxygen barrier
- HDPE core or MDO-PE skin for stiffness; balance asymmetry to avoid curl
- mLLDPE + HDPE core lets 30–40 µm replace 60–80 µm
N: Beyond additives, properties are designed by structure. Hexene and octene LLDPE are tougher than butene grades. EVA and ionomers improve sealing, ionomers especially through grease and powder contamination. A thin EVOH core gives oxygen barrier while staying recyclable. And a stiff HDPE core between tough metallocene skins allows a 30 to 40 micron web to replace a 60 to 80 micron film.

B: Current trends
- All-PE laminates: MDO-PE or BOPE replaces PET or BOPP outer web
- Design for recycling (CEFLEX D4ACE, Sept 2025): mono-PE, EVOH below ≈5 %
- PFAS-free processing aids replacing fluoropolymer PPAs
- Recycled content in core layers with virgin skins
- More layers, thinner film: 30–40 µm replacing 60–80 µm
N: The PE sealant is becoming the whole laminate. Machine-direction-oriented PE replaces PET as the print web so the pack can be recycled as polyethylene. Design-for-recycling guidelines limit barrier layers and adhesives. Fluoropolymer processing aids are being replaced by PFAS-free grades. And recycled resin is buried in the core between clean virgin skins.

T: Troubleshooting lamination defects traced to the film | tab8
N: Finally, a troubleshooting table. Low bond strength, tunnelling, curl, poor seals, gels and wrinkles in the laminate are traced to their likely cause at the film line, such as additive bloom, low treatment, unbalanced orientation or gauge variation, with the remedy for each.

C: Conclusions
- LDPE remains essential, now mainly as the 10–30 % melt-strength component
- BUR 2.0–3.0, frost line 2–4 die Ø, LDPE melt 190–215 °C
- mPE and plastomers: seal initiation −20 to −30 °C; sealant gauge roughly halved
- Place additives by layer; keep slip, antistat and fillers off the treated side
FW: Looking ahead
- All-PE recyclable laminates with MDO-PE
- PFAS-free processing aids
- PCR in core layers
N: LDPE is still essential in lamination film, but as a partner rather than the base. Values given are typical guidance and should be confirmed against resin supplier data sheets and line trials.
