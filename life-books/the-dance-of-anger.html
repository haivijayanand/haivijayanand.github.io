The Dance of Anger

Harriet Lerner · first published 1985 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Anger is a useful signal that something in our relationships needs to change, and the lasting change comes not from blaming or silencing ourselves but from calmly clarifying and changing our own part in the pattern.

Summary

Harriet Lerner, a psychologist and family therapist, wrote this book mainly for women who had been taught that anger is unattractive or dangerous, though its lessons fit anyone. Her starting point is that anger is not good or bad. It is a signal, telling us that we are being hurt, that our needs or values are being ignored, or that we are giving up too much of ourselves.

Lerner describes two common ways of handling anger that both fail. Some people swallow it, keep the peace and quietly lose touch with what they want. Others vent it through blaming, criticising and repeated fights. Venting feels active, yet it rarely changes anything, because the same argument simply plays out again and again.

Her central image is the dance. Relationships run in circular patterns, such as one partner pursuing while the other withdraws, or one person doing too much while another does too little. Each step invites the other's step. We cannot make someone else change, but if we change our own step, the whole dance has to shift.

Doing this means speaking clearly and calmly about ourselves rather than diagnosing the other person, and taking a position we can actually hold. Lerner warns that when we change, others often push back and try to pull us into the old pattern. Staying steady through that reaction, without attacking, is the real test.

She also shows how tension slides sideways into triangles, as when an unhappy couple focuses all its worry on a child, or a grievance with one relative is aired to another. And she traces how patterns learned in our first family repeat in our adult homes, so understanding those roots helps us respond more freely.

The overall message is hopeful and practical: go slowly, take small steps, and focus on the part you can change, which is your own.

309 words

One-line takeaway: This week, pick one recurring argument at home, notice the step you usually take in it, and try one small, calm change to your own response instead of trying to change the other person.

  1. How does Lerner view anger itself?

    Answer C. Lerner treats anger as a useful signal pointing to hurt, ignored needs or giving up too much of oneself.

  2. Why does Lerner consider venting an ineffective way to handle anger?

    Answer A. Blaming and repeated fights feel active but tend to keep the old pattern going.

  3. What is a cost of always swallowing anger to keep the peace?

    Answer D. Silencing anger can mean slowly giving up parts of oneself to avoid conflict.

  4. What does the 'dance' in the title refer to?

    Answer B. Lerner pictures relationships as repeating circular patterns that both people keep in motion.

  5. According to Lerner, what is the most reliable way to change a stuck pattern?

    Answer C. We cannot control others, but changing our own move forces the whole dance to shift.

  6. Which is an example of a circular pattern Lerner describes?

    Answer B. The pursue-and-withdraw cycle is a classic dance where each step provokes the other.

  7. What often happens when one person begins to change their part in a relationship?

    Answer D. Lerner warns that change usually meets resistance, and holding steady through it is the real test.

  8. How does Lerner recommend speaking when angry?

    Answer A. She favours clear, calm statements about one's own feelings and position rather than blaming the other.

  9. What is a triangle in Lerner's sense?

    Answer C. Triangles form when anxiety between two people is diverted onto or through a third person.

  10. Why does Lerner encourage looking at one's first family?

    Answer B. Understanding inherited patterns helps people respond more freely in their present relationships.
