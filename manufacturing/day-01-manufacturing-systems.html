Manufacturing Systems and Process Types

What a manufacturing system is, the five process types from job shop to continuous flow, the product–process matrix that matches a process to its volume and variety, and how a flexible packaging plant mixes several types under one roof.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: Product–process matrix❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Describe a factory as a system of inputs, transformation and outputs
  • Tell apart project, job shop, batch, line and continuous processes
  • Place a product on the product–process matrix and spot a mismatch
  • Explain make-to-stock, make-to-order and engineer-to-order
  • Know the four things every factory competes on: cost, quality, delivery and flexibility

2The factory as a system

Every factory takes inputs — materials, energy, people, machines, information and money — and transforms them into products a customer pays for. The transformation creates value only if the customer would pay more for the output than the inputs cost. Everything in this course — planning, quality, maintenance, safety, cost — is about running that transformation reliably, safely and at the lowest total cost.

Engineer's viewMachines, processes, settings, tolerances, cycle times. "Can we make it, and make it right?"
Manager's viewCapacity, cost, cash, delivery, people, customers. "Can we make it on time and at a profit — every day?"
The customer's viewRight product, right quality, right quantity, right day, right price. Nothing else counts.

The best plants are run by people who hold both views at once. A process engineer who knows the machine-hour rate makes better decisions; a manager who knows why a laminate delaminates sets better priorities.

3Five process types

TypeVolume / varietyFlowExamples
ProjectOne-off, uniqueResources come to the productBuilding a plant, a ship, installing a gravure press
Job shopVery low volume, very high varietyJumbled — each job its own routeTool room, cylinder engraving, maintenance workshop, prototype shop
BatchLow to medium volume, medium varietyDisconnected, in lotsPrinting jobs, pouch making, machining families of parts, pharma granulation
Line (repetitive)High volume, low varietyConnected, pacedCar and two-wheeler assembly, bottling, a dedicated sachet line
ContinuousVery high volume, commodityContinuous, 24 × 7Refinery, paper mill, blown-film extrusion, polymer plant
The product–process matrix (Hayes and Wheelwright). Plot product volume and standardisation on one axis and process type on the other. Successful plants sit on the diagonal: low volume and high variety with flexible job shops, high volume and low variety with dedicated lines. Off the diagonal you pay for it — a flexible process making a commodity costs too much; a rigid line making many variants spends its life changing over.

4How the order meets the factory

StrategyWhat is heldLead time to customerExample
Make-to-stock (MTS)Finished goodsShortestStandard carry bags, stretch film, bolts
Assemble / finish-to-order (ATO)Semi-finishedShortPlain film in stock, printed to order; PCs configured to order
Make-to-order (MTO)Raw materialsMediumPrinted laminated pouches for a brand, custom gears
Engineer-to-order (ETO)Nothing specificLongestSpecial-purpose machines, test rigs, a new printing press

The point where the customer order enters is the order decoupling point. Upstream of it you plan to forecast; downstream you work to firm orders. Moving it is a strategic decision: holding plain film lets a converter promise ten days instead of twenty-five.

5A flexible packaging plant

Running example: one plant, several process typesA typical converter makes printed laminated pouches and rolls for food, FMCG and pharma customers. Blown-film extrusion runs nearly continuously (LDPE / LLDPE / HDPE, days per campaign). Printing (flexo, rotogravure, digital) and lamination run as batches of customer jobs with heavy changeovers — cylinders or sleeves, inks, viscosity, register. Slitting and pouch / bag making are batch processes with many small jobs. Cylinder engraving or plate making is a job shop. One order flows through all of them — which is why scheduling, WIP and changeover (Week 2) decide whether the plant delivers on time.

Other industries look the same through this lens. A machine shop has job-shop machining feeding a batch assembly; a food plant has continuous cooking feeding batch packing; an auto supplier has batch press shops feeding a paced assembly line.

6What a factory competes on

CostMaterial yield, conversion cost per kg or per piece, overheads (Days 6, 26, 28).
QualityConformance to specification, consistency, no complaints (Week 3).
DeliveryShort lead time and on-time in full (OTIF) (Days 2, 8, 9, 12).
FlexibilitySmall batches, quick changeovers, new products fast (Days 11, 19).

A plant cannot be best at all four at once with the same process. Order winners win the business (a lower price, a shorter lead time); order qualifiers only let you be considered (food-safety certification, barrier properties that meet the spec). Know which is which for each customer segment.

7Worked example

Where does a new product fit?A customer asks for 3 million printed sachets a month of one design, for two years. Current route: gravure print (batch) → laminate (batch) → slit → pouch machine (batch, shared with 40 other SKUs). Volume high, variety one → this sits at the "line" end of the matrix but is being made in a batch process. Options: dedicate one pouch machine and keep cylinders mounted; or quote with the extra changeover and WIP cost. Decision depends on margin: dedicating removes ~12 changeovers a month (Day 11) and cuts lead time (Day 12).

8How to map your own plant

  1. List every product family and its annual volume and number of variants (SKUs).
  2. List every process step and classify it: job, batch, line or continuous.
  3. Mark where customer orders enter (the decoupling point) for each family.
  4. Place each family on the product–process matrix. Circle the ones off the diagonal.
  5. For each family write its order winner and order qualifiers.
  6. Ask: is the process type right for the volume? Is the stock held in the right place?

10Pro tips and common mistakes

  • One plant, several matrices. Do not force one process type on the whole factory; separate high-runners from specials ("plant within a plant").
  • Volume is per variant, not per plant. 500 tonnes a month across 600 SKUs is a batch business, not a continuous one.
  • Managers: ask engineers for the process type behind a missed delivery. Engineers: ask managers for the order winner behind a priority.
  • Do not move the decoupling point without moving the stock and the planning method with it.

11Practice

  1. Classify every department of your plant by process type. Which one sets the lead time?
  2. Name one product family that sits off the diagonal of the matrix and say what it costs you.
  3. For your two biggest customers, write the order winner and two order qualifiers.
