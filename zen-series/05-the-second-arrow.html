The Story

A student came to his teacher, upset. A colleague had criticised his work in front of others, and he had hardly slept since.

The teacher asked, “If a man is struck by an arrow, does it hurt?” “Of course.” “And if a second arrow strikes him in the same place?” “It hurts even more.”

The teacher nodded. “The first arrow you could not avoid; it came from outside. But the sleepless nights, the replaying, the ‘why me’, the speech you keep writing in your head — whose arrow is that?”

The student was quiet for a long time. Then the teacher said:

“We cannot always avoid the first arrow. The second one we shoot ourselves.”

Reflection

The first arrow is the event: the remark, the loss, the illness, the delay. It hurts, and it is allowed to hurt. The second arrow is our reaction to the pain — resentment, worry, self-pity, the story we tell about what it means. Often the second wound goes deeper than the first, and keeps bleeding long after the first has healed.

We cannot stop every first arrow. But we can learn to notice the moment we reach for the second one, and put the bow down. This teaching comes from the Buddha’s Sallatha Sutta; the classroom setting here is a retelling.

Carry this

Feel the first arrow. Don’t shoot the second.
