1Journey map: the emotion curve

List each touchpoint and rate how the customer feels (−5 to +5). People remember the peak and the end most (Kahneman's peak-end rule), so fix the worst dip and make the ending good. Example: buying a two-wheeler.

3Handling a complaint: LEARN

Listen without interrupting · Empathise ("I'm sorry this happened") · Apologise and own it · Resolve quickly, ideally at first contact · Notify and follow up, then fix the root cause. A well-handled complaint can leave a customer more loyal than one who never had a problem (the service recovery paradox), but only if recovery is fast and generous.

1SERVQUAL and the gaps model

Parasuraman, Zeithaml & Berry (1985, 1988) define service quality as the gap between expectations and perceptions across five dimensions: reliability, assurance, tangibles, empathy, responsiveness (RATER). The customer gap (Gap 5) is caused by four provider gaps:

Critiques: difference scores have psychometric problems; SERVPERF (perceptions only) often predicts as well. Still the most used diagnostic structure.

2Service-profit chain

Heskett et al. (1994): internal service quality → employee satisfaction → retention and productivity → external service value → customer satisfaction → loyalty → growth and profit. The empirical links are real but weaker than the original claims; the chain is strongest in high-contact services.

3Customer lifetime value

CLV = m × r ÷ (1 + d − r) m = annual margin, r = retention rate, d = discount rate (infinite horizon, margin received at the end of each year retained; add m for a margin received now)
CLV

The margin multiple r/(1 + d − r) explodes as r → 1, which is why small retention gains are so valuable and why Reichheld & Sasser (1990) reported that a five-point retention gain lifted profits 25–85% across their sample.
