Lean Thinking

James P. Womack and Daniel T. Jones · first published 1996 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Any organisation can create more value with less effort by defining value through the customer's eyes and relentlessly removing waste from the way that value is made.

Summary

Womack and Jones follow up their earlier study of Toyota by asking a broader question: can the ideas behind Toyota's production system work in any business? Their answer is yes, and they distil those ideas into a way of thinking that managers in manufacturing, services and beyond can apply. The enemy throughout is waste, which they describe using the Japanese word muda: any activity that consumes resources but creates nothing the customer values.

They organise lean thinking around five principles. First, specify value precisely, from the point of view of the end customer, for a specific product at a specific price and time. Many firms instead define value by what their existing assets and departments happen to produce.

Second, identify the value stream: every step needed to take a product from concept to launch, from order to delivery, and from raw material to the customer's hands. Mapping it usually shows that many steps add no value at all, and that some of them can be removed at once while others must wait for new methods or technology.

Third, make the remaining steps flow. Traditional organisations group work by function and move it in large batches, so products spend most of their time waiting in queues. The authors urge firms to arrange work around the product so it moves continuously without stops.

Fourth, let the customer pull. Instead of pushing goods out according to forecasts, a lean firm makes only what is requested, when it is requested, which cuts inventory and exposes problems.

Fifth, pursue perfection. Each improvement reveals further waste, so the cycle never ends.

Much of the book is made up of detailed case studies of American, German and Japanese companies that made this transformation, along with practical advice on leadership, organisation and sequencing the change. The authors argue that the gains in speed, quality and cost are large and lasting.

312 words

One-line takeaway: Define value as the customer sees it, map the value stream, make it flow, let customers pull, and keep removing waste without end.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What does the Japanese term 'muda' refer to in Lean Thinking?

    Answer B. The book's central target is muda, meaning waste: effort and resources spent on things the customer does not value.

  2. According to the authors, from whose point of view must value be defined?

    Answer A. Lean starts by specifying value as the end customer sees it, not as existing departments or assets would like it to be.

  3. What is a 'value stream'?

    Answer C. The value stream covers every action required to design, order, make and deliver a specific product.

  4. What does mapping the value stream typically reveal?

    Answer D. The authors find that much of the activity in a value stream adds no value, and part of it can be cut at once.

  5. Why do the authors criticise traditional 'batch-and-queue' production?

    Answer A. Grouping work by function and moving it in large batches leaves products sitting in queues between steps.

  6. What does the principle of 'flow' ask organisations to do?

    Answer C. Flow means removing stops and queues so that the product moves steadily from one step to the next.

  7. Under the 'pull' principle, a lean firm produces goods:

    Answer B. Pull means nothing is made upstream until someone downstream asks for it, which reduces inventory.

  8. Why is 'perfection' included as the fifth principle?

    Answer D. As value flows faster, hidden waste becomes visible, making continuous improvement an endless cycle.

  9. What broader claim do the authors make about lean ideas?

    Answer C. The book generalises Toyota's methods into principles and shows firms in several countries and sectors applying them.

  10. What kind of evidence makes up much of the book?

    Answer A. The authors illustrate their principles through in-depth accounts of real companies changing their operations.
