Why do π, e and i keep showing up everywhere?

Three numbers turn up in bridges, bank loans, bearings, electrical circuits, sound, light and spinning shafts. They are not random constants someone made up — each is the answer to a very simple question. Play with the live demos below to see where each one comes from and where you will meet it at school, college and work.

π Pi — the ratio of turning

Take any circle. Divide its circumference by its diameter. You always get the same number, π. Because everything that rotates — wheels, shafts, gears, planets, alternating current — moves along a circle, π appears whenever something turns or repeats.

π = 3.14159 26535 89793 23846 26433 83279 50288 41971 69399 37510 … (never ends, never repeats)

1 · Unroll a circle

A wheel of diameter 1 rolls along the ruler. The red line is the paint its rim leaves behind. After exactly one turn, where does it stop?

The purple curve is a cycloid — the path of a point on the rim. It also gives the fastest slide between two points (the brachistochrone).

2 · Find π by throwing darts

Throw random darts at a square. The quarter-circle covers π/4 of it, so π ≈ 4 × (darts inside ÷ all darts). This "Monte Carlo" idea is used to estimate risk, reliability and radiation shielding.

3 · Race of the π formulas

Kerala's Madhava of Sangamagrama (c. 1400) found an infinite series for π two centuries before Europe. Later formulas converge far faster. Slide to add terms.

10

Aryabhata (499 CE) wrote that a circle of diameter 20 000 has circumference about 62 832 → π ≈ 3.1416. Easy fractions: 22/7 = 3.142857 (2 decimals right), 355/113 = 3.1415929 (6 decimals right).

4 · π at the workbench

Every rotating-machinery calculation hides a π. Change the numbers.

Rotating machinesω = 2πN/60, power P = 2πNT/60, cutting speed v = πDN — gearboxes, lathes, turbines, pumps.
Waves & vibrationsin(2πft): every sound, AC supply (50 Hz in India), radio wave and machine vibration.
StatisticsThe bell curve has √(2π) in it — exam marks, measurement errors, manufacturing tolerances.
StructuresEuler buckling load P = π²EI/L² for columns; area and stress in round shafts.
Space & GPSOrbits, satellite paths and map projections are built from circles and ellipses.
Signal processingThe Fourier transform (used in MP3, JPEG, vibration spectra) uses e−2πift.

e Euler's number — natural growth and decay

Whenever something changes at a rate proportional to its own size — money earning interest, bacteria multiplying, a hot cup cooling, a capacitor charging, a vibration dying out — the answer contains e. It is the only base whose curve has a slope exactly equal to its own height.

e = 2.71828 18284 59045 23536 02874 71352 66249 77572 47093 69995 …

1 · Where e comes from: compound interest

A bank pays 100% interest a year. If it adds interest n times a year, ₹1 becomes (1 + 1/n)n. More frequent compounding helps — but only up to a limit. That limit is e.

2 · The curve that is its own slope

Move the point. The tangent's slope of y = ex always equals the height of the curve. That is why calculus loves e: d/dx(ex) = ex.

3 · Time constant τ (RC circuits, sensors, cooling)

A first-order system rises as 1 − e−t/τ and falls as e−t/τ. After one τ it has done 63.2% of its change; after 5τ, 99.3% — the engineer's "practically settled".

Meet it in: thermocouple response time, capacitor charging, motor speed run-up, filter design, tank draining, drug levels in blood.

4 · Damped vibration

A struck bell, a car suspension, a machine on its mounts: the vibration shrinks inside an envelope e−ζωt. Change the damping ratio ζ.

FinanceContinuous compounding A = Pert; "rule of 70" for doubling time comes from ln 2 ≈ 0.693.
ElectronicsRC and RL circuits, diode equation I = Is(eV/VT−1), filters.
Radioactivity & medicineN = N₀e−λt; carbon dating, drug dosing, X-ray attenuation.
Heat transferNewton's law of cooling; fin efficiency; heat exchangers.
ProbabilityNormal distribution e−x²/2, Poisson failures, exponential reliability R(t) = e−λt.
Machine learningSoftmax, sigmoid 1/(1+e−x), log-loss — all built on e and ln.

i The imaginary unit — a number that rotates

i is defined by i² = −1. The name "imaginary" is unfortunate: i is best seen as an instruction, "turn 90° anticlockwise". Doing it twice turns you 180°, which is the same as multiplying by −1. Anything that rotates or oscillates — AC power, vibrations, waves, control loops — becomes simple arithmetic with i. Electrical engineers write it j because i is already used for current.

i¹ = i · i² = −1 · i³ = −i · i⁴ = 1 · then the cycle repeats

1 · Multiply by i = turn 90°

Drag the purple point z anywhere. The faded points are i·z, i²·z = −z and i³·z. They always sit at right angles, on the same circle.

2 · Phasors: a rotating arrow makes a sine wave

A vector spinning at constant speed casts a sine wave. Two arrows with a phase angle between them give two shifted waves — exactly how AC voltage vs current, or two vibration sensors, are compared.

3 · AC circuit impedance with j

Resistors, coils and capacitors combine as one complex number Z = R + j(XL − XC). Its length gives the current; its angle gives the phase.

Why not just use angles?

With complex numbers, rotating becomes multiplying, and differentiating a wave becomes multiplying by iω. A differential equation turns into ordinary algebra. That one trick powers:

  • Frequency response, Bode plots and the s-plane in control engineering
  • The FFT that turns a vibration signal into a spectrum of frequencies
  • Phase between accelerometers to find unbalance and misalignment
  • Quantum mechanics (Schrödinger's equation has an i in it)
  • Airfoil design by conformal mapping (Joukowski transform)
  • Fractals like the Mandelbrot set: z → z² + c

eiπ+1=0 Where π, e and i meet

Euler's formula eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ says that "growing" in the imaginary direction does not make you bigger — it makes you go round a circle. Put θ = π (half a turn) and you land on −1, giving the identity that links the five most important numbers: 0, 1, π, e and i.

eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ   ⟹   eiπ + 1 = 0

Watch eiθ being built

Just as e = lim (1 + 1/n)n, we can compute eiθ = lim (1 + iθ/n)n: take n tiny steps, each one "a little bit sideways". The red path is those n steps. As n grows, it hugs the unit circle and ends at cos θ + i sin θ.

Reading the result

Two everyday consequences:

  • cos θ = (eiθ + e−iθ)/2 — every real vibration is two counter-rotating phasors. This is why an FFT shows "negative frequencies".
  • Trig identities for free: ei(a+b) = eiaeib gives cos(a+b) and sin(a+b) in one line.
  • Powers of complex numbers: (r eiθ)n = rⁿ einθ (De Moivre) — used for roots of polynomials and filter design.

φ The golden ratio — the most irrational number

Split a line so that the whole is to the long part as the long part is to the short part. That ratio is φ = (1 + √5)/2. It satisfies φ² = φ + 1, and ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…) home in on it. Because φ is the number hardest to approximate by fractions, nature uses it to pack seeds and leaves without overlap. (Indian poet-mathematicians like Virahanka and Hemachandra described this sequence centuries before Fibonacci.)

φ = 1.61803 39887 49894 84820 45868 34365 63811 77203 … · 1/φ = 0.61803… · φ² = 2.61803…

1 · Sunflower seed packing

Each new seed is placed at a fixed turning angle from the previous one. At the golden angle 360°/φ² ≈ 137.508° the seeds fill the disc evenly. Nudge the angle by a fraction of a degree and gaps or spokes appear.

2 · Golden rectangle and spiral

Cut a square off a golden rectangle and what remains is again a golden rectangle — forever. Quarter-circles through the squares trace the golden spiral.

3 · Fibonacci ratios → φ

OptimisationGolden-section search finds the minimum of a function by shrinking the interval by 0.618 each step — used in design optimisation and line searches.
Computer scienceFibonacci hashing, Fibonacci heaps, and the worst case of Euclid's GCD algorithm.
NaturePinecones, pineapples, sunflowers and leaf arrangement (phyllotaxis) use Fibonacci spirals.
DesignOften used for pleasing proportions in layouts and logos — though many popular "golden ratio" claims about art are myths.

√2 The first number proven irrational

The diagonal of a unit square is √2. The Baudhayana Sulba Sutra (c. 800 BCE) gives it as 1 + 1/3 + 1/(3·4) − 1/(3·4·34) ≈ 1.4142157, correct to five decimals. The Greeks proved no fraction can equal it: if √2 = p/q in lowest terms, then p² = 2q², so p is even, so q is even too — a contradiction.

√2 = 1.41421 35623 73095 04880 16887 24209 69807 … · 1/√2 = 0.70710 67811 …

The paper in your printer is built on √2

ISO 216 paper has sides in the ratio 1 : √2. Fold it in half and the half has the same shape. A0 has an area of 1 m²; each next size is half. That is why an A4 page shrinks perfectly onto A5 at 71% (= 1/√2) on a photocopier.

A-series sizes

√2 and 1/√2 elsewhere

  • RMS value: AC peak = √2 × RMS, so 230 V mains peaks at about 325 V.
  • −3 dB point: a filter's cut-off is where amplitude falls to 1/√2 (half power).
  • 45° geometry: sin 45° = cos 45° = 1/√2 — bracing, chamfers, vector components.
  • Music: the tritone in equal temperament is a frequency ratio of √2.

More special numbers you will meet

A gallery of numbers that quietly run engineering, science and daily life.

0
Brahmagupta, 628 CE

Zero as a number with its own rules (Brahmasphutasiddhanta) made the place-value decimal system — and all of computing — possible.

√3
1.73205 08075 …

Three-phase power: line voltage = √3 × phase voltage, so 230 V phase gives 400 V line. Also the height of an equilateral triangle and hexagonal nut geometry.

ln 2

Half-life = 0.693/λ. Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ growth % ("rule of 70"). 3 dB ≈ factor of 2 in power.

2π
6.28318 53071 …

One full turn in radians. Converts hertz to rad/s: ω = 2πf. Some people argue it should have been "the" circle constant (τ).

π² ≈ g
9.8696 vs 9.81 m/s²

Not a coincidence: the metre was once proposed as the length of a pendulum that beats seconds, T = 2π√(L/g) = 2 s ⇒ g = π² m/s² for L = 1 m.

γ
0.57721 56649 …

Euler–Mascheroni constant: the gap between 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/n and ln n. Appears in number theory, Bessel functions and the coupon-collector problem. Nobody knows yet if it is irrational!

1729
Ramanujan's taxi-cab number

The smallest number expressible as a sum of two cubes in two ways: 1³ + 12³ = 9³ + 10³. Ramanujan noticed this instantly when G. H. Hardy called it "dull".

∞
not a number, a direction

Limits, asymptotes, infinite series and Cantor's discovery that some infinities are bigger than others.

c, h, k…
physical constants

Speed of light c = 299 792 458 m/s (exact by definition since 1983), Planck's h, Boltzmann's k — since 2019 the SI units themselves are defined from these.

2¹⁰ = 1024
kibi vs kilo

Why "1 KB" was once 1024 bytes. Powers of two set memory sizes, ADC resolution (a 16-bit ADC has 65 536 levels) and FFT block lengths.

Try it: Kaprekar's magic 6174

D. R. Kaprekar, a school teacher from Devlali, Maharashtra, found this in 1949. Take any 4-digit number with at least two different digits. Arrange the digits largest-first and smallest-first, subtract, and repeat. You always reach 6174 within 7 steps.
