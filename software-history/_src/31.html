01The requirement: books that balance, without an accountant's training

Double-entry bookkeeping — every transaction recorded as a debit in one account and a credit in another — dates from 15th-century Italy, described by Luca Pacioli in 1494. Indian businesses also kept the traditional bahi-khata. By the 1980s, mainframe accounting existed for large companies, but small traders kept handwritten ledgers, and closing the books each year took weeks.

Shyam Sunder Goenka ran a business supplying raw materials and machine parts to textile mills in southern and eastern India. Unable to find software that handled his accounts, he asked his son Bharat Goenka, a mathematics graduate, to write one. The result, launched in 1986 as an MS-DOS program, was Peutronics Financial Accountant.

02Tally's journey

1986 — Peutronics Financial Accountant. Basic accounting on MS-DOS. The company was incorporated in 1991 and renamed Tally Solutions in 1999; it is based in Bengaluru.
1990s — Tally 4.5, 5.4. Keyboard-driven screens, fast on modest PCs, and "codeless" configuration. Tally spread through a network of partners and training institutes; "Tally knowledge" became a job qualification for accountants.
1999 — Tally 6.3. Windows version.
2005 — Tally 7.2. VAT compliance as Indian states moved from sales tax to VAT.
2006 — Tally 8.1 and 9. Multilingual versions, including Indian languages; payroll and TDS.
2009 — Tally.ERP 9. A broader business management system with remote access and a support subscription (TSS).
2016–17 — GST. GST began on 1 July 2017. Tally was shortlisted as a GST Suvidha Provider in 2016; Release 6 of Tally.ERP 9 supported it. The 2025 rate restructuring (5%, 18% and 40% from 22 September) again forced updates.
2020 — TallyPrime. A redesigned interface with search ("Go To") and e-invoicing support as GST e-invoicing began. TallyPrime Edit Log (2022) answered the Companies Act audit-trail requirement.
2025–26 — TallyPrime 7. TallyPrime 7.0 launched in December 2025; further 7.x releases in 2026 added automation, connected banking and compliance features.

05At the top: ERP systems

Large organisations use Enterprise Resource Planning systems that combine finance with purchasing, production, stores, HR and maintenance. SAP (founded 1972 in Germany by former IBM engineers) released R/2 for mainframes in 1979 and the client–server R/3 in 1992, which became the corporate standard; its successor S/4HANA arrived in 2015. Oracle E-Business Suite, Microsoft Dynamics and Infor compete. Indian public-sector units, railways and defence production organisations use SAP and Oracle systems; government accounting has its own platforms such as PFMS.

Lesson shared by Tally and SAP: the tax law drives the software. VAT (2005), GST (2017), e-way bills (2018) and e-invoicing (2020) forced upgrades across India.

06Try it: GST on an invoice

Under GST, a sale within the same state carries CGST + SGST (split equally); a sale to another state carries IGST. Accounting software decides this from the place of supply.

Since 22 September 2025 the main GST rates are 5% and 18%, with 40% for select luxury and demerit goods (the old 12% and 28% slabs were removed). Illustration only — check the rate for each item.

08Keeping books safe

  • Back up the data folder regularly to another disk; Tally's backup and restore are built in.
  • Audit trail. From 1 April 2023 companies must use accounting software that records an edit log that cannot be disabled; TallyPrime Edit Log provides it.
  • Export to Excel, XML or JSON (GST returns) for auditors and analysis.
  • Licences. Cracked "Tally" copies are common and often carry malware (Episode 32); use licensed copies or the free Educational mode for learning.

10Lessons

  1. Fit the user. Tally won by being fast, keyboard-driven and usable by a clerk, not by having the most features.
  2. Ecosystem matters. Partners, training institutes and trained accountants made Tally hard to displace.
  3. Regulation is a product feature. Each tax change was both a threat and an opportunity.
  4. Double entry is an error check. The same idea — every change balanced by another — is used in engineering data integrity.
