High Output Management

Andrew S. Grove · first published 1983 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

A manager's output is the output of the team and the neighbouring groups they influence, so management is a production process that can be measured and improved.

Summary

Andy Grove wrote this book while running Intel, and he treats management as an engineer would: a process with inputs, outputs and bottlenecks. His central idea is simple. A manager's output is not the work they personally do, but the output of their own team plus that of the other groups they influence. Everything else in the book follows from this definition.

He begins with a production lesson, using the example of serving a simple breakfast on time. Any process has a limiting step around which the rest should be planned. Problems should be caught at the cheapest stage, before value is added. Good managers watch a small set of leading indicators, and pair them so that pushing one measure, such as speed, does not quietly damage another, such as quality.

Next comes leverage. Because a manager's time is limited, the useful question is which activities raise the team's output the most. Gathering information, nudging decisions, setting an example and making decisions all count as managerial work. Training staff is one of the highest-leverage tasks a manager can take on.

Grove gives meetings a clear purpose. Regular one-on-ones belong to the subordinate and keep information flowing; staff meetings and reviews coordinate the group; other meetings exist only to solve a specific problem. For decisions, he favours open debate, a clear decision, and then full support from everyone, including those who disagreed.

He also describes planning through objectives with measurable key results, an approach later adopted widely as OKRs, and explains how large firms combine central and local control.

Finally, he addresses people. When performance falls short, the cause is either lack of ability or lack of motivation, and the manager's tools are training and motivating. How closely to supervise should depend on a person's experience with the specific task, not on seniority or personal liking.

305 words

One-line takeaway: Judge yourself by your team's output, and spend your limited time on the activities with the greatest leverage.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. How does Grove define a manager's output?

    Answer C. Grove measures managers by the results of the groups they lead or influence, not by their personal activity.

  2. What example does Grove use to explain basic production principles?

    Answer A. He uses a simple breakfast operation to illustrate limiting steps, inventory and quality checks.

  3. Where in a process does Grove say problems are best detected?

    Answer D. Fixing a defect early is cheaper because less work and value have been invested in it.

  4. Why does Grove recommend pairing indicators?

    Answer B. A paired measure, such as quality alongside output, guards against over-reacting to one number.

  5. What does 'managerial leverage' refer to?

    Answer A. High-leverage activities are those that most raise the output of the people the manager affects.

  6. Whose meeting is a one-on-one, according to Grove?

    Answer C. The subordinate sets the agenda, so the meeting serves their needs and surfaces information.

  7. What does Grove expect after a decision has been reached in an open debate?

    Answer D. His model is free discussion, a clear decision, then everyone backing it.

  8. The planning approach in the book, based on objectives and measurable results, later became widely known as:

    Answer B. Grove's objectives-with-key-results method is the root of the OKR practice popular today.

  9. According to Grove, when someone is not performing, the cause is usually:

    Answer C. He reduces underperformance to these two causes, answered by training and motivation respectively.

  10. What should determine how closely a manager supervises a person?

    Answer A. Grove ties management style to task-relevant maturity: less experience calls for more structure.
