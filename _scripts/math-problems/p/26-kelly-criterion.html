1. The problem

Someone offers you an even-money bet on a coin that lands heads 60 % of the time; you always bet heads. You start with ₹10,000 and can bet any fraction of your current money on each toss, again and again.

The odds are in your favour, so should you bet everything each time? Half? What fraction makes your money grow fastest?

2. The answer

Best fraction
20 %
of current money each toss: f* = p − q/b = 0.6 − 0.4 = 0.2
Growth at 20 %
+2.0 %
per toss for a typical run — ₹10,000 grows to roughly ₹3 crore in 400 tosses.
Bet 40 % instead
−0.24 %
per toss: you shrink towards nothing, despite the edge.

Betting everything wins only if every toss comes up heads — almost certain ruin. Over-betting is worse than under-betting: twice the Kelly fraction gives roughly zero growth (here slightly negative).

3. Working it out

Bet a fraction f at odds b to 1 (even money: b = 1). A win multiplies your money by (1 + bf), a loss by (1 − f). Wealth is a product of such factors, so what matters in the long run is the average of their logarithms — the growth rate per bet:

G(f) = p ln(1 + bf) + q ln(1 − f)

Setting dG/df = 0 gives John Kelly's 1956 result:

f* = p − q / b      (edge ÷ odds)

For p = 0.6, b = 1: G(0.2) = 0.6 ln 1.2 + 0.4 ln 0.8 = 0.0201. G(0.4) = 0.6 ln 1.4 + 0.4 ln 0.6 = −0.0024.

The trap is that the average money after many bets grows fastest by betting everything, yet the typical person ends up broke. A product of random factors is dominated by rare lucky runs.

5. Four gamblers, same coin

All four see the same tosses. They bet ½ Kelly, Kelly, 2 × Kelly and your fraction. Money on a log scale.

6. Fine print

  • Kelly assumes you know p exactly. In real life your estimate of the edge is uncertain, and over-betting is so costly that professionals usually bet "half Kelly" — about three-quarters of the growth with far smaller swings.
  • Edward Thorp used it at blackjack in the 1960s and later in investing. Claude Shannon worked on related ideas with Kelly at Bell Labs.
  • The same logic sizes any repeated risk taken out of a pot that compounds — and explains why "never risk ruin" beats "maximise the average".
  • This page is about mathematics, not a recommendation to gamble or a guide to investing.
