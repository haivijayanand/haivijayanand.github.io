Self-test

Forty reference charts spanning 1905–2045 and latitudes −34° to +60°, computed here from a full ephemeris and baked in as fixed expectations. The page checks itself against them on load, so a frame or convention error shows up as a number rather than as quietly wrong daśā dates.

Conventions, stated explicitly. Longitudes are geometric, referred to the mean equinox of date — no nutation, no aberration. Both terms are under 40″, which is an order of magnitude below the error a four-minute birth-time uncertainty puts into the ascendant, so the choice is immaterial in practice; it is documented because a later "fix" that adds them would break the self-test for no gain. Rāhu and Ketu are the mean node, which differs from the true node by up to ~1.6°. Historical time zones come from the browser's own IANA database, so a 1943 Kolkata birth correctly resolves to +06:30 rather than +05:30.

Sub-components in full

Bhāva Bala — house strength

A different quantity with a different formula: the lord's full Ṣaḍbala + a directional term for the house + the aspects falling on its midpoint. Not a sum over the planets sitting in it.

Caveats. Houses here are whole-sign, so the bhāva madhya is taken as the sign midpoint; under Śrīpati or other cusp systems the aspectual term would differ. Sources disagree on whether Aquarius belongs to the Nara group — it is included here as the water-bearer, a human figure. Sagittarius and Capricorn each split mid-sign between two classes, which whole-sign houses cannot express, so the midpoint decides. Some texts add two further terms, Bhāva Śubha-Aśubha and Bhāva Dina-Rātri, which are not implemented.

Is summing them the right approach?

The tradition says yes — Ṣaḍbala is the sum, and BPHS gives a minimum per planet against which to judge it. But three things are worth knowing before you read a total as a verdict.

Which planet is strong where

Dik Bala answers a different question from exaltation. Exaltation asks how comfortably a planet expresses its nature; direction asks in which sphere of life it has leverage. A Sun in Aries inside the 10th house has both — peak sign alignment and full directional power.

The solar system drawn from the same Keplerian elements the charts use. Switch the centre to Earth and turn trails on to watch the retrograde loops draw themselves.

What is honest here and what is not. Positions come from the JPL Keplerian elements used everywhere else in this tool, so the geometry is real. Notional orbits space the rings evenly by rank — order preserved, distance discarded — because at true scale the inner four planets crowd into 40 px. True relative sizes make Mercury 0.5 px and Mars 0.7 px, which is the honest picture and also an unreadable one; the Sun is capped or it would be 139 px across and swallow the diagram. The Moon is exaggerated 60× — at true scale its orbit is 0.07 px. Only the ecliptic plane is drawn: inclinations run 7° for Mercury and under 3.4° for the rest, so little is lost.

Three things worth looking for. Switch to Earth-centred with 10-year trails: the loops are retrograde episodes, and you can count them — roughly 4.7 for Mars per decade, 9.7 for Saturn. Watch Mercury and Venus stay tethered close to the Sun, which is why they never oppose it and why Bhadra is the rarest Mahāpuruṣa yoga. And flip North to South: the same motion reverses handedness, which is all that separates a South Indian chart from a North Indian one.

Newtonian attraction between each body and the Earth, computed as F ∝ M₁M₂ / r², expressed as a percentage of the pull the Moon exerts on the Earth at its mean distance. Mass is in Earth masses; distance is in Earth–Moon distances, where 1 unit = 384,400 km.

Two directions, easily confused. Dṛṣṭi is where a planet looks — a relationship to other houses, asymmetric, and the basis of the aspect lines on the chart tabs. Dik Bala is where a planet is strong — a single house, based on the cardinal directions: 10th is South and the zenith, 4th is North and the nadir, 1st is East where things rise, 7th is West where they set. The Sun and Mars are strongest overhead at the 10th, Jupiter and Mercury rising in the 1st, the Moon and Venus at the nadir, Saturn setting in the West. The nodes are left blank in the computed columns: Rāhu and Ketu own no sign, have no agreed exaltation, and their dṛṣṭi is disputed — some sources give them the 5th, 7th and 9th like Jupiter, others none at all. Rather than pick one, the tool omits it. Only the columns marked green are computed; the kāraka significations and the descriptions are stored.

Two things worth noticing. 27 nakṣatras × 4 pādas = 108 pādas, and the zodiac holds exactly 108 navāṁśas — pāda and navāṁśa are the same division counted differently, which is why the D-9 chart and the nakṣatra pāda always agree. And nāḍī is derivable: it runs Ādi–Madhya–Antya in blocks of three, reversing each block, and that rule reproduces the published list for all 27 with no mismatches. It is computed here rather than stored. Gaṇa, yoni, deity and symbol are not derivable — no rule generates them, so they are stored and marked as such. The star identifications are the astronomy underneath: Kṛttikā is the Pleiades, Rohiṇī is Aldebaran, Citrā is Spica, Jyeṣṭhā is Antares, Svāti is Arcturus, Śravaṇa is Altair.

Sign attributes

Every one of these is arithmetic on the sign's index — element is i mod 4, mode is i mod 3, parity is i mod 2. Nothing is stored.

Element is the trine relationship under another name. Two signs share an element exactly when they are 4 or 8 apart — trinal — and that holds for all 144 pairs with no exceptions. So "trines are harmonious" and "same-element signs are harmonious" are one statement, not two. Opposition and the 3rd/11th pair complementary elements (fire with air, earth with water); squares pair adjacent ones. In Parāśarī practice mode does more work than element — movable/fixed/dual drives Kendrādi Bala, the D-16, D-20 and D-45 vargas, and the Nābhasa Āśraya yogas, while element surfaces mainly in the D-27. The Bhāva class in the last-but-one column is a different cut again, used only for Bhāva Digbala.

Where the rulerships come from

Rulership is not twelve separate stipulations. Seat the two luminaries on the Cancer–Leo axis, then walk outward in both directions at once, assigning the remaining five in order of orbital period. Drag the control to build it up step by step — each planet's two signs are mirror images across that axis, so every chord below is symmetric about it.

Why Jupiter and Mercury can never be yogakāraka

Yogakāraka needs one house from the kendra set {4, 7, 10} and one from the trikoṇa set {5, 9}. There are only six such pairs, and they sit at just three separations. A planet can reach one only if the gap between its own two signs matches.

Two consequences fall out with no doctrine involved. Jupiter — the greatest natural benefic — is permanently barred from the system's most auspicious functional status, for every rising sign, purely because its signs sit 3 apart. And a separation of 2 would qualify via (5,7) and (7,9), but no planet owns signs 2 apart, so that slot goes unused.

Everything above is computed, not looked up. What it cannot tell you is the phala — the predicted effects of a given planet in a given house. That layer is an enumerated list (BPHS devotes chapters 12–23 to it, and a further chapter to the 144 bhāva-lord placements), it is not derivable from these rules, and the classical texts disagree with one another about it. The pink cells are where the derivable layer runs out: a planet owning one auspicious and one inauspicious house simultaneously. The texts acknowledge these and defer to the practitioner's judgement rather than resolving them.

Friendship, derived

The saptavargaja figures assume natural relationships only. The full scheme adds tātkālika (temporary) friendship — planets in the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 10th, 11th or 12th from a planet are its temporary friends, the rest temporary enemies — which combines with the natural grade to give great friend (22.5) and great enemy (1.875). That layer needs an actual chart, so it is not shown here. The virūpa values are also not agreed: the widely used set runs 45 / 30 / 22.5 / 15 / 7.5 / 3.75 / 1.875, while BPHS as translated gives 45 / 30 / 20 / 15 / 10 / 4 / 2. The top two rungs match and the rest do not — the structure was transmitted, the numbers were not.

Pakṣa Bala — the Moon's sliding nature

The Moon's nature is not a switch but a ramp. BPHS gives it as a formula: fold the Sun–Moon elongation into 0–180°, divide by 3, and that is the Pakṣa Bala of every benefic in virūpas; subtract it from 60 for every malefic. The Moon's own share is doubled. Drag through a lunation to see it move.

The two solid lines are mirror images summing to a constant 60 — one quantity, read from both ends. That is what makes this layer derivable: it is a formula, not a list. Note what it implies about the Moon itself. A Moon a day past new is nominally waxing and so nominally benefic, yet its Pakṣa Bala is near zero, while every malefic in the chart is near its maximum. The bright-half / dark-half binary that gets quoted in summaries is a coarse reading of a continuous curve.

Every planet in every sign, split at the mūlatrikoṇa boundary, with both strength components side by side. The Dignity grid shows only Saptavargaja, which is why exaltation appears to score less than mūlatrikoṇa there — exaltation is scored by Ucca Bala instead, and the two must be added.

How to read it. Saptavargaja scores who owns the sign — 45 mūlatrikoṇa, 30 own, 15 friend, 7.5 neutral, 3.75 enemy — and gives exaltation nothing. Ucca Bala scores angular distance from the debilitation degree and runs 0–60. Only their sum is comparable across placements. Because Ucca Bala slides continuously, the band rows show it at the band midpoint with the range in brackets; the ★ rows are the exact exaltation and debilitation degrees, where it reaches precisely 60 or 0. The highest total any planet can reach is Mercury at Virgo 16–20°, the sole placement where exaltation, mūlatrikoṇa and own sign coincide.

Where a planet's friendships come from. The houses are counted from its mūlatrikoṇa sign — not from wherever it happens to sit in your chart, which is why the resulting table is the same for everyone. Lords of the 2nd, 4th, 5th, 8th, 9th and 12th are friends; lords of the 3rd, 6th, 7th, 10th and 11th are enemies; the exaltation lord is added as a friend, and anything landing on both lists becomes neutral.

Why the relationship is not mutual. Because the count starts from each planet's own mūlatrikoṇa, and those sit at different points in the zodiac, the view is one-way: 11 of the 21 pairs are asymmetric. Mercury counts the Sun a friend while the Sun counts Mercury neutral; Mars counts the Moon a friend while the Moon counts Mars neutral. That asymmetry is why the tradition needs a five-fold relationship combining this fixed table with a chart-specific temporary one, rather than a simple symmetric list. And note what the rule does not explain: it compresses 42 relationships into one six-house rule, but no text says why those six houses — the set includes the 8th and 12th, which are duḥsthānas, and excludes the 7th and 10th, which are kendras.

What actually happens when one planet sees another, quantified. This uses sphuṭa dṛṣṭi — a continuous function of angular separation — not the whole-sign dṛṣṭi drawn on the chart tabs. The two are different models from the same corpus: whole-sign drives yogas and predictions, sphuṭa drives Ṣaḍbala, and they disagree at house boundaries.

How to read the matrix. Each row is a planet casting its gaze; each column is a planet receiving it. A cell is the sphuṭa dṛṣṭi value in virūpas, signed by the nature of the aspecting planet — blue where a benefic looks, red where a malefic does. Net received sums each column: that is the balance of support and affliction on that planet. Verification: the curve reproduces all six published anchor values exactly — 30°→0, 60°→15, 90°→45, 120°→30, 150°→0, 180°→60 — and equals the published piecewise formula across 30–180° to zero error. Why the Jupiter–Saturn yogas are generational

Jupiter takes 11.86 years to orbit, Saturn 29.46. Their synodic period — the time for one to lap the other — is 19.86 years, so the angle between them advances only 18.13° per year. Everyone born inside the same window shares the same Jupiter–Saturn relationship, whatever else is in their charts.

The trine holds for about five continuous years in every twenty. So the claim that a person with Guru–Śani in trine "gets what he wills" is a claim about a quarter of humanity, arriving in five-year blocks — every child born across those years carries it equally, alongside their own outcomes. The Great Conjunction of December 2020 in Aquarius is the most recent same-sign window; the previous fell in 2000 and the next comes around 2040. A configuration shared by an entire birth cohort cannot distinguish individuals within it, which is a structural limit no amount of interpretive skill gets around.

On the Vedic attribution. Verses about resolve and diligent effort are sometimes quoted from the Ṛgveda in support of this yoga. Those verses are about human resolution and say nothing of planets, houses or aspects — and the horoscopic apparatus this yoga depends on (twelve houses reckoned from a rising sign, planetary aspects) reached India through Hellenistic transmission in the 2nd–3rd centuries CE, more than a millennium after the Ṛgveda was composed. The sentiment and the technique are both real; they simply come from different places and different eras.

Two things the arithmetic settles. First, "five will not form" is nearly right but not quite — 42 charts in a million simulated ones carried all five. It is rare, not impossible, and the sometimes-heard claim that Mercury and Venus make it astronomically impossible is wrong: Mercury in Gemini and Venus in Pisces are three signs apart, a valid kendra relationship, and within the ~75° the two can be separated. Second, the ranks do not survive contact with the frequencies. On the broader reading, "equal to a king" describes about 915 million living people and "a king" about 141 million. The grades were written for a world with one king per kingdom.

The standard escape clause. Every source adds that mere presence does not guarantee the result — the yoga must be unafflicted, not aspected by malefics, and awaits a favourable daśā of the planet concerned. Those conditions are applied after the placement is known and none is quantified, so they can absorb any failed prediction without the yoga itself ever being wrong. It is the same structure as the Kemadruma cancellations, which repeal 98.6% of that rule's own cases.

How the frequencies were obtained. A 400,000-chart Monte Carlo with the lagna and the outer planets drawn uniformly, and Mercury and Venus constrained to their real elongation limits from the Sun (28° and 47°). That is an approximation — actual planetary distributions are not uniform over short epochs — but it is the right order of magnitude, and one internal check confirms the method: the yogakāraka form of Rāja yoga comes out at 50.2%, against the exact 50% you get from six of the twelve lagnas having a yogakāraka.

What the column actually shows. Around 74% of charts carry some Rāja yoga, 57% a Vipareeta Rāja yoga, 42% Kemadruma, 34% a Mahāpuruṣa yoga — and 100% a Saṅkhya yoga, by construction. Add the 1800 Nābhasa sub-varieties and the hundreds of Dhana combinations, and most charts match dozens of named yogas of opposite valence with no weighting rule for combining them. That is why a yoga hit is better read as "present, as it is in a third of charts" than as a verdict. The tradition says as much: every horoscope carries hundreds of good and bad yogas, and all of them are supposed to be synthesised before any conclusion is drawn.

Two clocks, and why both are needed. Daśā is fixed at birth and unfolds by arithmetic from the Moon's nakṣatra — it never consults the sky again. Gochara is the actual sky and knows nothing about your birth. The tradition holds that an event needs both: the daśā to promise it and the transit to trigger it. That is a doubling of free parameters, and it is worth seeing plainly — with two independent clocks and twelve houses each, some combination is always available to explain any outcome after the fact.

On Sade Sati. Saturn sits in one sign for everybody alive at a given moment; what differs between people is which house that is from their own natal Moon. Since Sade Sati is Saturn in the 12th, 1st or 2nd from the Moon, exactly 3 of 12 Moon signs qualify at any instant — 25% of everyone, about 2 billion people. It lasts about 7.5 years and returns every 29.5, so a person spends a quarter of life in it. The doctrine is internally consistent; it simply describes a quarter of humanity at all times.

Which bala each transition moves. A sign ingress changes the planet's sign and its house at once, so it shifts Sthāna (through Uccha, Saptavargaja, Ojayugma and Drekkāṇa) and Dig together — the before-and-after column sums those. A station flips the motional state, so it shifts Ceṣṭā, typically between 60 retrograde and 30 or less direct. A daśā or antardaśā boundary and a Śani period boundary move no bala whatever: the first is Viṃśottari timing, the second gochara, and neither enters Ṣaḍbala. Showing them in one list is the point — the tradition reads strength, timing and transit together, and they are genuinely different quantities. One caveat carried over: Ṣaḍbala is defined on the birth moment, so these transit values answer "how strong is this planet in the sky now" rather than anything classical.

Your twelve houses: what each contains, who rules it, where that lord went, and what the tradition calls it. The columns are shown side by side and not combined — classification, Bhāva Bala and lordship are independent judgements that often disagree, and any single score would be my invention rather than a classical measure.

The three views show different things, not the same grid in three colours. Classification is doctrine — it comes from the house number alone, so it is the same twelve-cell pattern for every person who has ever lived, and each cell gives the house's strength family, its puruṣārtha and its natural kāraka. Bhāva Bala is arithmetic on this chart — each cell gives the score, its rank among the twelve, and the three parts it is built from (the lord's Ṣaḍbala, Digbala, and net dṛṣṭi), drawn to scale. Where they disagree subtracts one from the other, and that is the view worth dwelling on: a house the doctrine calls good but this chart leaves weak is a promise with little behind it, and a house called bad but strongly backed is a difficulty with force in it. Across the three stored charts the correlation between the two measures is about +0.07 — that is, near zero — which is exactly why they are not merged.

How the influences are accounted — and where the tradition stops short. Three things bear on a house: its lord's condition (held primary — a house is judged by its lord), the planets sitting in it, and the planets aspecting it. The panel breaks all three out. But note what the only quantified measure does: Bhāva Bala counts the lord and the aspects and ignores the occupants entirely — its total is exactly Bhavādhipati + Digbala + Dṛṣṭi, verified. So the factor practitioners weigh most heavily in reading is the one factor the classical score omits, and no text supplies a weighting between the three. The occupant detail here is therefore shown, not scored.

On the verdict column. The tradition classifies houses by several overlapping schemes that agree on only seven of the twelve. Kendra 1/4/7/10, Trikoṇa 1/5/9, Duḥsthāna 6/8/12, Upacaya 3/6/10/11, Mārakā 2/7, Triṣaḍāya 3/6/11, Dhana 2/11 — and, cutting across all of them, the four puruṣārtha trines: Dharma 1/5/9, Artha 2/6/10, Kāma 3/7/11, Mokṣa 4/8/12, which name the aim a house serves and carry no good or bad sense at all. Every family a house belongs to is shown as a chip on its card; hover or tap a chip for what quoting that family means. Houses 1, 4, 5, 9 and 10 come out unambiguously good and 8 and 12 unambiguously bad; 2, 3, 6, 7 and 11 are contested by the tradition's own categories — the 6th is both a house of suffering and one where malefics are said to thrive, the 7th both a structural pillar and a killer. Lordship inverts things again: under kendrādhipatya doṣa a benefic ruling a kendra loses its benefic power, so the strongest houses corrupt good planets and redeem bad ones. Where that applies the lord column reads "Conflicted".

This identifies planets, not a date. The doctrine designates the lords of the 2nd and 7th as mārakā — the houses that hold what sustains life, and so are held to govern its end. That designation follows mechanically from lordship and is shown here. The step that converts it into a predicted time — mārakā daśā plus transit trigger — is deliberately not implemented; see the note below.

Mārakā lords for every lagna

Why the timing step is absent. Converting a mārakā designation into a date requires āyurdāya, and the classical corpus offers roughly 32 methods of it, with the choice between them itself decided by chart-dependent strength rules. Published worked examples on a single chart give Piṇḍāyu 79 years against Aṁśāyu 67 years 9 months — an eleven-year spread with no principled tiebreak, and practitioner literature concedes that intuition arbitrates. A tool that emitted one number from that would be manufacturing a precision the sources do not contain. Of everything in this system, death timing is also where a wrong output does the most harm.

What the tradition itself says. The same texts that teach the calculation add that no astrologer can in fact predict death, and that a practitioner should identify periods warranting attention to health rather than name a date. That caution stands on its own terms, independently of the empirical record.

The timing layer. Viṃśottari allots 120 years across nine planets in a fixed order; the Moon's nakṣatra at birth fixes which period is running and how much of it remains. Everything below follows by arithmetic from one number — the Moon's longitude.

Antardaśā inside the selected mahādaśā

What is and is not computed here. The period boundaries are exact arithmetic — nakṣatra lord, proportional balance, then the fixed order, with sub-periods at (A × B) ÷ 120. Nothing is looked up or interpreted. What the arithmetic cannot supply is the meaning: the claim that a given planet's period brings a given kind of year is not derivable from any of this, and it is the point at which the timing layer hands off to the enumerated one. Note also that Rāhu and Ketu take 25 of the 120 years between them despite owning no signs — the daśā scheme does not inherit the lordship constraints that govern everything on the Lagna rules tab. Dates use a 365.2425-day year; traditional reckoning is by solar or sidereal years and can shift long periods by several weeks.

Viṃśottari is not the only scheme. Aṣṭottarī runs to 108 years, Yoginī to 36, and the Jaimini chara daśās are reckoned by sign rather than by planet. Applied to the same chart they place different periods in the same year, and the literature offers no rule for deciding between them — the choice is made by school and habit.

What these are, and what they are not. Every condition here is exactly computable — it is a position, and positions are astronomy. What is claimed about them is a separate matter that has not been tested. Two things are worth holding while reading the list. The base rates are large: Saturn is in one of its three named difficult positions relative to the Moon for well over half a life, and Mercury is combust on more than four days in ten. And they are the same for everyone — Sade Sati occupies 25% of every life ever lived, because Saturn spends a quarter of its 29.5-year circuit in three of the twelve signs. Durations vary with the planet's speed: the Moon's periods run about 50 to 61 hours depending where its apogee sits, and that migrates round the zodiac in 8.85 years.

What this does and does not do. It computes the classical marriage conditions for two charts and reports, date by date, which are satisfied. It does not rank dates or call any of them auspicious — that step is the untested layer, and it is the one place where a confident-looking answer from a program could lead a family to act. The counts are shown because families are often told a date is rare or uniquely fortunate, and the arithmetic is worth seeing.

One chart or two. Set Conditions for to a single person and the partner's tārā and candra bala are dropped, leaving the four general conditions plus that person's two. This is the useful mode before a match exists — it shows which dates are open in principle. Expect roughly ten times as many qualifying dates, drawn from several nakṣatras rather than one: intersecting two people's Moon conditions is what collapses the calendar so sharply, and seeing both figures side by side is the clearest illustration of it in the tool.

The conditions used, and where they are contested. Accepted nakṣatras: Rohiṇī, Mṛgaśīrṣa, Maghā, Uttara-phalgunī, Hasta, Svāti, Anurādhā, Mūla, Uttara-āṣāḍhā, Uttara-bhādrapadā, Revatī — lists vary between regions and lineages. Rejected tithis: 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 14, and the new and full moon. Weekdays: Monday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday. Guru and Śukra asta — Jupiter or Venus within 11° and 10° of the Sun — closes weeks at a time and is treated as a hard bar in South Indian practice. Tārā bala rejects the 3rd, 5th and 7th tārā counted from each person's janma nakṣatra; whether the 1st (janma tārā) should also be rejected is disputed, and it is allowed here. Candra bala rejects the Moon in the 4th, 8th or 12th from each natal Moon. Not implemented: the ceremony-hour lagna, rejected months, regional almanac rules, and the couple's daśā — all of which a practitioner would weigh.

Putting it together

How to read this honestly. Sections 1–5 are exact: geometry, a fixed lordship table, and daśā arithmetic. The step the tradition takes next — that these frames decide what actually happens to each person — has not been shown in controlled tests. Keep the base rates in view: each "verdict" in section 2 holds for one person in twelve per sign at any moment, so a favourable Saturn (3 of 12 houses) is true for 25% of everyone alive right now, a favourable Jupiter (5 of 12) for 42%, and a favourable Venus (9 of 12) for 75%.
