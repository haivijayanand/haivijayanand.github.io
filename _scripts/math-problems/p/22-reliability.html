1. The problem

  • Part A. A test rig has 100 parts. Every one must work for the rig to run, and each part is 99 % reliable over a test. How reliable is the rig?
  • Part B. A cooling loop needs one pump. Is it better to fit one excellent pump (99 % reliable) or two ordinary pumps (90 % each) in parallel, either of which can do the job?

2. The answer

Part A: 100 parts in series
36.6 %
0.99100. Excellent parts, unreliable machine.
Part B: two 90 % pumps in parallel
99 %
1 − 0.1² — the same as the expensive pump. Three of them give 99.9 %.

Series multiplies successes, so reliability falls fast. Parallel multiplies failures, so it rises fast — but only if the failures are truly independent.

3. Working it out

With independent parts of reliability R (and failure chance F = 1 − R):

Series of n:  Rsys = Rn      Parallel of n:  Rsys = 1 − Fn
k-out-of-n (e.g. 2-out-of-3 voting):  Rsys = Σi=k…n C(n, i) Ri Fn−i

For a long series chain a handy estimate is Rn ≈ e−nF: 100 parts with F = 1 % give e−1 ≈ 37 %. To keep a 100-part machine at 99 %, each part needs 99.99 %.

The catch: common-cause failure

Two pumps fed from the same power supply, sharing the same strainer or built from the same faulty batch can fail together. In the simple beta-factor model a fraction β of each pump's failures hits both at once. With β = 10 %, the two-pump system drops from 99.0 % to about 98.2 %, and no number of extra pumps can push it above 1 − βF = 99 %.

6. Fine print

  • These formulas are for one mission or test of fixed length. Over time, reliability falls as R(t) = e−λt for parts with a constant failure rate λ; the series and parallel rules apply at each moment.
  • Redundant parts that are never checked can be failed already, unnoticed ("latent failures"). Regular proof tests are what keep parallel redundancy honest.
  • Voting (2-out-of-3) sensors are used where a false trip and a missed trip both matter, such as overspeed and over-temperature protection on test rigs.
