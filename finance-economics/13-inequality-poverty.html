Inequality & Poverty

How a country can cut poverty fast while the gap between rich and poor stays wide. Covers how poverty and inequality are measured, why the numbers disagree, where India stands in 2026, and what policy can do. Pick your level.

Level 1 · For school students

Poverty is about having too little. Inequality is about how unevenly things are shared. They are related, but they are not the same.

1What is poverty?

Poverty means not having enough money or resources for basic needs: enough food, a safe home, clean water, schooling and health care.

To count the poor, governments draw a poverty line: a minimum amount a person needs each day. Anyone spending less than that is counted as poor. The World Bank's line for extreme poverty is $3 a day (adjusted for what money buys in each country), which is about ₹80 a day in India today.

2What is inequality?

Inequality means some people have much more than others. It is about the gap, not about how much the poorest have.

A country can have less poverty and more inequality at the same time: if everyone gets richer but the rich get richer much faster, fewer people are poor, yet the gap grows. The animation below shows this.

4Why are some people poor?

📚 Education and skillsBetter-educated people usually get better-paid jobs.
🌾 Kind of workMany families depend on small farms or daily-wage work, which pays little and is uncertain.
🏥 Health shocksOne illness can push a family into debt and poverty.
🗺️ Where you are bornChildren born in poorer states, villages, or disadvantaged communities often have fewer chances.

5How does a country help?

  • Free or cheap food grain for about 80 crore people through ration shops.
  • Free schools and midday meals, so children study and eat well.
  • Health insurance for poor families, so one illness does not ruin them.
  • A rural jobs guarantee: since July 2026, every village household can ask for up to 125 days of paid work a year (it was 100 days before). Wages are sent straight to bank accounts.
  • Taxes: richer people pay a higher share of income as tax, which pays for these things.

The World Bank says about 49.6 crore Indians crossed the $4.20 line between 2011 and 2023, one of the fastest declines anywhere, helped by more jobs (especially for rural women) and larger social transfers. India has had no official national poverty line since the Tendulkar (2011-12) and Rangarajan (2014) estimates; the government also cites NITI Aayog's multidimensional poverty index (health, education, living standards). The World Bank's own multidimensional measure was 15.5% in 2022-23.

PPP (purchasing power parity) converts dollars using what money buys locally, not the market exchange rate. One "PPP dollar" buys in India what one dollar buys in the US, which is about ₹20–21 at 2021 prices, far less than the ₹74 market rate of 2021.

3Why the inequality numbers disagree

Consumption surveys (HCES)Ask households what they spend. Gini 0.237 rural, 0.284 urban in 2023-24. The rich spend a small part of income and rarely answer surveys, so this shows low inequality.
World Bank (from surveys)Consumption Gini about 23–26, which places India among the more "equal" countries on this measure.
Income (World Inequality Lab)Combines tax records, national accounts and surveys. The top 10% get about 58% of income; the top 1% about 22.6%, the highest since Independence.
WealthMost unequal of all: the top 1% own about 40%, the top 10% about 65%. Land, housing, gold and shares are concentrated.

So "inequality is low" and "inequality is among the world's highest" can both be quoted from respectable sources. Always ask: consumption, income or wealth? Survey or tax data?

4The many faces of inequality in India

  • Rural vs urban: average urban spending per person was about 70% higher than rural in 2023-24, down from 84% in 2011-12.
  • Between states: poverty and spending differ several-fold between richer southern and western states and poorer eastern and central ones.
  • Gender: women earn only about 18% of India's labour income (World Inequality Report 2026), mainly because far fewer women are in paid work.
  • Social groups: Scheduled Tribe and Scheduled Caste households still have lower average spending, though the gaps have narrowed.
  • Opportunity: a child's schooling, health and job prospects still depend heavily on the parents' income and location.

6Why it matters to you

  • Falling into poverty is often one shock away: a hospital bill, job loss or crop failure. Health insurance and an emergency fund are the best protection (see Topic 14, Insurance & Risk).
  • Markets follow spending: companies selling to the top 10% (premium cars, flats) and to the mass market (biscuits, two-wheelers) can see very different demand. This is sometimes called a "K-shaped" pattern.
  • Escaping poverty is not the same as being secure: many households just above $4.20 a day are vulnerable to falling back.

Level 3 · Review for experts

Measurement (Lorenz, Gini, Palma, FGT), the survey–national-accounts gap, theories of inequality, mobility, and the policy debates.

1Measuring poverty: FGT and beyond

FGT: P_α = (1/N) · Σ_{y_i < z} ((z − y_i)/z)^α α = 0 headcount ratio · α = 1 poverty gap (depth) · α = 2 squared gap (severity, weights the poorest most)
  • The headcount ratio violates the monotonicity axiom (Sen, 1976): making a poor person poorer does not raise it. Policy targeted on headcount favours those just below the line.
  • The World Bank moved to 2021 PPPs in 2025, raising the extreme line from $2.15 to $3.00. India's 2022-23 HCES also changed survey method (multiple visits, modified mixed recall period), which lowers measured poverty against 2011-12. Comparisons across the break need care.
  • Multidimensional poverty (Alkire–Foster): deprivation counts across indicators with a cut-off k. It is sensitive to indicator choice and weights, but captures services that consumption misses.

2Lorenz curve and Gini: calculator

G = 1 − Σ_k (X_k − X_{k−1})·(Y_k + Y_{k−1}) Trapezoid rule on the Lorenz curve. G = 2 × area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve.

The defaults are illustrative. A Gini from five quintiles understates the true Gini because it assumes equality within each quintile. Try the WIL-style income split by setting the richest 20% far higher.

3The survey–national-accounts gap

  • Survey consumption in India covers a shrinking fraction of national-accounts private consumption. Missing top incomes (non-response, under-reporting) and the consumption–income gap both bias survey inequality downward.
  • WIL (Chancel and Piketty, 2019, updated in WIR 2026) splice surveys with income-tax tabulations and allocate unreported national income, often with Pareto top tails. The result is a top-10% income share of about 58%. Critics (e.g. Bhalla and others) argue the assumptions, given sparse Indian tax data, overstate concentration.
  • The two views are reconcilable: low consumption inequality at the bottom and middle, combined with very high income and wealth concentration at the top. The Palma ratio (top 10% / bottom 40%) is more sensitive to the tails than the Gini.

4Theories: growth, structure and capital

  • Kuznets (1955): an inverted U, where inequality rises as labour moves from agriculture to industry, then falls. The evidence is weak, and India's slow structural transformation (many workers still in agriculture, Topic 11) complicates the story.
  • Growth elasticity of poverty: growth reduces poverty faster where initial inequality is low and growth is labour-intensive (Ravallion; Datt and Ravallion on Indian states).
  • Piketty: when r > g, inherited wealth grows faster than income, so wealth concentrates. India's post-1991 rise in top shares fits capital-intensive, skill-biased growth plus asset-price gains.
  • Skill-biased technical change and returns to education widen wage gaps; credit and land constraints keep the poor from investing (poverty traps, Banerjee–Duflo).

5Mobility and opportunity

  • The "Great Gatsby curve" (Corak; Krueger): more unequal countries tend to have lower intergenerational mobility. Indian studies (Asher, Novosad and Rafkind) find low educational mobility, especially for SC/ST groups and in poorer regions, with gains for SCs and stagnation for Muslims.
  • Inequality of opportunity (Roemer) separates "circumstances" (caste, gender, birthplace, parents) from "effort". A large share of Indian consumption inequality is explained by circumstances.

6Policy toolkit and debates

  • Transfers: India's in-kind food subsidy and DBT reduce poverty at the bottom; targeting errors and exclusion persist. UBI proposals (Economic Survey 2016-17) versus targeted schemes.
  • Taxation: India's tax-to-GDP ratio is modest and relies heavily on indirect taxes. WIR proposes a wealth tax on the very rich; critics cite capital flight, valuation problems, and India's own abolition of its wealth tax in 2015.
  • Pre-distribution: education quality, health, female labour-force participation, land and credit access, labour-intensive manufacturing (Topics 11 and 12).
  • Measurement: a new HCES and a regular income survey, plus publishing tax tabulations, would narrow the gap between camps.

7Review checklist: open questions

  1. How much of the post-2011 poverty decline is real, and how much reflects the HCES method change?
  2. Which inequality measure should guide Indian policy: consumption Gini, income top shares, or wealth?
  3. Is a wealth or inheritance tax feasible and desirable in India?
  4. Should India adopt a new official poverty line, and at what level (for example $4.20 PPP)?
  5. How large is the vulnerable group just above the line, and what insurance does it need?
  6. Can growth become more labour-intensive enough to lift the bottom 50% share?
