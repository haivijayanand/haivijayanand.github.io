Differential Equations Part II · Part A · Chapter 03 · Equations 21–30

Structures and Machine Elements

The differential equations behind strength of materials and machine design: beam bending and shear deformation, column buckling, beam vibration, thick cylinders and rotating discs, gear-mesh dynamics, viscoelasticity, creep and fatigue crack growth. Here x is often position along a part, not time.

📐 10 equations🧪 10 live labs✍ 11 worked examples❓ 5-question quiz

◆In this chapter

  • Read EI·y⁗ = q as a chain: load → shear → moment → slope → deflection
  • Know when shear deformation (Timoshenko) matters and when buckling governs
  • Compute beam natural frequencies, hoop stresses and disc stresses
  • Model gear-mesh stiffness variation as a time-varying (Mathieu-type) system
  • Predict relaxation, creep and fatigue life by integrating rate laws

Boundary-value problems arrive in this chapter: instead of a starting value you have conditions at both ends (supports, free surfaces). The equations are still ODEs — just in space. The last three cards return to time: materials that flow, creep and crack.

21Euler–Bernoulli beam equation

ODElinearfourth orderboundary valueEuler and Daniel Bernoulli, 1750
EI·d⁴y/dx⁴ = q(x) V = −EI·y‴, M = EI·y″, θ = y′

y deflection · q distributed load (N/m) · E Young's modulus · I second moment of area · V shear · M bending moment

Plane sections stay plane and perpendicular to the neutral axis; curvature is proportional to bending moment, y″ = M/EI. Differentiate twice more using dM/dx = V and dV/dx = −q and you get the fourth-order equation. Four integrations, four constants — fixed by two conditions at each end: pinned (y = 0, M = 0), fixed (y = 0, θ = 0) or free (M = 0, V = 0).

Derivation in four lines

Equilibrium of a slice: dV/dx = −q and dM/dx = V. Geometry: strain at height z is ε = −z·κ, curvature κ = y″ for small slopes. Material: σ = Eε, so M = ∫σz dA = EI·y″. Combining: (EI·y″)″ = q, which is EI·y⁗ = q for constant EI.

Simply supported steel beam, span 3 m, I = 1000 cm⁴, 10 kN at mid-span. Maximum deflection and moment?EI = 210e9 × 1000e-8 = 2.1×10⁶ N·m² y_max = PL³/(48EI) = 10⁴ × 27/(48 × 2.1e6) = 2.68 mm M_max = PL/4 = 7.5 kN·m
A cantilevered bracket, 0.4 m long, carries a uniform 5 kN/m (I = 50 cm⁴). Tip deflection?y = wL⁴/(8EI) = 5000 × 0.0256/(8 × 210e9 × 50e-8) = 128/840 000 = 0.152 mm
Where you meet itShafts (deflection at bearings and gears), machine frames and beds, test-rig bed plates, cranes, bridges, PCB stiffness, MEMS cantilevers, atomic force microscope tips.

22Timoshenko beam (shear deformation)

ODElinearsystemboundary valueTimoshenko 1921
κ·G·A·(y′ − φ)′ + q = 0 EI·φ″ + κ·G·A·(y′ − φ) = 0
Point load, simple support: ymax = PL³/(48EI) + PL/(4κGA)

φ rotation of the cross-section (no longer equal to y′) · G shear modulus · κ shear coefficient (5/6 for rectangles)

Euler–Bernoulli ignores shear strain. For long slender beams that is fine; for short deep beams, sandwich panels, gear teeth and the high modes of shafts, shear adds deflection and lowers natural frequencies. The extra term grows as (h/L)² relative to bending.

A short, deep steel beam: 100 × 200 mm section, 0.6 m span, 100 kN at centre. Bending and shear deflections?I = 0.1 × 0.2³/12 = 6.67×10⁻⁵ m⁴, A = 0.02 m², G = 80 GPa Bending: PL³/(48EI) = 100e3 × 0.216/(48 × 210e9 × 6.67e-5) = 0.032 mm Shear: PL/(4κGA) = 100e3 × 0.6/(4 × 0.833 × 80e9 × 0.02) = 0.011 mm Shear adds 35 % — Euler–Bernoulli would be badly unconservative here.
Where you meet itGear-tooth deflection (mesh stiffness), short stubby shafts and spline teeth, sandwich and composite panels, deep girders, high-frequency vibration of beams, finite-element "thick beam" elements.

23Euler buckling of a column

ODElineareigenvalueEuler 1744
EI·y″ + P·y = 0 → Pcr = π²·EI/(K·L)²

P axial load · K effective-length factor (pinned–pinned 1, fixed–free 2, fixed–fixed 0.5, fixed–pinned 0.7)

A straight column under compression has the trivial solution y = 0 for every load — but at special loads a bent shape also satisfies equilibrium. It is an eigenvalue problem: y = sin(πx/L) works only when P = π²EI/L². Above that load the straight column is unstable and bows sideways suddenly, long before the material yields if the column is slender.

From the moment equation to the eigenvalue

Cut the bent column at x: the internal moment balances P·y, so EI·y″ = −P·y. With k² = P/EI, y = A sin kx + B cos kx. Pinned ends: y(0) = 0 → B = 0; y(L) = 0 → A sin kL = 0. A non-zero A needs kL = nπ, so P = n²π²EI/L². The lowest, n = 1, is the buckling load. Imperfections and eccentricity (Perry–Robertson, secant formula) lower the real failure load.

A 20 mm diameter steel push-rod, 1 m long, pinned at both ends. Buckling load versus squash load (yield 250 MPa)?I = π × 0.02⁴/64 = 7.85×10⁻⁹ m⁴ P_cr = π² × 210e9 × 7.85e-9/1² = 16.3 kN Squash load = A·σ_y = 314 mm² × 250 MPa = 78.5 kN → buckling governs by a factor of 4.8. Halve the length and P_cr rises fourfold to 65 kN.
Where you meet itPush-rods, hydraulic cylinder rods, screw jacks and lead screws, columns and struts, thin-walled panels (local buckling), drill strings, railway rails in hot weather.

24Beam vibration (Euler–Bernoulli dynamic)

PDElinearfourth orderDaniel Bernoulli 1742; Rayleigh · Part I, Day 13: Waves and Laplace
EI·∂⁴w/∂x⁴ + ρA·∂²w/∂t² = 0 ωn = (βnL)²·√(EI/(ρA·L⁴))

w(x,t) lateral displacement · ρA mass per length · βnL: simply supported nπ; cantilever 1.875, 4.694, 7.855; free–free 4.730, 7.853

Add inertia to the beam equation and bending waves appear. Unlike a string, the frequencies are not harmonics: for a cantilever they stand in the ratio 1 : 6.27 : 17.5. Each mode has its own shape with nodes — the information a modal test (impact hammer and accelerometers) measures.

A steel ruler, 300 mm overhang, 25 × 1 mm, clamped to a desk. First natural frequency?I = 0.025 × 0.001³/12 = 2.08×10⁻¹² m⁴, EI = 0.4375 N·m², ρA = 7850 × 25e-6 = 0.196 kg/m f₁ = (1.875²/2π)·√(EI/(ρA·L⁴)) = 0.5595 × √(0.4375/(0.196 × 0.0081)) = 9.3 Hz Second mode: 6.27 × 9.3 = 58 Hz — the buzz you hear when you twang it.
Where you meet itShafts and pipes (support spacing against flow-induced vibration), turbine and fan blades, tuning forks, MEMS resonators, machine beds, bridge decks, aircraft wings.

25Lamé equations (thick-walled cylinder)

ODElinearboundary valueLamé and Clapeyron 1833
d/dr[ (1/r)·d(r·u)/dr ] = 0 σr = A − B/r², σθ = A + B/r²

u(r) radial displacement · σr, σθ radial and hoop stress · A, B from the pressures at ri and ro

Equilibrium of a ring element plus Hooke's law gives an Euler-type ODE whose solution is u = C₁r + C₂/r. The hoop stress is largest at the bore and falls off as 1/r² — so making a cylinder thicker helps less and less. That is why gun barrels and high-pressure vessels use shrink fits or autofrettage to pre-compress the bore.

A hydraulic cylinder: bore radius 50 mm, outer radius 100 mm, 100 MPa internal pressure. Peak hoop stress? Compare with the thin-wall estimate.A = p·r_i²/(r_o² − r_i²) = 100 × 2500/7500 = 33.3 MPa, B = A·r_o² σ_θ(r_i) = A(1 + r_o²/r_i²) = 33.3 × 5 = 167 MPa; σ_θ(r_o) = 2A = 67 MPa σ_r(r_i) = −100 MPa. Thin-wall p·r_mean/t = 100 × 75/50 = 150 MPa — 10 % low at the bore.
Where you meet itHydraulic cylinders and accumulators, gun barrels, pressure vessels, interference (shrink) fits of gears and bearings on shafts, pipes, hubs and press-fitted bushes.

26Rotating disc stresses

ODElinearboundary valueStodola 1903
d(r·σr)/dr − σθ + ρ·ω²·r² = 0 solid disc centre: σmax = (3 + ν)/8 · ρω²R²

ω angular speed · ρ density · ν Poisson's ratio · R outer radius

Centrifugal force on each ring element is balanced by radial and hoop stresses. Stress scales with (rim speed)², so the limit of any rotor is its tip speed: about 400–500 m/s for steel discs. A central hole doubles the hoop stress at the bore — which is why turbine discs are thick at the hub.

A solid steel disc, R = 250 mm, at 10 000 rpm (ν = 0.3). Peak stress? With a small central bore?ω = 1047 rad/s, rim speed ωR = 262 m/s σ_max = (3.3/8) × 7850 × 1047² × 0.25² = 222 MPa at the centre (σ_r = σ_θ) Small bore: σ_θ ≈ (3 + ν)/4 · ρω²R² = 444 MPa — twice as much.
Where you meet itTurbine and compressor discs, flywheels (energy storage limit), grinding wheels (burst speed), centrifuges, gear blanks and high-speed accessory gears, rotor burst containment.

27Gear-mesh dynamics (time-varying mesh stiffness)

ODElineartime-varyingHarris 1958; Özgüven and Houser 1988
me·x″ + c·x′ + km(t)·x = F + km(t)·e(t) km(t) = k̄·(1 + α·cos(z·Ω·t)), fmesh = z·n/60

x dynamic transmission error along the line of action · me equivalent mass J₁J₂/(J₁r₂² + J₂r₁²) · km mesh stiffness, varying as one or two tooth pairs share load · e(t) profile errors

As gears roll, the number of tooth pairs in contact alternates (between 1 and 2 for a contact ratio of 1.6), so the mesh stiffness pulses at the mesh frequency. That is both a forcing (static transmission error) and a parametric excitation (Mathieu, equation 18). Resonances appear when the mesh frequency or its harmonics hit the mesh natural frequency, and parametric instabilities near twice it — the source of gear whine and of sidebands in gearbox vibration spectra.

A pinion with 23 teeth runs at 6000 rpm. Mesh stiffness 2.8×10⁸ N/m, equivalent mass 0.5 kg. Mesh frequency and the speed for mesh resonance?f_mesh = 23 × 6000/60 = 2300 Hz f_n = √(k/m_e)/2π = √(5.6e8)/6.283 = 3766 Hz Mesh resonance when z·n/60 = f_n → n = 3766 × 60/23 = 9830 rpm; 2nd harmonic hits it at 4910 rpm. On a vibration spectrum: peaks at 2300 Hz and 4600 Hz with sidebands at ± shaft speed (100 Hz).
Where you meet itGear whine and noise, accessory gearbox vibration signatures, transmission-error testing, gear diagnostics (sidebands, tooth cracks reduce stiffness), helical gears (higher contact ratio smooths k(t)).

28Viscoelasticity (Maxwell and Kelvin–Voigt)

ODElinearfirst orderMaxwell 1867; Kelvin 1865; Voigt 1892
Maxwell (spring + dashpot in series): dε/dt = (1/E)·dσ/dt + σ/η → stress relaxation σ = σ₀·e−t/τ
Kelvin–Voigt (in parallel): σ = E·ε + η·dε/dt → creep ε = (σ₀/E)·(1 − e−t/τ), τ = η/E

E spring modulus · η dashpot viscosity · τ relaxation / retardation time

Rubbers, polymers, gaskets, wood, bitumen and biological tissue are part spring, part fluid. Hold them stretched and the stress relaxes (Maxwell); hang a weight and they creep towards a limit (Kelvin). Real materials combine both (standard linear solid, Burgers model). The time constant decides whether a part behaves like a solid or a liquid for your load: Silly Putty bounces but flows.

A bolted joint with a polymer gasket: preload 20 kN, gasket relaxation time 1000 h. Preload after 500 h?σ = σ₀·e^(−t/τ) = 20 × e^(−0.5) = 12.1 kN — 40 % lost. Re-torque after first run-in, use a stiffer gasket, or longer bolts (more stored stretch per µm lost).
Where you meet itGaskets and seals, rubber engine and equipment mounts, bolted joints with soft layers, polymer gears and bushes, tyres, biological tissue, asphalt and concrete creep.

29Norton creep law

ODEnonlinearfirst orderNorton 1929; Bailey
dε/dt = A·σn·e−Q/RT bolt relaxation: dσ/dt = −E·A·σn → σ = [σ₀1−n + (n − 1)·E·A·t]1/(1−n)

n stress exponent (3–8 for metals) · Q activation energy · T absolute temperature

Above about 0.4 of their melting temperature, metals deform slowly and permanently under constant stress. The steady (secondary) creep rate is a power of stress, so a small rise in stress or temperature shortens life dramatically. Combine with a strain limit and you get creep life; put it inside a bolt at fixed length and you get relaxation of preload.

A steam-pipe steel has n = 5 and creeps at 1×10⁻⁸ per hour at 100 MPa. What if the stress rises to 120 MPa? Strain in 100 000 h?Rate ratio = (120/100)⁵ = 2.49 → 2.49×10⁻⁸ per h Strain in 100 000 h = 2.49e-8 × 1e5 = 0.25 % (a common 1 % design limit leaves margin) A 20 % stress increase cut the life by 60 %.
Where you meet itTurbine blades and discs, boiler and steam pipes, bolts in hot flanges, solder joints in electronics, lead and polymer components even at room temperature, glacier flow.

30Paris law (fatigue crack growth)

ODEnonlinearfirst orderParis and Erdogan 1963
da/dN = C·(ΔK)m, ΔK = Y·Δσ·√(πa) → N = ∫ da / (C·(Y·Δσ·√(πa))m)

a crack length · N cycles · ΔK stress-intensity range (MPa√m) · C, m material constants (steel: m ≈ 3) · Y geometry factor

Once a fatigue crack exists, its growth per cycle is a power of the stress-intensity range. Because ΔK grows with √a, the crack accelerates: most of the life is spent while the crack is tiny. That is the basis of damage-tolerant design — inspect at intervals shorter than the time to grow from the smallest detectable crack to the critical one.

A steel part: C = 1×10⁻¹¹ (m/cycle, MPa√m), m = 3, Y = 1.12, Δσ = 100 MPa. Cycles to grow a crack from 1 mm to 10 mm?YΔσ√π = 112 × 1.772 = 198.5 N = 2/((m − 2)·C·(YΔσ√π)^m) · (a₀^−½ − a_f^−½) = 2/(1e-11 × 7.82e6) × (31.6 − 10.0) = 5.5×10⁵ cycles From 1 to 2 mm takes 2.1×10⁵ of those — over a third of the life in the first millimetre. At 6000 rpm (one cycle per revolution) that is only about 1.5 hours.
Where you meet itInspection intervals for aircraft (damage tolerance), rotating shafts and gears with cracks, pressure vessels, welded structures, railway axles, wind-turbine blades.
