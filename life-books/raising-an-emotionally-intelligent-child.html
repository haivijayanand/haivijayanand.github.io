Raising an Emotionally Intelligent Child

John Gottman · first published 1997 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

When parents treat a child's feelings as a chance to connect and teach, rather than a problem to stop, children learn to understand and manage their emotions, which helps them in friendships, school and life.

Summary

John Gottman, a psychologist known for decades of research on families, wrote this book with Joan DeClaire to answer a practical question: what can parents do to help children handle feelings well? His answer comes from watching many families closely over years. Being clever or well behaved is not enough; children also need emotional skills, and parents are their first teachers.

Gottman describes four broad ways parents respond to a child's emotions. Dismissing parents brush feelings aside or try to distract the child. Disapproving parents criticise or punish the feelings themselves. Laissez-faire parents accept every emotion but offer little guidance or limits. Emotion coaching parents accept the feelings and also help the child learn what to do with them.

Emotion coaching is the heart of the book, and it follows five steps. First, notice the child's emotion, especially when it is still small. Second, see that moment as a chance to grow closer and to teach, not as a nuisance. Third, listen with empathy and let the child know the feeling makes sense. Fourth, help the child find words to name what they feel. Fifth, set clear limits on behaviour while helping the child work out a better way to solve the problem.

A key point is that all feelings are acceptable, but not all actions are. A child may be furious with a sibling, yet hitting is still not allowed. Separating the feeling from the behaviour lets parents be both warm and firm.

Gottman reports that coached children tend to calm themselves more easily, get along better with other children and do better at school. He also stresses the important role fathers play, and how stress and conflict between parents can affect children.

The approach asks for patience, not perfection. Parents will miss moments, but steady effort builds trust that lasts into the teenage years.

306 words

One-line takeaway: This week, when your child is upset, pause before fixing or scolding, name the feeling you see out loud, such as 'you seem really disappointed', and listen for a minute before talking about what to do next.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What is the main skill the book encourages parents to teach?

    Answer B. The book focuses on helping children recognise and handle their feelings, with parents acting as emotion coaches.

  2. What do dismissing parents tend to do with a child's sadness?

    Answer A. Dismissing parents treat negative feelings as unimportant and try to make them go away quickly.

  3. How do laissez-faire parents differ from emotion coaches?

    Answer C. Laissez-faire parents are accepting but do not help children learn how to act on or solve the problems behind their feelings.

  4. Which parenting style criticises or punishes the child for having certain feelings?

    Answer D. Disapproving parents judge the emotions themselves, not just the behaviour.

  5. What is the first step of emotion coaching?

    Answer B. Coaching begins with noticing what the child is feeling, ideally before it grows large.

  6. How does the book suggest parents view a child's emotional moment?

    Answer D. Gottman frames emotional moments as opportunities to connect and to teach.

  7. Why does the book encourage helping children name their feelings?

    Answer A. Labelling emotions helps children make sense of what is happening inside them and soothe themselves.

  8. Which statement best matches the book's view on feelings and behaviour?

    Answer C. The book separates feelings, which are always allowed, from behaviour, which can still have limits.

  9. What benefit does Gottman link to emotion-coached children?

    Answer B. His research associates emotion coaching with better self-calming, friendships and school performance.

  10. What does the book say about parents who sometimes miss emotional moments?

    Answer D. The approach asks for patience and consistency rather than getting every moment right.
