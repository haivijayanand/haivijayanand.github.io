1The world around a business

The business environment is everything outside a business that affects it: customers, competitors, the weather, the government, new technology and people's habits.

A business can control its own prices, products and staff. It cannot control the rain, a new tax, or a rival opening next door. But it can prepare for them.

1Environmental uncertainty (Duncan, 1972)

Duncan classified perceived uncertainty along two dimensions: complexity (number and dissimilarity of relevant factors) and dynamism (rate of change). Structure should follow: stable-simple environments suit mechanistic organisations; dynamic-complex ones need organic structures, boundary-spanning roles, planning and forecasting (Burns & Stalker; Lawrence & Lorsch on differentiation and integration).

2Scanning and scenario planning

Aguilar (1967) distinguished undirected viewing, conditioned viewing, informal search and formal search. Effective scanning has an owner, a cadence and a link to decisions. Scenario planning (Shell, Wack 1985) builds 3–4 internally consistent futures from two critical uncertainties, then tests strategies for robustness rather than forecasting a point.

1. Focal question and horizon 2. Driving forces (PESTEL) 3. Rank by impact × uncertainty 4. Pick two critical uncertainties → 2×2 5. Write narratives 6. Signposts + robust and contingent moves

3Limits of PESTEL

Lists without weights are inert; factors interact (a regulatory change is often driven by a social one); and the frame is static. Combine it with five-forces analysis of industry structure (Ch. 50), stakeholder mapping (Ch. 05) and real options thinking (Ch. 45). In India, state-level variation in regulation and policy (power tariffs, labour rules, incentives) often matters more than national averages.
