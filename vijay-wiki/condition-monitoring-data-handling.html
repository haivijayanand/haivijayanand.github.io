Handling Long-Duration Test Data: Volumes, Reduction, Storage and Trending

K Vijay Anand · Vijay_Wiki quick-reference article · 24 November 2026
Data acquisitionCondition monitoringData managementTrending

A thousand-hour endurance test with vibration sampled at tens of kilohertz on many channels produces terabytes of raw data. Keeping everything is expensive and nobody looks at it; keeping too little loses the evidence when something fails. A good plan keeps compact features continuously, raw bursts periodically, and full raw data around events.

Key takeaways

  • Raw volume = sample rate × channels × bytes per sample × time: 25.6 kS/s × 16 channels × 4 bytes ≈ 5.9 GB per hour.
  • Store features (RMS, peak, crest factor, kurtosis, band RMS, order amplitudes) every 0.5–1 s: thousands of times smaller.
  • Add periodic raw bursts (e.g. 10 s every 10–30 min) and event-triggered raw capture with pre-trigger.
  • Use self-describing binary or columnar formats, consistent file naming, synchronised clocks and metadata with every file.
  • Set alert and alarm thresholds from each channel's own baseline statistics, and watch the trend slope, not only the level.

3. Formats, naming and metadata

  • Use binary formats with embedded metadata (e.g. TDMS, HDF5, vendor formats) for raw data; columnar formats (Parquet) or a database (SQLite, PostgreSQL) for features.
  • Name files with test, rig, date-time in ISO 8601 and type: TEST01_RIGA_20261124T0830_raw.h5.
  • Store with every file: sample rate, channel names, sensor serials and sensitivities, units, orientation, speed reference, software version.
  • Synchronise clocks (NTP or PTP) across acquisition systems so process and vibration data align.
  • Keep two copies on different media; checksum on transfer; write once, never edit raw files.

Rate of changeAlarm on slope, e.g. more than 20 % rise in 24 h

Practical rule

Decide before the test what will be kept, at what rate, with which thresholds, and who looks at it each day. Data nobody reviews provides no protection.
