Every rolling bearing reduces friction the same way: two hardened rings separated by rolling elements that are spaced by a cage. The geometry of those four parts is exactly what sets the defect frequencies in the last tab.

Ball bearings against roller bearings

The rolling element shape decides the contact geometry, and that decides load capacity, speed limit and misalignment tolerance.

A ball touches each raceway at a point that spreads into a small ellipse under load. A roller touches along a line. The line contact carries far more load for the same envelope but has higher friction, a lower speed limit and much less tolerance of misalignment — which is why a line-contact bearing is nearly always paired with a self-aligning or spherical arrangement when shaft deflection is expected.

Decode a bearing designation

ISO 15 / ISO 355 style basic numbers with SKF-family prefixes and suffixes. Try 6205-2RS1/C3, 22220 EK/W33, NU 2220 ECM, 32210 J2/Q, 7208 BECBP, 608.

How the basic number is built

Characteristic defect frequencies

A local defect produces an impact every time a rolling element passes over it. The repetition rate is fixed by kinematics, so each fault location has its own signature frequency.

Bearing geometry
Estimate geometry from bore and OD

Uses Pd = (d+D)/2, Bd ≈ 0.30(D−d), Z ≈ πPd/(1.6·Bd). Good to a few percent for deep groove ball bearings only — use it to sanity-check, not to report.

The formulas

With shaft rotational frequency S in Hz, and writing r = (Bd/Pd)·cosθ for the geometry ratio, the standard inner-rotating case gives:

FTF = (S/2)·(1 − r) cage / train frequency BPFO = (Z/2)·S·(1 − r) = Z · FTF ball pass frequency, outer race BPFI = (Z/2)·S·(1 + r) = Z · (S − FTF) BSF = (Pd/2Bd)·S·(1 − r²) ball spin frequency (element rotation) BDF = 2 · BSF ball defect frequency, the usual observed line

If the outer ring rotates instead, the cage runs with the outer ring and the two ball-pass expressions swap their signs: FTF = (S/2)(1+r), BPFO = (Z/2)S(1+r), BPFI = (Z/2)S(1−r). The BSF expression is unchanged. The tool applies whichever case you select.

Every one of these is a relative speed. S is the difference in rotational speed between the two rings, which for a normal machine is simply the shaft speed.

Why 2×BSF and not BSF

A ball spins about its own axis at BSF. A spall on the ball strikes a raceway once per ball revolution — but it strikes the inner race and then the outer race within one revolution, so the impact rate seen at the housing is usually 2×BSF. A defect that only reaches one raceway, or an element that skids, will show BSF instead. Look for both.

Rule-of-thumb estimates when geometry is unavailable

For a typical bearing with 8 to 12 elements and r near 0.2, the ratios collapse to usable approximations:

FTF ≈ 0.38 · S to 0.42 · S BPFO ≈ 0.4 · Z · S BPFI ≈ 0.6 · Z · S BPFO + BPFI = Z · S ← always exactly true, use it as a check

That last identity is the fastest audit of any bearing frequency table: add the outer and inner ball-pass orders and you must get the element count.

Reading the signature

Frequency tells you where the defect is. The pattern around that frequency tells you how the load reaches it, and the envelope tells you how far it has progressed.

The disappearance of a clean defect frequency is not recovery. In stage 4 the spall has spread far enough that impacts are no longer periodic, and the spectrum flattens. Judge it on overall level and trend, never on the defect line alone.

Practical cautions

IssueWhat to do
Defect frequencies are non-integerThat is the point. A line at exactly 3× or 4× shaft speed is far more likely blade pass, coupling or gear mesh than a bearing. Bearing lines land between the orders.
SlipReal cages slip 1–2% under light or variable load, so measured lines sit slightly below calculated ones. Allow a ±1–2% search band when you set alarms.
Contact angle changes with thrustDeep groove bearings develop a contact angle under axial preload, which shifts every frequency. Angular contact and tapered bearings shift with load too. Calculate a band, not a line.
ResolutionTo separate BPFI sidebands at 1× you need line spacing well below the shaft frequency. For a 30 Hz shaft, aim for 3200 lines or more over the chosen span.
Two bearings, same defect frequencyCommon on a gearbox where identical bearings sit on the same shaft. Separate them by sensor location and phase, not by frequency.
Gearbox shaftsUse the speed of the shaft that bearing is on, not the input speed. Apply the gear ratio first.
E-suffix and cage variantsA reinforced bearing of the same basic number can have a different element count. Confirm Z from the maker's data before writing the alarm band.

Worked check

A 6205 on a 1780 rpm shaft, inner ring rotating: S = 29.67 Hz, r = 7.94/39.04 = 0.2034.

FTF = (29.67/2)(1 − 0.2034) = 11.82 Hz = 0.398 × BPFO = (9/2)(29.67)(1 − 0.2034) = 106.4 Hz = 3.585 × BPFI = (9/2)(29.67)(1 + 0.2034) = 160.7 Hz = 5.415 × BSF = (39.04/15.88)(29.67)(1 − 0.2034²) = 69.9 Hz = 2.357 × 2×BSF = 139.8 Hz = 4.714 × check: 3.585 + 5.415 = 9.000 = Z ✓ check: FTF/S = 0.398, inside the expected 0.38–0.42 band ✓
Calculated values are kinematic ideals. Confirm Z, Bd, Pd and θ against the manufacturer's data before setting alarm bands on a production machine.
