Built to Last

Jim Collins and Jerry I. Porras · first published 1994 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Enduring great companies hold a fixed core ideology while relentlessly stimulating change and progress in everything else.

Summary

Jim Collins and Jerry Porras spent six years studying what they called visionary companies: firms that had led their industries for generations and were widely admired. They paired each one with a solid but less outstanding rival from the same era and industry, such as Merck with Pfizer or Hewlett-Packard with Texas Instruments, and asked what truly separated them. Over many decades the visionary group delivered stock returns far above both its rivals and the general market.

Their first finding overturns several myths. These companies seldom began with a brilliant product idea, and they did not depend on charismatic, larger-than-life leaders. Their founders were more like clock builders than time tellers: instead of personally announcing the right answer, they designed organisations that could keep producing good answers long after they had gone.

At the centre of each enduring company sits a core ideology, made up of a few deeply held values and a purpose that goes beyond making money. Profit matters, but it is treated like oxygen, needed for life rather than being the point of life. The specific values differ from firm to firm; what counts is that they are authentic and consistently honoured.

The second half of the formula is a restless drive for progress. Visionary companies set bold, long-range goals that energise people, keep experimenting and retain what works, and refuse to treat any level of performance as good enough.

To protect the core, they build strong, almost cult-like cultures that suit believers and push out those who do not fit. They also tend to grow leaders from within rather than hiring outside saviours, which keeps the ideology intact across generations.

The authors urge managers to reject the tyranny of choosing either stability or change. Great companies embrace both, preserving their core while changing practices, and they align every system, policy and incentive so that the ideology is lived, not merely written down.

316 words

One-line takeaway: Build an organisation, not a personality: anchor it in timeless values and purpose, then change everything else boldly and continuously.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. How did Collins and Porras design their research?

    Answer B. Each visionary company was paired with a good but less outstanding competitor so the differences between them could be identified.

  2. What does the 'clock building, not time telling' idea mean?

    Answer C. A clock builder creates a lasting mechanism, whereas a time teller relies on personal brilliance that ends when they leave.

  3. Which belief did the study find to be a myth?

    Answer A. Many visionary companies started without a brilliant product idea and found their way through trial and adaptation.

  4. What two elements make up a company's core ideology in the book?

    Answer D. The authors define core ideology as enduring values plus a fundamental reason for existing that goes beyond profit.

  5. How do visionary companies view profit, according to the authors?

    Answer B. Profit is compared to oxygen: essential for life, but not the reason the company exists.

  6. What is a BHAG?

    Answer A. A Big Hairy Audacious Goal is a clear, compelling and ambitious target that stimulates progress.

  7. What do the authors mean by the 'Genius of the AND'?

    Answer C. Visionary companies refuse forced either-or choices and pursue seemingly contradictory goals together.

  8. Why do visionary companies have 'cult-like' cultures?

    Answer D. A strong, demanding culture protects the core values so that the ideology survives over time.

  9. Where do visionary companies usually find their top leaders?

    Answer B. Home-grown leaders understand and carry the core ideology, ensuring continuity across generations.

  10. What does 'try a lot of stuff and keep what works' describe?

    Answer C. Many visionary companies advanced by running many small experiments and building on the ones that succeeded.
