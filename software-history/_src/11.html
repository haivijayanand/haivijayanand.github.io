01The requirement: the page before 1985

For a century, printed text was set — first in hot metal on Linotype machines (from 1886), then from the 1960s on phototypesetters that exposed characters onto photographic paper. A typical brochure or newsletter went through a chain of specialists:

Writer's typed manuscript→Typesetter re-keys it with codes→Galley proofs, corrections→Paste-up artist cuts and waxes columns onto boards→Camera room makes film→Plate and press

Every correction meant another trip to the typesetter. Typesetting systems from Compugraphic, Linotype and others cost tens of thousands of dollars and used their own incompatible codes. Small organisations — clubs, schools, shops, departments — could not afford typeset documents at all and used typewriters and photocopiers.

The phrase "desktop publishing" is credited to Paul Brainerd, founder of Aldus, who needed a name for what PageMaker made possible.

02Try it: write a page in PostScript

PostScript is a complete programming language. A page is a program; the printer runs it. It uses a stack: numbers are pushed first, then an operator uses them (100 200 moveto). The origin is the bottom-left corner and units are points (1/72 inch). Edit the program and press Render, or load an example.

Supported here: newpath moveto lineto rlineto rmoveto closepath stroke fill setgray setrgbcolor setlinewidth arc translate rotate scale gsave grestore findfont scalefont setfont show — a tiny subset of the real language, which has hundreds of operators.

04See it: what a typesetter controls

Desktop publishing put typographic controls that had belonged to trained compositors into everyone's hands. Change the settings and watch the paragraph.

The AMAGB endurance programme generated thousands of hours of process and vibration records. Turning those records into a readable test report — with tables, plots, captions and references laid out across columns and pages — is exactly the job desktop publishing was invented to do. Typography decides whether the reader finds the result easy or tiring to read.

Watch the headline: with kerning off, pairs such as AV, To and WA leave visible gaps. Justified text without hyphenation in a narrow column produces "rivers" of white space — the problem InDesign's paragraph composer was built to reduce.

05Version explorer

Blue chips: the pioneers (PageMaker, Ventura, Quark, Publisher). Amber chips: InDesign and today's tools.

Leadership changed hands twice, both times on a platform shift. PageMaker led on the early Mac; QuarkXPress took over with precision and XTensions; InDesign arrived with native Mac OS X support and PDF/transparency handling while QuarkXPress 5 still ran only in the old Mac environment — and printers and publishers moved across in the early 2000s.

06The font wars

  • Type 1 (1984): Adobe's high-quality PostScript font format with hinting. Its specification was secret and encrypted; other makers could only produce the simpler Type 3 fonts.
  • Royal/TrueType (1991): Apple, wanting independence from Adobe licensing, developed TrueType and shared it with Microsoft. It shipped in Mac System 7 and Windows 3.1 (Episode 02). Adobe responded by publishing the Type 1 specification and releasing Adobe Type Manager (1989) to render Type 1 on screen.
  • OpenType (1996–2000): Microsoft and Adobe ended the war with a joint format that can hold either kind of outline, plus thousands of glyphs and layout rules — essential for Indian scripts.
  • Retirement: Adobe ended support for Type 1 fonts in its applications in January 2023. Documents built on old Type 1 fonts must be converted or re-set.

07Indian-language desktop publishing

Latin text is a simple string of letters. Indian scripts are abugidas: a consonant carries a vowel sign that may sit before, after, above, below or around it, and consonants join into conjuncts. A computer must store the letters in spoken order but draw them in a different visual order and shape.

See it: what is stored vs what is drawn

Old "font-hack" documents stored the visual glyph sequence of one particular font in ordinary ASCII codes. They print correctly only with that font, cannot be searched or sorted, and break when the font changes. Converting such archives to Unicode is still a common job in Indian newspapers and government offices.

09Retirements

  • PageMaker: Adobe bought Aldus in 1994. PageMaker 7.0 (2001) was the last version; Adobe ended development in 2004 and moved users to InDesign.
  • Ventura Publisher / Corel Ventura: Corel bought Ventura in 1993; Corel Ventura 10 (2002) was the last release.
  • Microsoft Publisher: released in 1991 for Windows. Microsoft announced its end of life in 2024; Microsoft 365 subscribers lost access after 1 October 2026 and support for perpetual versions ended on 13 October 2026. Microsoft points users to Word and PowerPoint, and .PUB files must be converted.
  • Separate Affinity apps: Canva bought Affinity's maker Serif in March 2024 and on 30 October 2025 replaced Affinity Designer, Photo and Publisher with one free Affinity app.
  • Still going: InDesign (Creative Cloud), QuarkXPress (annual releases), Scribus, and the new free Affinity.

11Lessons for engineers

  1. A revolution needs a complete chain. The Mac, the LaserWriter, PostScript and PageMaker were each limited alone; together they replaced an industry.
  2. Device independence wins. Describe the result, not the machine — PostScript's idea lives on in PDF and SVG.
  3. Store meaning, not appearance. Unicode stores letters, the font decides shapes; font-hack text stored shapes and lost its meaning.
  4. Platform transitions are when leaders fall. Quark missed Mac OS X; InDesign was ready.
