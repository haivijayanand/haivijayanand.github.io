The 5 Love Languages

Gary Chapman · first published 1992 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

People give and receive love in different ways, and a relationship thrives when each partner learns to express love in the way the other most naturally feels it.

Summary

Gary Chapman spent many years as a marriage counsellor and kept meeting couples who sincerely loved each other yet still felt unloved. His explanation is simple: each of us has a main way of feeling loved, a kind of emotional language, and we tend to show love in our own language rather than our partner's. One spouse works late to provide, while the other only longs for an evening together, and both end up hurt.

Chapman describes five such languages. Words of affirmation means sincere praise, encouragement and kind words. Quality time means giving someone your full, undistracted attention, whether in a real conversation or a shared activity. Receiving gifts is not about cost but about the thought behind a small token. Acts of service means easing the other person's load, such as cooking a meal or fixing what is broken. Physical touch includes holding hands, a hug or simply sitting close.

He uses the picture of an emotional love tank. When it is full, a person feels secure and can handle disagreements calmly. When it runs empty, small problems feel huge and criticism stings. He also points out that the early thrill of falling in love fades, usually within about two years, and after that lasting love becomes a choice we make each day.

To find your partner's language, Chapman suggests noticing what they ask for most, what they complain about most, and how they usually show love to others. Your own language is often revealed by what hurts you most when it is missing.

The message is practical and hopeful. Speaking an unfamiliar language may feel awkward at first, but it can be learned like any skill. Chapman applies the same idea to children, friends and colleagues, reminding parents that every child needs all five, while one usually matters most to them.

One-line takeaway: This week, guess the love language of one person in your family, then do one small thing each day in that language and notice how they respond.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What is the central idea of the book?

    Answer B. Chapman argues that each person has a primary love language and that love lands best when expressed in that language.

  2. Which of these is NOT one of Chapman's five love languages?

    Answer C. The five are words of affirmation, quality time, receiving gifts, acts of service and physical touch; shared finances is not one of them.

  3. What image does Chapman use for a person's sense of being loved?

    Answer A. He speaks of an emotional love tank that is either full or running empty.

  4. What happens, according to the book, when someone's love tank is empty?

    Answer B. An empty tank leaves a person feeling insecure, so ordinary difficulties loom larger.

  5. Why do sincere couples often still feel unloved?

    Answer C. Love expressed in the wrong language can go unnoticed even when it is genuine.

  6. For someone whose language is receiving gifts, what matters most about a gift?

    Answer D. Chapman stresses that a gift is a visible symbol of love; cost is not the point.

  7. What does Chapman say about the early 'in love' feeling?

    Answer B. He notes the initial euphoria fades, typically within about two years, after which love must be chosen.

  8. Which is a suggested clue to discovering your own love language?

    Answer A. The absence of your primary language tends to cause the deepest hurt, revealing what you need.

  9. A spouse who feels most loved when their partner cooks dinner or handles a chore likely speaks which language?

    Answer D. Easing someone's burden through helpful deeds is the language of acts of service.

  10. What does the book say about speaking a love language that does not come naturally to you?

    Answer C. Chapman treats love languages as learnable skills that grow easier with effort.
