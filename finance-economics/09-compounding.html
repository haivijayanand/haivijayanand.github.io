Level 1 · For school students

Compounding is growth that builds on itself, like a snowball rolling downhill.

1What is compounding?

Compounding means earning growth not only on what you started with, but also on the growth you have already earned. The bigger it gets, the faster it grows.

Roll a small snowball down a snowy hill. As it rolls it picks up snow, and because it is now bigger, it picks up even more snow on the next turn. Money in a bank works the same way: you earn interest, the interest is added to your money, and next year you earn interest on that too. (You met this in Topic 02, where simple and compound interest raced each other.)

3The magic number 72

To find out how long it takes for something to double, divide 72 by the yearly growth rate.

4Start early: the story of Asha and Bala

Asha saves ₹1,000 every year from age 15 to 25, then stops. Bala starts at 25 and saves ₹1,000 every year until 60. Both earn 10% a year.

AshaSaves for 10 years: ₹10,000 in total
BalaSaves for 35 years: ₹35,000 in total
At 60, who has more?Asha! About ₹4.9 lakh, against Bala's ₹3.0 lakh. Her money had 10 extra years to snowball. Time matters more than amount.

5Compounding is everywhere

  • Learning: each new idea makes the next easier to understand. Getting just 1% better every day makes you about 37 times better after a year (1.01365 ≈ 37.8).
  • Nature: bacteria and plants grow by doubling, until they run out of food or space.
  • Countries: an economy growing 7% a year doubles in about 10 years, which is how countries escape poverty.
  • Trees and habits: small, regular actions add up to big results over time.

6The dark side of compounding

  • Debt grows the same way. An unpaid credit-card bill can double in under two years.
  • Inflation compounds too: prices rising 6% a year double in about 12 years, so money kept idle loses value (Topic 01).
  • Bad habits compound: skipping a little study every day adds up just as fast as good habits do.

8Check yourself

Level 2 · For working people

The formula, the rules of thumb, and how compounding works for you (time, saving) or against you (fees, inflation, debt). This is educational only, not financial advice.

1The formula, and quick rules

Future value: FV = P × (1 + r/m)^(m·t) r = annual rate, m = compounding periods a year, t = years Doubling time: ≈ 72 / r(%) (69.3 / r for continuous compounding; rule of 114 for tripling, 144 for quadrupling)
  • Compounding has three levers: rate, time and regularity. Time is the exponent, which is why it dominates.
  • Always compare effective annual yields (Topic 02) and real, after-tax returns (Topics 01 and 06).
  • Rates compound multiplicatively: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover.

2Calculator: early starter vs late starter

3Costs compound too: the fee drag

A difference of 1% a year in fees sounds small. Compounded over decades it can eat a fifth or more of your final wealth. That is why low-cost index funds and direct plans matter (Topic 07).

4Inflation: compounding in reverse

  • At 6% inflation, ₹1 lakh of today's spending will need about ₹3.2 lakh in 20 years, and about ₹5.7 lakh in 30 years (Topic 01).
  • Your real return is what compounds your purchasing power: 7% nominal with 6% inflation is only about 1% real, which doubles your purchasing power in about 70 years, not 10.
  • Plan goals in today's money, then inflate them. Plan investments in real returns.

5Debt: compounding against you

  • Credit cards at about 3.5% a month compound to roughly 51% a year. A ₹50,000 balance paying only the minimum can take years to clear and cost more than the original purchases.
  • Home loans: early EMIs are mostly interest (Topic 02). Prepaying in the first years saves the most, because it removes principal that would have compounded for decades.
  • The order of priorities: clear high-interest debt before investing. Paying off a 36% loan is a guaranteed 36% "return".

6Taxes and compounding

  • Tax deferral lets the whole amount keep compounding: equity gains are taxed only when you sell (Topic 06), while FD interest is taxed every year.
  • EEE products such as PPF (exempt when you invest, while it grows and when you withdraw) compound fully tax-free, which is why 7.1% in PPF can beat a higher taxable FD rate.
  • Frequent switching between funds resets the clock and triggers tax, interrupting the compounding.

7Compounding and the country

  • Small differences in growth become huge differences in living standards. At 7% real growth, income per person doubles in about 10 years; at 4% it takes 18. Over 30 years, that gap is roughly 8 times versus 3 times today's level.
  • South Korea and China compounded high growth for decades to transform their economies. India's ambition of "Viksit Bharat by 2047" is, at its core, a compounding target.
  • Compounding works on debt too: a government whose interest rate exceeds its growth rate sees debt snowball (Topic 08).
  • Population and climate: carbon emissions accumulate rather than vanish, so delayed action compounds the cost.

8Sensible habits

  • Start early, even with small amounts. Automate SIPs and increase them with each pay rise (a step-up).
  • Keep costs low, stay invested, and avoid interrupting compounding through panic selling.
  • Kill high-interest debt first.
  • Think in real, after-tax terms, and review once a year, not every day.

Level 3 · Review for experts

Continuous compounding and log returns, arithmetic vs geometric means and volatility drag, present-value machinery for retirement, growth convergence, and why humans misjudge exponentials.

1Continuous compounding and log returns

lim (1 + r/m)^(m·t) = e^(r·t) as m → ∞ continuous rate ρ = ln(1 + r) Log returns add over time: ln(W_T / W_0) = Σ ln(1 + r_t) doubling time = ln 2 / ρ ≈ 69.3 / ρ(%)
  • The rule of 72 is a convenient approximation: 72 has many divisors and corrects for the error of 69.3 at typical rates (around 8%).
  • Log returns are additive over time and roughly normal for diversified indices over short horizons, which makes them the natural unit for compounding risk.

2Arithmetic vs geometric mean: volatility drag

Geometric (compound) growth ≈ μ − σ²/2 Median wealth after T years ≈ W₀ · e^{(μ − σ²/2)·T} +50% then −50% → arithmetic mean 0%, geometric mean −13.4%, wealth −25%
  • Reducing volatility at the same arithmetic return raises compound growth. This is the core of the case for diversification and rebalancing (Topic 07) and against leverage.
  • Leveraged daily-reset products and F&O positions suffer amplified drag, a mechanical reason long-term retail outcomes are poor.
  • The Kelly criterion maximises long-run geometric growth: f* = μ/σ² for a lognormal asset. Over-betting beyond f* lowers growth and raises ruin risk.

3Present value and annuities: the retirement problem

PV of a growing annuity (first payment C, growth g, discount r, n periods) = C · [1 − ((1+g)/(1+r))^n] / (r − g) Perpetuity: C/r Growing perpetuity: C/(r − g) Required SIP: corpus × r_m / ((1 + r_m)^N − 1)

This is a deterministic sketch: sequence-of-returns risk, longevity risk and taxes all matter. A real plan stress-tests the numbers, for example with Monte Carlo simulation, and keeps a margin of safety.

4Compounding in growth economics

  • "Once one starts to think about [growth], it is hard to think about anything else" (Lucas, 1988). Small differences in g, compounded, explain most cross-country income gaps.
  • Convergence speed: in Solow-type models, the gap to steady state closes at about 2% a year (Barro and Sala-i-Martin), a half-life of about 35 years. Catching up is slow without rising TFP growth.
  • Kaldor's facts and balanced growth: exponential growth is the norm for GDP per capita at the frontier (about 2% a year since the 1870s in the US).
  • Exponential vs logistic: real processes such as epidemics, technology adoption and UPI growth (Topic 04) follow S-curves. Extrapolating the exponential phase is a classic forecasting error.

5Why people misjudge compounding

  • Exponential-growth bias (Stango and Zinman, 2009): people linearise compound processes. They underestimate loan costs and the growth of savings, and borrow more as a result.
  • Present bias and hyperbolic discounting (Laibson, 1997): preferring immediate rewards undermines long-horizon compounding. Commitment devices (auto-SIPs, lock-ins, EPF/NPS defaults) help.
  • Interrupted compounding: missing the market's best few days, often clustered near the worst ones, sharply reduces long-run returns. Selling in panic is a compounding killer.
  • A misattributed quote: "compound interest is the eighth wonder of the world" is widely attributed to Einstein, without any reliable source.
