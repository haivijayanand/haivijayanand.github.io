Hold Me Tight

Sue Johnson · first published 2008 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Lasting love is an emotional bond between two people, and couples grow closer when they learn to recognise the anxious patterns that drive them apart and instead reach for each other, asking and answering the simple question: are you there for me?

Summary

Sue Johnson, a clinical psychologist and one of the developers of Emotionally Focused Therapy, wrote this book to share with couples what she had learned in many years of helping partners. Her starting point is simple: adult love is an attachment bond, much like the bond between a young child and a parent.

Drawing on attachment research, she argues that we are made to depend on a few close people. When we feel our partner is near and open to us, we feel safe and can face the world. When that connection seems at risk, we feel a deep alarm, and we often react with blame, nagging, coldness or silence.

Johnson calls the repeated arguments that follow demon dialogues. In one, each partner tries to prove the other is the problem. In the most common, one partner pushes for contact through criticism while the other pulls away to avoid conflict, and each move makes the other worse. In a third, both partners shut down and the relationship grows cold.

The real issue in these fights, she says, is rarely the dishes or the money. Underneath lies a single question: are you there for me? A secure bond rests on three things: being accessible, being responsive and being emotionally engaged.

The book is organised around seven conversations that couples can have together. Partners learn to spot their negative cycle and see it as the shared enemy, to name the raw spots and old hurts that trigger them, and to calmly revisit a difficult moment. The heart of the book is the conversation in which each partner shares their softer fears and needs and the other responds with care.

Later conversations cover forgiving painful injuries, keeping affection alive and building rituals that protect the bond over time. The message is hopeful: partners who learn to reach for each other can rebuild trust and closeness.

312 words

One-line takeaway: This week, when a familiar argument starts with your partner, pause and say what you are feeling underneath the anger, such as worry about being apart, and ask what they are feeling too.
