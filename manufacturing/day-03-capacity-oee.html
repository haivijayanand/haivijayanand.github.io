Capacity and Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE)

How much a machine or plant can really make, and why it makes less: design, effective and actual capacity, utilisation and efficiency, and Overall Equipment Effectiveness — availability × performance × quality — with the six big losses behind it.

⏱ about 2–3 hours🧪 Lab: OEE loss waterfall❓ 5-question quiz

1Today's goals

  • Distinguish design, effective and actual capacity
  • Calculate utilisation and efficiency
  • Calculate OEE from shift data and break it into availability, performance and quality
  • Classify downtime into the six big losses
  • Know what a realistic OEE target is and why TEEP is different

2Three kinds of capacity

MeasureMeaningExample: 9-colour gravure press
Design capacityMaximum output at rated speed, all time available300 m/min × 24 h × 365 d
Effective capacityDesign minus planned losses: shifts, holidays, planned changeovers and maintenance3 shifts, 340 days, 4 changeovers a day
Actual outputWhat came off the machine as good productFrom the production log
Utilisation = actual output ÷ design capacity Efficiency = actual output ÷ effective capacity

Capacity in a converter is not one number: a press has capacity in metres per minute, but customers buy kilograms or pieces, and kilograms depend on web width, thickness and density. Always state the unit and the product mix behind a capacity figure.

3Overall Equipment Effectiveness

OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality Availability = run time ÷ planned production time Performance = (ideal cycle time × total count) ÷ run time = actual speed ÷ rated speed Quality = good count ÷ total count

OEE answers one question: of the time we planned to make product, how much made good product at full speed? It strips out planned shutdowns so that it measures how well the machine is run, not how many shifts were scheduled.

Loss groupSix big lossesFlexible packaging examples
Availability1. BreakdownsDrive fault, doctor-blade holder failure, heater element
2. Set-up and adjustmentCylinder change, ink wash-up, register set, die change on pouch machine
Performance3. Small stops and idlingWeb breaks, splice failures, sensor trips, waiting for rolls
4. Reduced speedRunning a 300 m/min press at 180 to "be safe" on a difficult job
Quality5. Start-up rejectsMake-ready waste until colour and register are right
6. Production rejectsBond strength failures, pin-holes, colour variation, seal leaks
Benchmarks. 85 % OEE is often quoted as "world class" for discrete lines; most batch converting plants measure 45–65 % honestly. What matters is the trend and the loss Pareto, not the headline. TEEP (total effective equipment performance) = OEE × loading (planned time ÷ all calendar time) — it shows how much capacity you have before you need a new machine.

4Worked example

OEE of a pouch machine for one shiftShift 480 min, planned break 30 min → planned time 450 min Downtime: changeover 55 min + breakdown 20 min = 75 min → run time 375 min Availability = 375 / 450 = 83.3 % Rated 120 pouches/min; total count 36 000 → ideal time 300 min Performance = 300 / 375 = 80.0 % Good pouches 34 560 (1440 rejects) → Quality = 96.0 % OEE = 0.833 × 0.800 × 0.960 = 64.0 % Biggest loss: speed (75 min equivalent) and changeover (55 min) — not quality.

5Setting up OEE on the shop floor

  1. Fix the ideal (rated) speed per machine and per product group — and write it down. Do not use "the speed we usually run".
  2. Define planned time: what counts as planned shutdown (no orders, holidays, planned PM) and what does not.
  3. Give operators a short, fixed list of downtime reasons (10–15 codes) mapped to the six losses.
  4. Count total and good output; book make-ready waste separately.
  5. Review OEE and the loss Pareto daily at the machine; weekly with management.
  6. Attack the biggest loss with a small team (Days 11, 17, 20); measure again.

7Pro tips and common mistakes

  • Don't game the ideal speed. Lowering it raises Performance on paper and hides the loss.
  • OEE is for improving a machine, not comparing operators or plants with different product mixes.
  • Measure OEE on the bottleneck first (Day 4) — an hour lost there is an hour lost for the plant.
  • In converting, add a material view alongside OEE: make-ready waste in kg often costs more than the minutes.

8Practice

  1. Collect one week of shift data for one machine and calculate OEE.
  2. Build the six-loss Pareto. Which loss is biggest?
  3. Calculate TEEP for that machine. Do you need a new one?
