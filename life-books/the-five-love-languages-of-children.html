The Five Love Languages of Children

Gary Chapman and Ross Campbell · first published 1997 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Every child needs to feel loved in a way they can truly receive, and when parents learn to speak all five love languages, and especially their child's main one, the child grows more secure, cooperative and ready to learn.

Summary

Gary Chapman, a marriage counsellor, teamed up with Ross Campbell, a child psychiatrist, to apply the love languages idea to raising children. Their starting point is simple: most parents love their children deeply, yet many children do not feel loved. The gap is often about how love is expressed, not how much is felt.

The authors picture each child as carrying an emotional tank. When the tank is full, a child feels safe and is far better able to listen, behave and learn. When it runs low, problems with behaviour, mood and schoolwork tend to grow. Keeping the tank full, they argue, is the foundation for everything else a parent hopes to teach.

They describe five ways love is given and received: physical touch, such as hugs and a hand on the shoulder; words of affirmation that praise effort and express affection; quality time with full, undivided attention; gifts chosen with thought; and acts of service, helping a child in ways that fit their age. Children need all five, but most have one that speaks to them most strongly.

To find a child's main language, the authors suggest watching how the child shows love to others, noticing what they most often ask for, and listening to what they complain about. They caution that the main language is often hard to read in very young children, so little ones should simply receive plenty of all five.

The book insists that love should be unconditional, not earned by good behaviour. It also warns against using gifts as a substitute for time and attention. Discipline, the authors say, works best when it comes from a child who already feels loved, and correction should be given in a way that does not wound the child's main language. Later chapters cover helping children handle anger, and how single parents and couples can apply these ideas at home.

312 words

One-line takeaway: This week, spend a few minutes each day noticing how your child shows affection and what they ask you for, then deliberately give them more of that, such as a hug, a kind word, or ten minutes of undivided play.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. What is the central problem the book sets out to address?

    Answer A. The authors note that many children feel unloved even when their parents love them, because love is not expressed in a way they receive.

  2. What image do the authors use for a child's need for love?

    Answer B. They describe an emotional tank that must be kept full for a child to feel secure.

  3. According to the book, what tends to happen when a child's emotional tank is full?

    Answer C. A full tank gives a child the security that supports good behaviour and learning.

  4. Which of these is NOT one of the five love languages?

    Answer D. The five languages are physical touch, words of affirmation, quality time, gifts and acts of service.

  5. What do the authors say about how many love languages a child needs?

    Answer A. All five matter, but one usually speaks most strongly to each child.

  6. Which is a suggested way to discover a child's main love language?

    Answer B. How a child expresses love to others is a strong clue to how they like to receive it.

  7. What advice do the authors give for very young children whose main language is unclear?

    Answer C. Because the main language is hard to read early on, little ones should receive all five generously.

  8. What kind of love does the book say parents should give?

    Answer D. The authors stress that love should be given freely, not as a reward.

  9. What warning do the authors give about gifts?

    Answer B. Gifts lose their meaning when used as a substitute for genuine attention and presence.

  10. How does the book connect love and discipline?

    Answer C. A child who feels secure in a parent's love is more open to guidance and correction.
