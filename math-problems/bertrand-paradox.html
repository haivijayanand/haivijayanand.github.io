Bertrand's Paradox

1. The problem

Draw an equilateral triangle inside a circle. Now draw a chord of the circle at random. What is the chance the chord is longer than a side of the triangle?

Joseph Bertrand asked this in 1889 to show that "at random" is not a complete instruction.

2. The answer — it depends how you choose

Method 1: two random points on the circle
1/3
Method 2: random point on a random radius, chord square to it
1/2
Method 3: random midpoint anywhere inside the circle
1/4

Each method is a perfectly reasonable meaning of "random chord", and each gives a different answer. A probability is only defined once the way of choosing is fixed.

3. Working it out (circle of radius 1, triangle side √3)

  • Method 1: fix one end of the chord at a vertex of the triangle. The chord is longer than a side when the other end falls on the arc opposite that vertex, which is 1/3 of the circle.
  • Method 2: a chord is longer than √3 exactly when its distance from the centre is less than 1/2. A random distance along the radius is below 1/2 half the time.
  • Method 3: the midpoint must lie inside a circle of radius 1/2, which has 1/4 of the area.

Method 2 has a special property: it is the only one that gives the same answer if you shrink, move or rotate the circle — so physicists often call it the "natural" choice (throwing long straws onto a small circle gives method 2).

5. Fine print

  • The lesson carries straight into engineering statistics: "pick a random sample" needs a sampling rule. Picking random points on a component's surface, along its length or by volume gives different defect statistics.
  • E. T. Jaynes (1973) argued that the invariance argument makes 1/2 the only consistent answer when nothing else is known.
