Liquidity Ratios

Liquidity is the ability to pay bills as they fall due. A profitable company that cannot pay its suppliers and salary this month is in trouble. Liquidity ratios compare what will turn into cash soon with what must be paid soon. Pick your level.

Level 2 · For working people

Calculate and interpret the main liquidity ratios, and see how they can be flattered.

1The ratios

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities Quick ratio = (Current assets − Inventories − Prepaid) ÷ Current liabilities Cash ratio = (Cash + Marketable securities) ÷ Current liabilities Net working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities

Kaveri Foods' liquidity slipped: cash fell, short-term borrowing rose, and more of its current assets are now receivables and stock that must still be converted. The quick ratio below 1 means it needs to sell stock (or keep borrowing) to meet current liabilities.

2What is "good"?

Textbooks say current ratio 2 and quick ratio 1, but it depends on the business. A supermarket collects cash daily and pays suppliers in 45 days: a current ratio below 1 is normal. A machine-tool maker with long production cycles needs much more. Indian banks' working-capital assessment traditionally expects a current ratio of about 1.33 (the second method of lending, Tandon Committee). Compare with peers and the firm's own history.

Level 3 · For experts — a review

Why static ratios mislead and what cash-flow-based measures add.

1Defensive interval and cash-based liquidity

Defensive interval (days) = (Cash + Receivables + Marketable securities) ÷ Daily cash operating expenses Cash flow liquidity = (Cash + Marketable securities + CFO) ÷ Current liabilities

Static ratios ignore timing: ₹100 of receivables due in 90 days cannot pay ₹100 due tomorrow. The cash conversion cycle (Chapter 37) and a 13-week cash forecast (Chapter 14) are better tools for management. Lenders use drawing power (stock and receivables less margins) to set working-capital limits.

2Research and limits

Altman's Z-score (1968) uses working capital ÷ total assets as one of five predictors of distress; Beaver (1966) found cash flow ÷ total debt the single best predictor. Very high liquidity is not free: idle cash earns less than the cost of capital and can signal agency problems (Jensen's free cash flow argument). Liquidity under Ind AS 1 also requires disclosure of the maturity analysis of financial liabilities (Ind AS 107), which is more informative than any single ratio.
