How to Raise an Adult

Julie Lythcott-Haims · first published 2015 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Children grow into capable, confident adults not when parents clear every path for them, but when parents gradually step back and let them learn to manage their own lives.

Summary

Julie Lythcott-Haims spent about a decade as a dean of freshmen at Stanford University, and over those years she noticed something worrying. Many bright, accomplished students seemed unsure how to handle ordinary problems on their own, and some parents were still phoning, emailing and stepping in on their behalf. As a mother, she saw the same habits in her own home.

She describes overparenting as a mix of overprotecting, overdirecting and handholding. Out of love and fear, parents schedule every hour, guard children from all risk, argue with teachers and coaches, and sometimes do homework and applications for them. The goal is usually a safe, successful future, often measured by entry into a small set of prestigious colleges.

The book argues that this approach backfires. Young people who have rarely solved problems, faced disappointment or made their own choices can arrive at adulthood lacking basic life skills and the self-belief that comes from doing hard things. She links this pattern to rising anxiety and low confidence among students, while noting that parents also suffer from the stress of constant management.

Her alternative is practical. Teach everyday skills step by step: first do a task for your child, then do it together, then watch them do it, and finally let them do it alone. Give children real chores, let them talk to adults such as shopkeepers and teachers themselves, and allow plenty of free, unstructured play.

She also urges parents to let children make mistakes and feel the natural results while the stakes are still small, to praise effort and character rather than only grades, and to see that many colleges and many paths can lead to a good life.

Finally, she reminds parents to keep a life of their own. When adults model purpose, rest and resilience, children see what a capable grown-up looks like and feel trusted to become one.

311 words

One-line takeaway: This week, pick one task you still do for your child, such as packing a school bag, ordering at a counter or doing a simple chore, and teach it in stages until they can do it on their own.
