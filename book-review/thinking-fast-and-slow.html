Thinking, Fast and Slow

Daniel Kahneman · first published 2011 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

Human judgement runs on two modes of thinking, and the quick intuitive mode produces predictable errors that careful leaders can learn to anticipate.

Summary

Daniel Kahneman, a psychologist who shared the 2002 Nobel prize in economics, draws on decades of research, much of it with his late colleague Amos Tversky, to explain how people actually make judgements and choices. His central device is a pair of characters he calls System 1 and System 2.

System 1 is fast, automatic and effortless. It reads faces and forms instant impressions. System 2 is slow and deliberate. It does arithmetic, checks arguments and exercises self-control, but it tires easily and is often content to accept whatever System 1 suggests. Many errors arise when a confident intuition goes unchecked.

To answer hard questions quickly, System 1 relies on shortcuts. We judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind, so vivid events seem more common than they are. Arbitrary numbers we have just seen pull our estimates towards them. We judge by resemblance to a stereotype and ignore base rates. We build a coherent story from the little information we have and rarely ask what is missing.

These habits feed overconfidence. Experts in unpredictable fields trust forecasts that rarely beat chance, plans assume best cases, and people mistake luck for skill when they forget that extreme results tend to drift back towards the average.

The book then turns to choice. Prospect theory, Kahneman and Tversky's alternative to the classical model of rational decision, shows that people judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point, and that losses hurt more than equal gains please. The same option framed differently can therefore reverse a decision.

Finally, Kahneman separates the self that lives through experiences from the self that remembers them. Memory is dominated by peaks and endings rather than duration, so we often choose for the remembering self.

For managers, the lesson is to slow down important decisions, seek outside views and design processes that catch predictable biases.

313 words

One-line takeaway: Treat your confident intuitions as hypotheses, and build decision processes that check them against base rates, outside views and honest feedback.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. In Kahneman's framework, which description best fits System 1?

    Answer C. System 1 works quickly and without conscious effort, producing impressions and intuitions.

  2. Why does Kahneman describe System 2 as 'lazy'?

    Answer A. Effortful thinking is costly, so System 2 often endorses intuitive answers without scrutiny.

  3. Judging how frequent an event is by how easily examples come to mind is known as the:

    Answer D. Ease of recall stands in for actual frequency, which makes vivid or recent events seem more common.

  4. What does the anchoring effect describe?

    Answer B. An initial number, even a random one, biases later numerical judgements towards it.

  5. When people judge probability by how well something matches a stereotype, what do they typically neglect?

    Answer C. Representativeness leads people to ignore how common each category actually is.

  6. According to prospect theory, how do people evaluate outcomes?

    Answer B. Prospect theory holds that changes from a reference point, not final states of wealth, drive evaluation.

  7. What is loss aversion?

    Answer D. Losses weigh more heavily psychologically than equivalent gains, which shapes many choices.

  8. Describing the same choice as '90% survival' versus '10% mortality' and getting different decisions illustrates:

    Answer A. Logically equivalent descriptions can produce different choices depending on how they are framed.

  9. A manager praises a team after an exceptional quarter and is disappointed when the next quarter is closer to average. Kahneman would point to:

    Answer B. Extreme results often contain luck, so later results tend to move back towards the average regardless of praise.

  10. What does the distinction between the experiencing self and the remembering self show?

    Answer C. The remembering self neglects duration and gives heavy weight to the most intense moment and the end.
