Good Strategy Bad Strategy

Richard P. Rumelt · first published 2011 · reviewed by K Vijay Anand

A real strategy is a coherent response to a clearly diagnosed challenge, not a list of goals, slogans or ambitions.

Summary

Richard Rumelt argues that the word strategy has been worn down until it means almost anything: a vision statement, a budget target, a set of values or an inspiring speech. Most of what organisations call strategy, he says, is nothing of the kind. A true strategy is a focused answer to a specific challenge, and it is surprisingly rare.

He first describes the marks of bad strategy. One is fluff: grand, abstract language that sounds deep but says little. Another is failing to face the challenge, so the plan never names the actual obstacle. A third is mistaking goals for strategy, as when leaders announce growth targets and call that a plan. A fourth is setting objectives that are either a long, unprioritised wish list or so ambitious that nobody knows how to reach them. Bad strategy often comes from avoiding hard choices, or from the belief that positive thinking alone produces results.

Good strategy, in contrast, has a simple core that Rumelt calls the kernel. It has three parts. A diagnosis explains what is really going on and which difficulty matters most. A guiding policy sets the overall approach for dealing with that difficulty. Coherent actions are coordinated steps that carry out the policy and reinforce one another.

Rumelt then explores sources of strength that good strategies draw on. Leverage comes from concentrating effort where it will have the greatest effect. Proximate objectives are near-term targets that are clear enough to be achieved. Focus, design and the careful fitting together of activities create advantages that rivals find hard to copy. Leaders also gain by noticing shifts in technology or markets early, and by watching for inertia and decay inside their own organisations.

Finally, he treats strategy as a hypothesis to be tested. Leaders should think like scientists, question their own judgments and stay open to evidence. The lasting lesson is that strategy means choosing, and choosing means saying no.

320 words

One-line takeaway: Name the critical challenge honestly, choose one guiding approach to it, and back it with coordinated actions.

Test yourself — 10 questions

Tap an option to check it. Each answer shows a short reason.

  1. According to Rumelt, what is the main problem with most documents labelled 'strategy'?

    Answer B. Rumelt's central complaint is that much so-called strategy is fluff, goals or ambitions with no actual plan for overcoming an obstacle.

  2. What three elements make up the 'kernel' of a good strategy?

    Answer C. The kernel consists of a diagnosis of the situation, a guiding policy for dealing with it, and coherent actions that carry it out.

  3. In Rumelt's terms, what does 'fluff' refer to?

    Answer A. Fluff is superficial, buzzword-heavy language that disguises the absence of real strategic thinking.

  4. Why does Rumelt say that announcing an ambitious growth target is not a strategy?

    Answer D. Mistaking goals for strategy leaves out the diagnosis and the actions needed to get past the real difficulty.

  5. What is the purpose of the diagnosis in a good strategy?

    Answer B. The diagnosis simplifies a complex situation by naming the challenge that matters most.

  6. What makes a set of actions 'coherent' in Rumelt's framework?

    Answer A. Coherent actions fit together and amplify each other rather than pulling in different directions.

  7. What does Rumelt mean by a 'proximate objective'?

    Answer C. Proximate objectives turn a hard situation into a solvable problem that people can act on.

  8. Which idea best describes 'leverage' as a source of strategic strength?

    Answer D. Leverage comes from focusing resources on a pivotal point so that a limited effort produces a large result.

  9. According to the book, what often causes leaders to produce bad strategy?

    Answer A. Rumelt argues that bad strategy often comes from an unwillingness to choose and from a belief that enthusiasm alone brings results.

  10. How does Rumelt suggest leaders should treat a strategy once it is formed?

    Answer C. He encourages leaders to think like scientists, questioning their judgments and learning from results.
