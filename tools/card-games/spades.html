How to play Spades

Four players in two partnerships: You + Meena (across) vs Arun + Ravi. 52 cards, 13 each. Play is clockwise: You → Arun → Meena → Ravi. The deal rotates every hand.

Bidding

  • Starting left of the dealer, each player bids the number of tricks they expect to take (1–13) or Nil (0 — a promise to take no tricks at all).
  • The two non-nil bids of a partnership are added into the team contract.

Play

  • The player left of the dealer leads the first trick. Follow suit if you can; if not, play any card.
  • Spades are always trump. A trick is won by the highest spade, or if no spade was played, the highest card of the suit led.
  • Spades cannot be led until they are broken (a spade has been played on another suit), unless the leader holds only spades.

Variant choices: tricks taken by a Nil bidder do not count toward the partner's contract; they count as bags for the team. If both partners bid Nil, the contract is 0 and every trick taken is a bag. No blind nil. First lead is by the player left of the dealer (any card except a spade unless only spades). Game to 500: when a team reaches 500 at the end of a hand, the higher score wins; if level, another hand is played. A team that falls to −200 loses (mercy rule).

Computer players

  • Easy – rough bids, mostly random legal cards.
  • Normal – counts high cards, spade length and short suits to bid; wins cheaply, doesn't overtake partner, trumps when void.
  • Hard – also tracks every card played and every void, knows which cards are now the highest left (“boss”), avoids bags once the contract is made, protects a partner's Nil and attacks an opponent's Nil.

3. Partner's spade length

You hold s spades, so 13−s are among the other 39 cards. Partner's 13 cards are a random subset:
P(partner has k) = C(13−s,k)·C(26+s,13−k) / C(39,13)

Nil — when is it a good bid?

  • No A♠ or K♠, and ideally no Q♠ unless you hold 4+ small spades to cover it.
  • Three or fewer spades, all low (2–8). Every spade you hold is a potential forced trump win late in the hand.
  • In side suits, aces and kings are killers; a long suit with only low cards (2–6) is safe, a singleton K or Q is dangerous.
  • A short side suit (void/singleton) helps: you can discard your dangerous high cards on it.
  • Rough guide: if expected tricks below ≤ 1 and your highest spade is ≤ 9, nil succeeds roughly 6–7 times in 10 with a supportive partner.
  • Partner of a Nil bidder: lead high cards early and cover the Nil bidder's suit; opponents will lead low to force it.

Strategy tips

  • Bid what your hand is worth plus a little for your partner; teams usually bid 10–11 of the 13 tricks.
  • Avoid bags: once your contract is safe, duck. 10 bags cost 100 points.
  • Don't overtake your partner's winning card — play low and save your winner.
  • A side-suit ace usually wins only if that suit splits normally; with 5 missing among two hands, a 5-0 split happens only ~4% of the time (calculator 1).
  • Short side suits + spades = trump tricks. A void with 3+ spades is worth about one extra trick.
